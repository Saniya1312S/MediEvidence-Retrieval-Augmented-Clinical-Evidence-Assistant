Medical Image Segmentation: Foundation Model Adaptation versus
Task-Specific Training
Emre Kutlusana, Yongmin Lia,∗
aDepartment of Computer Science, Brunel University of London, Uxbridge, UB8 3PH, United Kingdom
Abstract
Foundation models pretrained on large natural-image collections are increasingly adapted for
medical image segmentation, in the expectation that they will replace models trained for each
task. Most comparisons, however, use data from the training distribution and a single taskspecific baseline whose configuration is rarely justified, and therefore provide limited evidence
of performance at new clinical sites. It also remains unclear how much of a foundation model
must be adapted, and what determines this. We adapted a foundation model, DINOv2, with
strategies ranging from tuning only its bias terms to full fine-tuning, and compared it with U-Nets
and ResU-Nets trained from scratch. Experiments covered colonoscopy, breast ultrasound and
dermoscopy, with testing on held-out data from the training source and on independent datasets
from other institutions. The adapted foundation model generalised better on every external
dataset. Within the training distribution, the task-specific models remained competitive when
training data were abundant, and delineated boundaries more precisely, while the foundation
model more often missed small lesions. Only a small fraction of the foundation model required
adaptation, and the capacity needed was determined by the imaging domain rather than by the
adaptation method. A widely used polyp test set was also found to duplicate images from another
benchmark. Foundation-model adaptation is therefore warranted where models must work across
sites, with capacity selected on the target domain.
Keywords:
medical image segmentation, foundation models, parameter-efficient fine-tuning,
cross-dataset generalisation, U-Net, ResU-Net, nnU-Net
1. Introduction
Vision foundation models, pretrained on hundreds of millions of images at a scale no clinical
dataset approaches, have made a question urgent for medical image segmentation. Given a new
dataset and task, is it better to adapt one of these models, or to train a task-specific architecture
from random initialisation as the field has done since U-Net?
The question matters because of where segmentation models are deployed. A model trained
at one hospital is intended for use at others, on images from different scanners, protocols and
patient populations. This distribution shift, rather than in-domain accuracy, is the principal
obstacle to clinical adoption, and it is invisible in the measurement usually reported. Every model
in this study is therefore evaluated under two regimes: in domain, on held-out data from the
training distribution, and out of distribution, on data from institutions that contributed nothing
to training.
The literature comparing the two approaches generally reports that adapted foundation
models win, and three properties of how that comparison is made limit what follows from it. It is
usually conducted within the training distribution, so a property of the evaluation is reported as a

property of the models. It is usually made against a single task-specific model whose configuration
is not itself defended, and Isensee et al. [1] show that many claimed improvements over U-Net are
made against a weakly configured instance of it; the two objections compound, since an advantage
so obtained can be an artefact of both at once. It is also usually made at a single operating
point, that is, at one choice of settings such as the adaptation rank or the training budget, so a
statement about one configuration appears to be a statement about the methods.
A parallel gap concerns how much adaptation a target requires. Dutt et al. [2], in classification
and generation tasks, report that parameter-efficient methods are most effective in low-data
regimes, making training-set size the governing quantity and one known before any experiment;
Teuber et al. [3] report the contrary directly, finding no such advantage even at very small training
sizes, and attribute the variation to properties of the dataset, which are not known in advance.
What neither establishes is whether the requirement belongs to the domain or to the method
supplying the adaptation.
The aim of this study is to determine, for medical image segmentation, when adaptation of a
pretrained foundation model is warranted in place of a task-specific architecture, and what governs
the amount of adaptation such a model requires. It does so along two axes. The architecture
axis compares an adapted foundation model with task-specific architectures trained from random
initialisation; the adaptation axis varies how much of the foundation model is adapted, measuring
how the choice of adaptation strategy affects cross-dataset generalisation and whether the capacity
required is a property of the domain or of the method supplying it.
Both axes span three imaging domains—colonoscopy polyp imaging, breast ultrasound and
skin lesion dermoscopy—each with one training source and held-out test sets, giving eight
out-of-distribution test sets, one of which (CVC-T) consists of images also present in another
(CVC-ColonDB; Section 3.2). The foundation model is a DINOv2-Small encoder with a minimal
linear decoder, adapted under strategies spanning 385 to 22.1 million trainable parameters. The
task-specific comparators are a plain U-Net and a residual U-Net, both under the nnU-Net recipe
and each tuned independently.
The contributions are threefold:
1. A controlled comparison of foundation-model adaptation against task-specific training from
scratch, with the evaluation regimes separated throughout and against baselines shown to
be competitive with published architectures under an identical protocol: adaptation leads
on all eight out-of-distribution test sets in DSC, IoU, sensitivity and HD95 under both
architectures and its advantage grows as training data shrinks within a domain, most clearly
in polyp, while in domain at the full training set no accuracy advantage was observed.
2. Empirical findings on adaptation capacity: the step from a frozen encoder to bias-only
tuning captures most of the available benefit in polyp and breast, LoRA at 2.7 per cent
of the parameters is equal or ahead of full fine-tuning in DSC on all five polyp sets across
three seeds, and the capacity a domain requires is a property of the domain rather than of
the method supplying it.
3. Practical recommendations for choosing between and evaluating the two approaches: when
adaptation is warranted, how much capacity to use and how to select it, which failure
modes to check before deployment, and which evaluation hazards in standard benchmarks
to control.
2. Related work
Medical image segmentation spans a wide range of imaging modalities, including X-ray
radiography [4], computed tomography (CT) [5, 6], magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [7, 8],
ultrasound [9], colonoscopy [10, 11], dermoscopy [12, 13, 14], fundus photography [15, 16] and
optical coherence tomography (OCT) [17, 18]. These modalities differ in how the image is formed,
in the appearance and scale of the anatomical targets and in how much annotated data is available,

and each has accumulated its own methods and benchmarks. The three domains studied here,
colonoscopy, breast ultrasound and dermoscopy, are drawn from this range, and the remainder of
this section reviews the segmentation architectures, foundation models, adaptation methods and
evaluation practices on which the study builds.
2.1. Segmentation architectures and nnU-Net
Before deep learning, segmentation methods were typically designed around the anatomy
and appearance of a single task, for example graph cuts for retinal vessels [19] and for retinal
layers in OCT [20, 21], and Gaussian mixture and other probabilistic models for vessel extraction
[22, 16]. Learned encoder–decoder networks have since largely replaced such pipelines, although
task-specific deep architectures continue to be proposed for individual targets such as colonoscopy
polyps [23, 24, 25, 26]. The U-Net of Ronneberger et al. [27] still dominates medical segmentation,
and of the many variants proposed since, McConnell et al. [28], comparing residual, dense,
inception and attention families across eight datasets, conclude that none dominates and that the
optimal choice is dataset-dependent. nnU-Net [29] reframed how such comparisons should be
conducted by proposing a procedure rather than an architecture: it derives the network topology,
patch size, resampling strategy, batch size and training schedule from a fingerprint of the dataset,
and its central empirical claim, that a U-Net so configured matches or exceeds most published
architectural modifications, makes the configuration of a baseline a methodological question
rather than an implementation detail. That claim was sharpened in nnU-Net Revisited [1], which
finds that many papers claiming to outperform U-Net do so against a weakly configured instance
of it, and which also reports strong performance from residual-encoder nnU-Net configurations,
evaluated on three-dimensional multi-class segmentation across large datasets. Both results
shape the design here: a residual variant, in the spirit of those configurations but with residual
blocks in both encoder and decoder, was adopted as the primary task-specific baseline, and
the 2015 architecture was deliberately not used, since employing it would have reproduced the
weak-baseline error in the direction that flatters the conclusions drawn here.
2.2. Foundation models and their medical adaptation
DINOv2 [30] is trained self-supervised on 142 million curated natural images with no labels,
and its patch-level features transfer to dense prediction without task-specific supervision during
pretraining. Because it has never seen a segmentation mask, whether its representations transfer
to medical segmentation is a question about representational generality rather than about label
overlap, which is why it was selected here. Its medical transfer has been assessed by Baharoon
et al. [31] across nine radiology benchmarks and by Ayzenberg et al. [32] in few-shot segmentation,
and Li et al. [33] report that pairing DINO features with a U-Net decoder supplies a strong
inductive prior for medical segmentation.
The second family originates with the Segment Anything Model [34], trained on a billion
masks over eleven million images, and is adapted to medical images by retraining on large-scale
medical data [35], by applying LoRA to the image encoder [36], by large-scale two-dimensional
adaptation [37] or by inserting adapters into a frozen backbone [38]; SAM-Mamba [39] reports
strong cross-dataset polyp performance and appears in the comparison of Section 4.2. A model
supervised on segmentation masks and a model that has never seen one require different amounts
of adaptation to reach a medical target, so a finding about how much adaptation one needs does
not automatically transfer to the other (Section 5.5).
2.3. Parameter-efficient adaptation
Parameter-efficient methods update a small fraction of a pretrained model’s parameters,
avoiding the memory and storage cost of full fine-tuning and, on small target datasets, the risk of
destabilising representations that took large-scale pretraining to acquire. Adapter modules [40]
and visual prompt tuning [41] add computation at inference, which is the property LoRA avoids.
3

--- PAGE 4 [pdf_text] ---
LoRA [42] freezes the pretrained weights and adds a trainable low-rank decomposition to selected
projections, so that the effective weight update is constrained to rank r; the rank controls the
capacity of the update and is the method’s principal hyperparameter, and because the adapters
can be merged into the base weights after training, inference cost is unchanged. Kalajdzievski
[43] shows that the conventional scaling factor slows learning and stunts performance at higher
ranks and proposes a rank-stabilised alternative, which is why the present work holds the scaling
constant so that the rank sweep of Section 4.5 varies rank and nothing else. BitFit [44] freezes all
weight matrices and trains only bias terms, under one tenth of one per cent of the parameters
in the models it was proposed for, and its authors report performance competitive with full
fine-tuning on language tasks with the advantage concentrated in low-data regimes. The absence
of a rank parameter and its different intervention site make it the natural control for testing
whether a capacity requirement is a property of low-rank factorisation or of the target, which is
the role it serves in Section 4.5.
The literature applying these methods to medical imaging contains an unresolved disagreement,
and it is the point at which this study enters. Dutt et al. [2] benchmark parameter-efficient
methods across medical image analysis tasks and report an ordering that depends on data volume:
in their HAM10000 classification experiment with ViT-Large, LoRA overtakes full fine-tuning
from approximately eighty per cent of the available data downward, while BitFit is superior only
below twenty per cent. Teuber et al. [3] evaluate parameter-efficient adaptation of SAM across
biomedical datasets and report instead that the relative effectiveness of these methods differs by
dataset in a way that data volume alone does not account for, and that for computational efficiency,
where in the encoder a method intervenes matters more than which method it is. The two are
not straightforwardly reconcilable, and no study separates them, which would require varying
data volume within a domain while comparing across domains at matched volume. Section 5.5
returns to the disagreement with results in hand.
2.4. Cross-dataset evaluation and data hygiene
Most published results are obtained on held-out data from the training source, which is not
the deployment setting, and under a consistent protocol well-tuned empirical risk minimisation
is competitive with most proposed domain generalisation algorithms [45]. The protocol of Fan
et al. [23] partitions five polyp collections into a fixed training portion and three held-out sets,
and its widespread adoption means published scores are comparable in experimental setup rather
than merely in metric, which is what permits the baselines here to be positioned against the
literature. U-Bench [46] evaluates 100 U-Net variants across 28 datasets and 10 modalities,
reserving eight unseen datasets for zero-shot evaluation, and reports that in-domain metrics have
largely saturated while zero-shot generalisation continues to separate methods. Ndipenoch et al.
[18] apply the same distinction within medical segmentation, comparing an adapted SAM against
nnU-Net variants across three OCT device vendors and reporting the task-specific models slightly
ahead. A domain qualifies for such a study only if it offers a training source with independently
acquired, comparably annotated, public test sets; three satisfy this and were chosen to differ in
modality, by a factor of more than four in training-set size, and in severity of shift. Selecting
domains that differ this way is what makes it possible to ask whether a regularity is a property of
a method or of the data it was measured on, which a single-domain study cannot ask.
Cross-dataset evaluation is only as sound as the separation between the sets compared.
Truong et al. [47] report that medical datasets often contain near-duplicate images that can
bias evaluation, identifying possible near-duplicates in the widely used Medical Segmentation
Decathlon and benchmarking their detection under transformations that include cropping and
compression. On metrics, Taha and Hanbury [48] set out the consequences of choosing among
them. On representations, centred kernel alignment [49] is the standard tool for comparing
representations across networks, and Nguyen et al. [50] use the debiased estimator adopted in
Section 4.6.

2.5. Research gaps
Three gaps follow.
1. When adaptation should be preferred. The literature does not settle when one approach
should be preferred over the other, because comparisons are typically made within the
training distribution and against a baseline whose configuration is not itself defended, the
weakness nnU-Net [29] and nnU-Net Revisited [1] identify in architectural comparisons.
2. How much adaptation a target requires. Separating the data-volume effects and datasetdependent outcomes of Section 2.3 requires varying data volume within a domain while
comparing across domains at matched volume, and testing the same question under two
methods that share no mechanism, so that any regularity found cannot be a property of
one method’s parameterisation.
3. How far the answers depend on configuration. nnU-Net Revisited [1] and U-Bench [46] reach
their conclusions by breadth, evaluating many methods under a common protocol, which
leaves little room to vary the configuration of any one comparison.
This study varies data volume on the architecture axis and compares domains and methods on
the adaptation axis; the interaction between capacity requirement and data volume is left open
(Section 5.4). For the third gap it takes the complementary trade-off, few methods examined at
many operating points, which is the reason it covers three domains and two adaptation methods,
examining their sensitivity to adaptation rank, training budget and evaluation regime.
3. Methods
3.1. Design and scope
The foundation model adopted is DINOv2 [30], in its smallest variant.
It was chosen
deliberately in preference to segmentation-specific or medical foundation models such as SAM
[34], which is trained on a billion masks, and SAMed [36], which fine-tunes SAM on medical
images. The aim is to demonstrate the effectiveness of the adaptation approach with a small,
general-purpose model that has never been trained on segmentation masks or medical images,
so that any advantage it shows can be attributed to adaptation rather than to prior exposure
to the target task or domain. Such a model represents a conservative case: if it suffices, larger
foundation models, and those pretrained on masks or medical data, would be expected to perform
at least as well. A finding about how much adaptation one model family needs does not, however,
automatically transfer to another (Section 2.2), and repeating the adaptation axis on SAM, which
SAMed adapts with LoRA in the same way as the configurations here, is left to future work
(Section 5.4).
The design is organised around two axes kept separate throughout, summarised in Fig. 1. The
architecture axis asks when adaptation outperforms task-specific training: an adapted DINOv2
encoder is compared against a plain U-Net and a residual U-Net (ResU-Net) trained from random
initialisation. The adaptation axis asks how much adaptation is required: the foundation model is
compared only against itself under strategies of increasing capacity. No task-specific model appears
on the adaptation axis, and no adaptation strategy is applied to the task-specific architectures,
which have no pretrained parameters to adapt.
The adaptation axis is conditional on the architecture axis, and the two intersect at a single
point rather than as a factorial design. Only one DINOv2 configuration enters the architectureaxis comparison, selected on in-domain validation rather than imported from the rank sweep, and
results from one axis are not used to support claims about the other. Claims on the adaptation
axis consequently rest on a single backbone, a limitation returned to in Section 5.4.
All models share training splits, evaluation code and metric definitions. Augmentation and
optimiser settings differ by model family, the task-specific models using a stronger pipeline than
the adaptation experiments. Where budgets or learning rates differ it is because each configuration

3.3. Adaptation strategies
All adaptation experiments use a DINOv2-Small encoder [30], the smallest of the released
variants at 22.1 million parameters against 1.1 billion for the largest, with a linear decoder head:
a single 1×1 convolution mapping the 384-dimensional patch embeddings to a one-channel logit
map, bilinearly upsampled to the input resolution, containing 385 parameters (Fig. 3c). This
minimality is intentional: a heavier decoder would absorb part of the adaptation and confound
the question of how much the encoder must change. Two consequences bound what Section 4
shows. The frozen configuration should be read as a minimal baseline; Li et al. [33] combine a
frozen DINOv3 backbone with an adapter, a projection module and a U-Net-style decoder, a
substantially different architecture. And the adapted model is not intended to compete with
architectures developed for a specific task, which pair large encoders with multi-scale decoders of
their own; Section 4.2 states that scope where published scores are introduced.

The governing principle is that each strategy is evaluated at its own selected setting rather than
a shared one, and this is more consequential than it appears. An earlier run of this study trained
full fine-tuning at a single flat encoder rate for a fixed fifteen epochs, without layer-wise decay
or warmup, and under that configuration it appeared clearly inferior, a conclusion that would
have been an artefact of the protocol rather than a property of the methods. One asymmetry
follows: BitFit received a per-domain learning-rate search whereas LoRA and full fine-tuning did
not, which favours BitFit, and Section 4.5 should be read accordingly.
3.4. Task-specific architectures
A comparison against one task-specific model leaves an evident objection: any deficit might
reflect a weakness in that implementation rather than a property of task-specific training. Two
architectures are therefore used, bracketing the task-specific axis (Fig. 3a, b). The residual U-Net
represents the residual design family and the plain U-Net the field’s conventional reference point,
and if both fall behind the deficit cannot be attributed to a single architectural choice.
3.4.1. Residual U-Net
The residual variant is in the spirit of nnU-Net ResEnc [1], with residual blocks in both
encoder and decoder, trained under the nnU-Net recipe [29]: 8,114,692 parameters in a five-level
encoder of base width 32 with residual blocks, instance normalisation, LeakyReLU and deep
supervision at every decoder level. The original U-Net of Ronneberger et al. [27] was deliberately
not adopted, since using the 2015 configuration would have reproduced the weak-baseline error in
the direction that flatters this study.
3.4.2. Plain U-Net
The plain variant is the plain convolutional U-Net that nnU-Net configures by default [29, 1],
identical except that the residual addition and 1×1 skip projection are removed, at 7,763,492
parameters.
3.4.3. Shared training recipe
Both follow the nnU-Net recipe [29]: stochastic gradient descent with momentum 0.99 and
Nesterov acceleration, a polynomial schedule, weight decay 3e-5, aggressive augmentation, deep
supervision and a combined Dice and cross-entropy objective. The augmentation policy was
determined empirically, and that search is reported in Section 4.4 where it also tests whether the
deficit is a configuration failure. Learning rates were searched independently for each architecture
on polyp over an identical grid, selecting 3e-3 for the residual variant and 1e-3 for the plain one.
In the skin domain both architectures ran at 3e-3; the plain variant’s polyp-selected rate was
not additionally evaluated there. One consequence bears on Section 4.2: the skin comparison is
the more controlled of the three, both models operating at the same rate, whereas in polyp and

3.5. Training protocol and model selection
DINOv2 operates on fourteen-pixel patches, so resolutions are multiples of fourteen: 224×224
at batch size 16 for polyp and breast, and 392×392 at batch size 8 for skin, where dermoscopy
boundaries are fine relative to the field of view. The task-specific models in skin run at 384×384,
because 392 is not divisible by the cumulative downsampling factor of their encoder. The data-size
sweep of Section 4.4 was run at 224 across all skin fractions to keep cost tractable, so those figures
sit at a different operating point and are compared only against themselves.
Adaptation strategies converge within tens of epochs, whereas task-specific models require an
order of magnitude more; within any single comparison the budget is identical, so no configuration
is advantaged by training duration. Task-specific models were trained for 300 epochs in polyp
and breast and 100 in skin (Table 4). Adaptation ran at two budgets, 20 epochs for the core
comparison and 40 for the capacity analyses following the budget control of Section 4.5, and
figures from the two are never combined within a domain. In the data-size sweep, budgets were
scaled inversely with subset size so each fraction received approximately equal optimiser steps,
without which the deficit would confound data volume with training duration.
The programme these budgets describe is substantial. Each of the three domains carries
seven adaptation configurations on the capacity axis, two task-specific architectures, three or four
training-set fractions, and a learning-rate search over four values for BitFit, with the same search
run in polyp for each of the two task-specific models; the core comparison between LoRA and full
fine-tuning is run at three seeds and at two ranks, and the capacity axis at two budgets. Running
few methods at many operating points, which Section 2.5 identifies as the trade-off this study
makes, is what accounts for that count, and it is also what makes Section 4.6 possible.
Checkpoints, learning rates, ranks and configurations were all selected on in-domain validation
DSC, and no out-of-distribution test set influenced any selection decision at any stage. This is
emphasised because it is load-bearing: a recurring observation is that the configuration optimal
in domain is not the one optimal out of distribution, and that carries weight only if selection was
genuinely blind to those sets.
3.6. Reproducibility, the measurement floor and evaluation
All experiments used PyTorch [61], HuggingFace Transformers [62] and the PEFT library,
with mixed precision [63], on NVIDIA L4 and A100 GPUs, with checkpoints written to persistent
storage so that interrupted sessions could resume without retraining. Seed 42 was used throughout,
with 123 and 2024 added for the core comparison of LoRA against full fine-tuning. BitFit and the
plain U-Net were run at seed 42 only and are reported as single-seed signal rather than multi-seed
evidence, and the two terms are used with that distinction throughout.
Seeding does not make these runs deterministic. Seeding covered the main process only, leaving
the dataloader workers’ augmentation sequence non-deterministic, and cuDNN determinism was
not enforced, so two runs at an identical seed may diverge. This was observed directly: a
repeated BitFit polyp run differed by up to 0.013 DSC out of distribution and 0.004 on validation.
Determinism was not enforced partway through because that would have made later runs noncomparable with earlier ones. Two skin analyses initially contained an evaluation error; both
were re-evaluated from the same checkpoints, and all figures reported here are the corrected ones.
That variation is adopted as a measurement floor, and its scope requires precise statement
because it does not apply uniformly. The band was derived from the difference between two
11

--- PAGE 12 [pdf_text] ---
single runs and therefore bounds point comparisons: where a configuration is represented by
one number, a difference below 0.013 DSC cannot be distinguished from run-to-run variation
and is not interpreted, irrespective of sign. This governs the capacity axis, both task-specific
architectures, the data-size sweep and every learning-rate search. It is a reference drawn from
one repeated configuration rather than a measured bound for every experiment.
It does not govern the paired significance tests of Section 4.5, which measure a different
quantity. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test applied to seed-averaged per-image vectors assesses not
the magnitude of a difference but its consistency across images: a small difference holding in
the same direction on most images is significant, while a larger one varying erratically is not.
The polyp comparison demonstrates this within a single table, where CVC-ColonDB shows a
difference of +0.0022 with the lowest p-value of the five at 0.0001, while ETIS shows the largest at
+0.0130 and is not significant at p = 0.1191. Reporting either criterion alone would misdescribe
the comparison, so multi-seed results are reported by direction and p-value with magnitude given
but not used as a threshold, and single-run comparisons against the band and never with a
p-value. Because the signed-rank statistic is computed on the ranks of the absolute per-image
differences, magnitude enters the test through those ranks; and because images rather than seeds
are the unit of pairing, the test does not directly assess variation across seeds.
Segmentation quality is reported with six metrics: the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and
intersection over union (IoU) for overlap; sensitivity and precision, which separate a missed lesion
from over-segmentation; and, for the boundary, two spatial-distance metrics in the taxonomy of
Taha and Hanbury [48], the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95), taken over the boundary
distances in both directions, and the average symmetric surface distance (ASSD), the mean of
the two directed mean boundary distances, together with the normalised surface Dice (NSD) [64],
the fraction of boundary points of each mask lying within a tolerance of the other’s boundary.
All are accumulated per image rather than pooled, since pooling weights each image by its
lesion area; per-image accumulation also yields a vector rather than a scalar, which is what
makes significance testing possible. Where multiple seeds are available, comparisons are tested
by Wilcoxon signed-rank [65] per test set, preferred to a paired t-test because per-image DSC
distributions are strongly left-skewed, and to a Mann–Whitney test because it retains the pairing.
Single-seed results are not tested, since a p-value on one run would characterise variation across
images rather than across runs.
Distances are measured in pixels at the resolution each model was evaluated at, because most
of the datasets do not record physical pixel spacing. That resolution differs between domains,
and within skin between the adapted and the task-specific models (Section 3.5), so HD95 and
ASSD are also reported divided by the image diagonal; only the normalised values are compared
across resolutions, and pixel values only within one. The NSD tolerance is defined as a fixed
fraction of the image width, 2/224, which is 2 pixels at 224, 3.4 at 384 and 3.5 at 392; results at
twice that tolerance are given in Table 8. Where exactly one of the two masks is empty, HD95
and ASSD are set to the image diagonal at that resolution and NSD to zero, and the number of
such images is reported together with the boundary metrics over the images where both masks
are non-empty. A prediction that is empty when a lesion is present is counted as precision zero.
No reference mask was empty at the evaluated resolution in any test set, so the case of an empty
reference never arose.
DSC was the only criterion used for selection. The other five metrics were computed afterwards
from the same checkpoints, and no model or configuration choice depends on them. Before they
were computed, the per-image DSC of every checkpoint was recomputed and compared with the
corresponding values reported in this paper, 130 checks in all across the tables of this paper;
every value agreed to within 0.001, the largest deviation being 0.0007.

Averages conceal how a model fails. Table 10 counts the images on which a model predicts no
lesion at all, although every reference mask in every test set contains one. The plain U-Net never
does so, and the residual variant on four polyp images. DINOv2 at the selected configuration

NSD on all three sets. Skin also allows a comparison within one domain, because its data-size
sweep was run at 224 pixels (Table 12). At 224 pixels the residual U-Net leads in NSD at the
2/224 tolerance on all three skin sets while DINOv2 still leads in DSC and HD95; at the native
resolutions DINOv2 leads in all of them. Within a domain, the direction of the fine-boundary
comparison therefore changes with resolution, which is consistent with the patch grid limiting
boundary precision. It is not an isolated test: the two pairs also differ in training budget, and at
the lower data fractions of the same sweep the NSD comparison is mixed. The empty predictions
on small polyps fit the same account, a lesion of one per cent of a 224-pixel image covering
between two and three of its 256 patches, but this was not tested directly.
4.4. More data closes the in-domain gap but not the out-of-distribution one
Two explanations were left open for the variation in deficit magnitude: the domains differ in
training-set size, and in the severity of the shift their test sets represent. This section addresses the
first. Each domain was retrained at 25 and 50 per cent of its training set, and polyp additionally
at 10 per cent, with source, architecture, evaluation and augmentation fixed, and budgets scaled
inversely with subset size, which isolates data volume from modality and task difficulty as the
cross-domain comparison cannot.
The cross-domain figures invite a straightforward reading: ordered by training-set size and by
out-of-distribution deficit the domains fall in inverse order, so the deficit appears to shrink as
data grows. That reading is incorrect, since the domains differ in modality, lesion appearance,
annotation protocol and test-set provenance, any of which could produce the same ordering.
Comparing points of equal size across domains, as Fig. 7 shows, contradicts the confounded
reading directly. At 518 images skin shows −0.013, at the boundary of the floor, while at 652
images, twenty-six per cent more data, polyp shows −0.132, ten times larger. The relationship does
not merely weaken across domains; it reverses. Within domains it does hold, falling monotonically
in polyp and less regularly in breast, and remaining indistinguishable from zero in skin at every
fraction. The correct statement is conditional: within a domain more data reduces the deficit;
across domains data volume does not predict it.
The in-domain column tells a different story, and the contrast is the substantive finding of this
section. Three of the seven configurations with an in-domain measurement fall within the floor,
polyp at its full training set, and breast at 119 and 239 images, and at 479 images the task-specific
model in breast is ahead by +0.021, a margin that clears it on the 54-image validation split. Only
polyp requires its full set to reach parity, and even at a quarter of its data the in-domain gap is
−0.050 against −0.135 out of distribution on the same models. The plain U-Net reproduces this,
its gaps moving from −0.034 and −0.121 at 326 images to +0.002 and −0.041 at 1,305, so the
behaviour is not specific to one model; the 326-image run stopped at epoch 367 of its 1,200-epoch
budget, so its deficit is not a training artefact. Stated directly: a task-specific model reaches
parity on data from the training distribution using, in breast, a fraction of the available data,
and does not reach it on data from a different source using all of it.

than of how much data it provides. Section 4.7 reports three attempts to identify it, none of

4.5. Adaptation capacity requirements differ by domain
Given that a foundation model is warranted where cross-site deployment is intended, the
remaining question is how much adaptation it requires. This section reports four experiments.
4.5.1. Parameter-efficient adaptation matches full fine-tuning
LoRA at rank 32 was compared against full fine-tuning in polyp across three seeds at 20 epochs,
with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on seed-averaged per-image vectors (Table 16). LoRA minus
full fine-tuning was +0.0058 on Kvasir (p = 0.0003), +0.0101 on CVC-ClinicDB (p = 0.0194),
+0.0022 on CVC-ColonDB (p = 0.0001), +0.0130 on ETIS (p = 0.1191) and +0.0006 on CVC-T
(p = 0.3931); for the last two the test records a failure to reject, not equivalence. LoRA is equal
or ahead on all five, significantly on three, and full fine-tuning wins nowhere despite updating
thirty-seven times as many parameters. The magnitudes are small and fall at or below the floor
governing single-run comparisons, but that floor does not apply to a paired test over images
whose scores are averaged across three seeds. Skin gives comparable differences, significant on two
of its three sets at the same rank. This is consistent with the wider literature and is not novel; it
is reported because the rest of the section depends on it. In the other metrics the comparison is
not one-sided (Table 17). LoRA is also ahead on all five sets in IoU and NSD, significantly on
three in each, and has significantly higher sensitivity on four, but its precision is significantly
lower on all three out-of-distribution sets, by up to 0.049 on CVC-ColonDB: at equal or better
DSC it covers more of each polyp and more of its surroundings than full fine-tuning does. LoRA
at rank 32 is therefore equal or ahead in DSC, IoU and NSD, not in every metric.
4.5.2. The rank requirement differs by domain
Swept over ranks 4, 8, 16 and 32 under the 20-epoch protocol, the domains give three profiles.
Skin gains nothing from rank beyond 4, no set moving beyond the floor and the largest change to
rank 32 being +0.005 on ISIC 2017*; breast improves to rank 16 then plateaus; polyp continues
improving at 32. Expressed as a requirement, skin needs approximately rank 4, breast 16 and
polyp at least 32, a factor of eight, so no single default serves all three. One feature of the training
logs qualifies this: at 20 epochs the low ranks were still improving while the high ranks had
converged, so part of what is measured as capacity may be a convergence rate.
4.5.3. The effect of the epoch budget
The qualification at the end of the previous section is testable, and this experiment tests
it. If the low ranks were still improving at 20 epochs while the high ranks had converged, then
extending the budget should shrink the measured rank effect where it reflects convergence and
leave it where it reflects capacity. Polyp and breast were therefore rerun at 40 epochs with all
else unchanged, and the rank effect was measured as each domain’s span at 20 epochs, rank 16
minus rank 4 in breast and rank 32 minus rank 4 in polyp (Table 18).
In breast the out-of-distribution rank effect largely dissolves. It vanishes on BrEaST, from
+0.030 to +0.001, where rank 4 comes within 0.001 of rank 16 given twice the budget, and
halves on BUS-UCLM, from +0.023 to +0.014, which sits at the edge of the floor.
What
appeared at 20 epochs as a requirement of rank 16 was therefore in substantial part a difference
in convergence speed. In polyp it survives and grows slightly, from +0.046 to +0.055 on CVCColonDB. Convergence was verified rather than assumed, the rank-16 and full fine-tuning runs
early-stopping at epochs 31 and 35 while the rest moved by no more than 0.001 over their final
six epochs.
The in-domain measurement behaves differently within the same domain: doubling the budget
leaves it almost unchanged, from +0.040 to +0.038, while the out-of-distribution effect largely
dissolves, so the two were measuring different things at 20 epochs, in-domain reflecting capacity
and out-of-distribution largely reflecting optimisation progress. The difference between reporting
a requirement of 16 and one of 4 in breast rests entirely on whether the experiment ran for 20 or

4.5.4. The requirement is not specific to LoRA
Rank belongs to LoRA’s mechanism, and it remains possible that what varies is how well
low-rank factorisation suits each domain’s required update. BitFit tests this, having no rank
parameter, intervening at bias terms throughout the encoder, and using 51,841 parameters, below
LoRA at rank 4 (Table 19, Fig. 8).
The domain ordering reproduces under BitFit. Taking BitFit minus LoRA r=32 across the
eight out-of-distribution sets gives −0.001, −0.008 and −0.004 in skin; +0.020 and +0.040 in
breast; and −0.003, −0.012 and −0.049 in polyp. On seven of eight sets, 51,841 parameters
match or exceed what 590,209 achieve, and the single deficit outside the floor is CVC-ColonDB.
Polyp is thus the only domain where the eleven-fold larger budget buys anything, and it is the
domain the rank sweep identified as requiring rank 32. Two methods differing in parameter
count, in intervention site, and in whether they possess a rank parameter at all place the same
domain above the others, which locates the requirement in the domain rather than in low-rank
factorisation.
Two features of the axis are consistent where the full axis is available. The initial step carries
most of the benefit: frozen to BitFit gains +0.192 on ETIS, +0.133 on CVC-ColonDB and +0.102
on CVC-T in polyp, and +0.124 and +0.079 in breast, and on every one the remaining step
to 22.1 million is smaller than the first. This is restricted to polyp and breast, the skin frozen
configuration not being comparable. And full fine-tuning does not lead: across the eight sets
it is highest only on ETIS, so twenty-two million trainable parameters purchase no consistent
advantage over fifty thousand, and this on the smallest DINOv2 variant, where the case for
updating the whole encoder is strongest.
Beyond the first step the domains diverge. In polyp performance continues to rise, CVCColonDB gaining +0.055 between ranks 4 and 32; in breast and skin it does not, the corresponding
changes being −0.003, −0.004 and between +0.001 and +0.005. Skin therefore requires very
little adaptation of any kind, a conclusion resting on the flatness of its axis rather than on any
frozen baseline. And the in-domain measurement diverges from the out-of-distribution one as it
did in the budget control: in breast, rank 4 to rank 32 gains +0.046 on validation while both
out-of-distribution sets move by less than 0.005. Additional capacity produces a model that fits
the training distribution better without generalising better.

4.6. Stability of the optimal configuration
The preceding sections each reported a comparison; this section examines the comparisons
themselves. Because the same comparisons were revisited, at two ranks, at two training budgets,
against a second selection criterion, and after correcting a mismatched representational comparison,
it is possible to observe which of their conclusions survive. Four comparisons were re-examined. In
three an initial finding did not hold: two when a single parameter of that configuration was varied,
and one when a mismatch between domains in estimator, rank and sample size was corrected.
The fourth compared the same runs under a second selection criterion. A study conducted at a
single operating point, which is the norm in this literature, would have reported all four in their
original form.
4.6.1. The rank artefact
The comparison between LoRA and full fine-tuning was first conducted at rank 8, with three
seeds and Wilcoxon testing, and produced a clear result: full fine-tuning led on two of three
skin sets, significantly on both (20 epochs; Table 20). At rank 32 the conclusion reverses on
ISIC 2016*, from −0.004 (p = 0.0010) to +0.003 (p = 0.0023), and weakens on ISIC 2017* from
significant to no difference, from −0.017 (p = 0.0004) to −0.002 (p = 0.3218), while PH2 favours
LoRA at both ranks; the same reversal occurs in polyp, where CVC-ColonDB moves from −0.024
at rank 4 to +0.022 at rank 32 in the single-seed 20-epoch rank sweep. Both configurations are
defensible: rank 8 is a common default and was not chosen adversarially, both comparisons used
three seeds and significance testing, and both reported p below 0.05 on ISIC 2016*. A reader
given either in isolation, with its methodology fully described, would have no basis for doubting
it. What separates them is a single hyperparameter of one of the two methods compared. The
finding that full fine-tuning outperforms LoRA is not false at rank 8; it is true at rank 8 and false
at rank 32, and reporting it without the rank makes it appear a statement about the methods
rather than about one configuration of one of them.
4.6.2. The selection-criterion artefact
Selection was performed on in-domain validation DSC throughout, with out-of-distribution
scores recorded but unused, which makes it possible to ask after the fact whether the configuration
selected was the one best out of distribution. In six independent searches, only one did. In the
BitFit learning-rate search in polyp at 40 epochs (Table 22), validation selects 1e-3, best on three
of five test sets but not on CVC-ClinicDB, where 1e-2 leads by 0.005, or CVC-ColonDB, where
3e-3 leads by 0.009. The plain U-Net search behaves identically, validation selecting 1e-3 while
CVC-ColonDB and CVC-T are better served by 3e-3. The BitFit search in skin diverges on one
of its three test sets, PH2 being better served by 3e-4 by 0.008. Two remaining instances are of
the same kind: in the ResU-Net search validation selected 3e-3 while ETIS was best served by
1e-2, and in the breast rank sweep in-domain performance rose monotonically with rank while
out-of-distribution performance did not. The sixth search, BitFit in breast, is the exception:
validation selected 3e-3, which was also best on both out-of-distribution sets, at 0.7337 on BrEaST
and 0.7449 on BUS-UCLM.
The criterion applied here is rank order on individual test sets: in five of six independent
searches, across two hyperparameters and two model families, the validation-optimal configuration
was not the out-of-distribution-optimal one on at least one test set. Under a stricter criterion the
picture is weaker. Averaged over the three polyp out-of-distribution sets the validation choice is
also the best of the four rates, at 0.757 against 0.744 for the nearest alternative, and the two
individual divergences of 0.005 and 0.009 fall below the floor; in skin the same criterion costs
0.002, again within it. What the searches establish is therefore that the validation optimum does
not coincide with the optimum on every deployment target, not that selecting on validation carries
a measurable performance penalty. The direction of the misalignment is structural, since selection
must use data available at training time, which is in-domain by construction; its magnitude is
small and was not the object of this study.

4.6.3. The mismatched-comparison artefact
Centred kernel alignment [49] was used to measure how far each strategy moves the encoder’s
representations from their pretrained state, and the initial analysis produced an interpretable
result: LoRA preserved the early blocks while full fine-tuning perturbed them throughout, fitting
the expectation that a constrained update should stay closer to its initialisation. But three
variables differed between the domains analysed, polyp using the biased estimator at rank 32
on one sample size and skin the debiased estimator at rank 8 on another. Repeated under one
consistent procedure, the debiased estimator of Nguyen et al. [50] at matched ranks with fixed
sample size, the result reverses: LoRA drifts further from the pretrained representation than full
fine-tuning, in both domains and at both ranks, with no exception at any block. The estimator
was not the cause; the reversal came from the mismatched rank and sample size. The corrected
direction is explained by layer-wise decay, which holds full fine-tuning’s lower layers close to
initialisation by construction while LoRA applies a uniform rate at every depth. Unlike the first
two cases, the original analysis was simply wrong rather than conditional on a defensible choice;
it is reported because the corrected finding is used in Section 4.7, and because the erroneous
version was interpretable and fitted theoretical expectation.
4.6.4. Synthesis
The fourth instability, the epoch-budget artefact, appeared in Section 4.5 because it determined
the budget used thereafter; Table 21 summarises all four. The four cases are independent, arising
in different experiments. Two follow from varying an operating point, the rank and the budget;
the other two are a post-hoc comparison of two selection criteria on the same runs and the
correction of a comparison whose estimator, rank and sample size had differed between domains.
What they share is that each was interpretable and internally consistent before it was examined a

second time: one was supported by significance testing across three seeds, the two operating-point
findings were undetectable from within the original experiment, and the mismatched comparison
was detectable only by checking its procedure.
4.7. Candidate explanations for the domain effect
The deficit of task-specific training varies by domain and not by architecture or training-set
size, and the capacity required varies by domain and not by method. In both cases skin is the
least demanding, though the two axes differ on the ordering of polyp and breast. If it could
be predicted from a measurable property, a practitioner could estimate in advance whether a
foundation model is warranted and what budget it requires. Three analyses were run. Two test
candidate predictors of the domain effect: distributional distance between training and test data,
and the spectral dimension of the fine-tuning update. The third asks a related question, whether
the difference between the two adaptation methods lies in the dimensionality of the solutions they
reach. None succeeded, and the negative results are reported because the failure of the obvious
explanations is itself informative.
Maximum mean discrepancy [67] was computed between the training set and each test set
on features from the pretrained encoder. The measure behaves sensibly on its own terms, the
in-domain sets being closest and the estimator returning 0.0380 against a resampled training split
and 0.0001 against a shuffled control. The prediction nonetheless fails, and on the most extreme
point: CVC-T has the highest MMD2 at 0.200, nearly twice CVC-ColonDB’s 0.113, yet is the
easiest out-of-distribution set at 0.867 under LoRA r=32 (20 epochs, three-seed mean), twelve
points above CVC-ColonDB and seventeen above ETIS. Since every CVC-T image also occurs in
CVC-ColonDB (Section 3.2), this compares a subset of one collection with the whole of it, which
sharpens the dissociation: the subset lies further from the training data than the collection it is
drawn from, and is easier. Skin gives the same picture in miniature: PH2 has the largest MMD2
at 0.0739, an order of magnitude above ISIC 2017* at 0.0070, and is not correspondingly harder.
Distance in the pretrained feature space is not difficulty, since a set can be far from the training
distribution in a way the encoder registers while remaining easy to segment.
The second candidate addresses the adaptation axis. If a domain requires a higher rank, the
update full fine-tuning finds might itself be higher-dimensional, and the singular value spectrum
of that update was computed across all encoder blocks. Measured as the number of singular
directions capturing 90 per cent of the update’s energy, it is 28.4 in skin, 39.0 in breast and 42.1
in polyp at 20 epochs (Table 23). At 20 epochs the measure tracks the ordering, skin lowest and
polyp highest, but two problems prevent it being a usable predictor. It separates the domains
far less than the rank sweep does, spectral dimension spanning a factor of 1.5 against eight for
rank, and breast sits closer to polyp than to skin on this measure while behaving like skin on the
capacity axis at the extended budget. And the measure is budget-sensitive, breast rising from
39.0 to 44.3 between 20 and 40 epochs, a change larger than the gap between breast and polyp at
either budget, so it is not intrinsic to the domain: the epoch-budget artefact in a second guise.

5. Discussion
5.1. Adaptation
Four findings concern adaptation itself: where it helps, what it costs, how much of it is needed,
and how far comparisons of it can be trusted.
1. The advantage of adaptation lies out of distribution.
Where the deployment target is
the training distribution and the full training set is available, no accuracy advantage for
adaptation was observed. Averaged over the two in-domain polyp test sets the plain UNet scores within the measurement floor of the adapted model, at fewer parameters and
no pretraining, and in breast both task-specific models score above it on the validation
split, though that split also served for model selection. On the smaller polyp subsets
adaptation does lead. Where the target is data from a different institution, scanner or
protocol, adaptation leads on every set examined: all sixteen comparisons across eight sets
and two independently tuned architectures, although the two on CVC-T repeat those on
CVC-ColonDB rather than adding independent evidence. Recomputed without CVC-T, the
polyp out-of-distribution means fall, but no ordering, sign or trend reported in Section 4
changes (Table 25). The margin varies by more than a factor of ten and is conditional
on training-set size in a specific way: within a domain more data reduces it, but across
domains data volume does not predict it.
2. The advantage has a cost in small lesions and fine boundaries. It is not uniform across
metrics and kinds of error (Section 4.3): it holds in overlap, sensitivity, precision and HD95,
but at the finest boundary scale the task-specific models are as good or better at 224 pixels,
and the adapted model more often misses a small lesion altogether, 13 polyp images at the
selected configuration against none for the plain U-Net.
3. Little adaptation is needed. The step from a frozen encoder to BitFit captures most of the
available benefit in polyp and breast, and beyond it LoRA at 2.7 per cent of the parameters

the in-domain effect intact. Of the four cases in Section 4.6, three revised a finding: two

comparison was corrected; in each the original version claimed the larger effect. The fourth
compared two selection criteria post hoc on the same runs.
5.2. Domain
The clearest result of this work is that the amount of adaptation required belongs to the
domain rather than to the method. The rank at which adaptation ceases to improve varies across
the three domains, polyp continuing to improve to rank 32, breast plateauing at 16 and skin
showing no movement beyond rank 4 under the 20-epoch protocol, though Section 4.5.3 shows
the breast effect largely dissolves out of distribution at twice that budget. Polyp is also the one
domain where BitFit falls behind, while skin and breast are served by the smallest budget tested
under both methods. That two methods differing by a factor of eleven in parameter count, in
intervention site, and in whether they possess a rank parameter at all place the same domain
above the others locates the requirement in the domain. Other configuration choices behave the
32

--- PAGE 33 [pdf_text] ---
same way: the learning rate selected on validation differed between architectures and between
domains, and the optimal budget differed by domain, so a study on any one of these domains
would have produced an internally consistent set of settings that does not transfer to the other
two.
The architecture axis places the domains in a related but not identical order. Both axes
separate skin from the other two, skin showing the smallest deficit of task-specific training as
well as the smallest capacity requirement, but they differ on polyp and breast, the mean deficit
in Table 6 being larger in breast than in polyp under both architectures. Four explanations for
the domain-dependence can be set aside. It is not the task-specific architecture, since the deficit
holds under two such models with identical sign on all eight sets, the simpler being the stronger
out of distribution. It is not the method, since the ordering holds under LoRA and BitFit,
which share no mechanism. It is not distance in the pretrained feature space, since the polyp
set with the highest discrepancy is the easiest. And it is not the encoder family, since the same
transformer without adaptation reaches 0.614 out of distribution in polyp against 0.737 for the
plain U-Net and in breast falls within the measurement floor of the plain U-Net, so the advantage
appears only once adaptation is applied. Architecture and pretraining remain confounded, since
no convolutional encoder pretrained at comparable scale was included, but the frozen result shows
the encoder family alone does not produce the effect. Training-set size is a fifth candidate and is
only partly addressed: on the architecture axis the deficit does not follow a simple ordering by
size, skin at 518 images showing a much smaller gap than polyp at 652.
The ordering also conceals a difference in what kind of shift each test set represents. Two of
three skin sets are earlier releases of the training archive, suggesting skin’s small deficit reflects a
milder shift rather than a property of dermoscopy. The prediction that follows fails: PH2, the
only independent skin source, should show the largest skin deficit and under the plain U-Net
shows the smallest. Within-domain variation is of comparable magnitude, so treating the domain
as the unit of analysis is a simplification the test-set-level results only partly support.
The account most consistent with the results is that domains differ in how much of the
required update is task-general rather than domain-specific, and that pretraining supplies the
former. A foundation model arrives with representations encoding much of what segmentation
requires, and adaptation supplies only the domain-specific remainder. Where that remainder is
small, little capacity is needed and pretraining transfers well; where it is large, more is needed,
and a model trained from random initialisation must learn the general component from target
data alone, which it can do well enough to fit the training distribution but not well enough to
transfer. This fits four observations: why the first step on the capacity axis carries most of the
benefit, why the requirement is method-independent, why task-specific models are competitive
in domain but not out of it, and why more data narrows the in-domain gap faster. It was not
tested, and Section 4.7 records the closest attempt.
That the plain variant outperforms the residual one is itself unexpected, and the most plausible
reconciliation is scale. Residual connections address optimisation difficulty in deep networks, and
at five encoder levels and under eight million parameters that difficulty may not arise, leaving the
additional skip projections as parameters that fit the training distribution without contributing
out of distribution. This is untested here.
5.3. Practical recommendations
The first four recommendations are for practitioners choosing between the approaches, the
last two for studies comparing them.
1. Use an adapted foundation model when the model will be deployed on data from other sites,
scanners or protocols. It led on all eight out-of-distribution test sets under both task-specific
architectures, in DSC, IoU, sensitivity and HD95, and its lead grows as training data shrink,
most clearly in polyp. When deployment stays within the training source and the full
training set is available, a well-configured U-Net is sufficient: no accuracy advantage for
33

--- PAGE 34 [pdf_text] ---
adaptation was observed there, and the case for a foundation model must then rest on
engineering effort or on one backbone serving several tasks.
2. Start with the smallest adaptation and do not default to full fine-tuning. Moving from a
frozen encoder to BitFit captures most of the benefit in polyp and breast, and LoRA at 2.7
per cent of the parameters equalled or exceeded full fine-tuning in DSC on all five polyp
sets.
3. Sweep the adaptation capacity on the target domain instead of inheriting it. The LoRA
rank required ranged from 4 in skin to 32 in polyp, and the same domain pattern appeared
under BitFit, so a value tuned on another dataset is not a valid default.
4. Check small lesions and boundaries explicitly before deployment. At 224 pixels the adapted
model missed small polyps entirely where the U-Nets did not, and a U-Net traced boundaries
more precisely on four of the five polyp and breast out-of-distribution sets. Where small
lesions or exact boundaries matter, report sensitivity, empty predictions and NSD alongside
DSC, and evaluate at the highest resolution the budget allows: at 392 pixels the adapted
model led in NSD on all three skin sets.
5. Report in-domain and out-of-distribution results separately. An in-domain result is not
evidence for cross-site deployment: on all three axes studied here the in-domain ordering
did not reliably carry over out of distribution, and in Section 4.5.3 the two regimes diverged
within a single experiment.
6. Establish a measurement floor, and vary the operating point, before reporting a difference as
a property of a method. Repeated runs at an identical seed differed by up to 0.013 DSC,
of the same order as the improvements this literature commonly reports, and two findings
here reversed or weakened when the LoRA rank or the training budget was changed; where
a comparison involves a hyperparameter of one method, establish the conclusion at more
than one value of it.
5.4. Limitations
Seven limitations bound these recommendations, each with the study that would remove it.
1. A single backbone. All four adaptation strategies were applied to DINOv2-Small, so while
the capacity requirement is shown not to be specific to the adaptation method, whether it
is specific to the backbone was not tested, and these results do not recommend DINOv2
over other backbones. Repeating the adaptation axis on a second encoder such as SAM
under an identical protocol would settle this.
2. Asymmetric tuning. BitFit received a per-domain learning-rate search while LoRA and
full fine-tuning used rates held constant from earlier experiments, which favours BitFit,
and part of its standing may reflect that search. The task-specific learning-rate search was
conducted on polyp alone, and in skin the plain variant ran at the residual variant’s rate.
3. Mostly single-seed results. Only the core comparison between LoRA and full fine-tuning
used three seeds and significance testing; the capacity axis, BitFit, the plain U-Net and the
data-size sweep are reported as signal and read against the measurement floor of Section 3.6.
Three configurations run three times each would give a per-domain variance estimate.
4. Three domains. They establish that the capacity requirement varies by domain, but not
what determines it or how to anticipate it for a fourth domain without its data; answering
that requires more domains under one protocol. Whether the requirement also varies with
training-set size was not tested, since the rank sweep used one training-set size per domain,
and the domain ordering should not be extrapolated beyond the three domains studied.
5. Training-set range. In-domain parity was observed at the full training sets, of 479 to 2,075
images, and in breast down to 119 images, while on the smaller polyp subsets adaptation
led; where parity breaks down was not located, so these results do not show pretraining
unnecessary in domain in general.
34

--- PAGE 35 [pdf_text] ---
6. A minimal decoder. The linear decoder isolates encoder adaptation but makes the frozen
configuration a minimal baseline; repeating the frozen and BitFit configurations with a
convolutional decoder would separate the decoder’s contribution.
7. The breast in-domain figure. It comes from the validation split, which was also used for
early stopping, and is therefore optimistically biased. Polyp’s in-domain sets are separate
from the validation split, and skin has no in-domain figure.
5.5. Relation to previous work
Evaluation hazards in standard benchmarks. Two hazards in the data used here were found
by content-based checks that file-hash comparison does not replicate. In the polyp protocol, a
pixel-level comparison shows that all 60 CVC-T images are identical to images in CVC-ColonDB,
as are 59 of their 60 masks, while no file is byte-identical, so an md5 check passes; this is consistent
with EndoScene defining its images as drawn from CVC-ColonDB [53]. Studies following the
standard protocol therefore count these images twice when reporting generalisation. In the breast
training source, a perceptual hash at a Hamming distance of five or less placed 212 of the 647
lesion-bearing BUSI images in 98 near-duplicate groups, only one of which contains byte-identical
files; keeping one image per group removed 114 images (17.6 per cent of the 647), and inspection
of a sample of four groups showed the same acquisitions annotated twice with differing masks.
The count depends on the threshold, from 89 images (13.8 per cent) at a distance of three to 137
(21.2 per cent) at eight. All 114 were removed before any experiment reported here.
That LoRA matches full fine-tuning at a fraction of the parameters is established outside
medical imaging and is confirmed here, and the BitFit result extends a claim reported for language
tasks in low-data regimes to dense prediction on medical images and to cross-dataset evaluation,
neither of which the original work or its medical successors examine.
More interesting is the disagreement of Section 2.3. Dutt et al. [2] attribute the ordering
among methods to data volume; Teuber et al. [3] attribute it to dataset characteristics. These
results support the second, and support the explanation it offers for the disagreement: Section 4.5
finds BitFit ahead of LoRA on both breast out-of-distribution sets, level on all three skin sets, and
behind on one polyp set, a ranking that changes with the dataset rather than holding globally. A
study on any one of these domains would report a different ordering, each correct about its own,
so the disagreement need not be an error on either side. One difference in scope should be noted
alongside the agreement. Their base model is SAM, supervised on segmentation masks, whereas
DINOv2 has never seen one, so the amount of adaptation each requires is not the same quantity;
and their evaluation does not separate in-domain from cross-dataset performance, whereas that
divergence is the central finding here.
Isensee et al. [1] report strong performance from residual-encoder configurations, which
motivated the residual variant used here. Under the conditions of this study, that variant,
with residual blocks in both encoder and decoder, did not outperform its plain counterpart.
Because it is not the nnU-Net ResEnc configuration and the setting differs, three-dimensional
and multi-class on larger datasets there, two-dimensional and binary here, the result does not
test their recommendation directly.
The literature on foundation models for medical segmentation generally reports adapted
models outperforming task-specific training. These results agree out of distribution and disagree
in domain, finding no advantage in either domain where an in-domain comparison is possible, with
parity reached at 119 training images in breast, against baselines Section 4.2 shows competitive
with published architectures. The discrepancy is most likely one of evaluation regime rather
than of result: studies reporting an advantage frequently evaluate on data from the training
distribution or do not distinguish the regimes, and where the distinction is drawn the advantage
concentrates entirely out of distribution. This locates the existing result more precisely rather
than contradicting it.
Independent support comes from breadth rather than depth. U-Bench [46] reports that
improvements under zero-shot generalisation are more consistently significant than those measured
35

--- PAGE 36 [pdf_text] ---
in domain, which is the same divergence between regimes observed from the opposite direction:
where this work finds an in-domain measurement failing to predict out-of-distribution behaviour,
U-Bench finds the out-of-distribution measurement separating methods the in-domain one does
not.
Finally, the comparison drawn throughout this study is binary, and a recent line of work
pursues a third option: distilling foundation-model representations into a lightweight architecture
so that the result carries those priors at the parameter count of a U-Net. Polyp-DiFoM [68] distils
SAM, DINOv2, OneFormer and Mask2Former into U-Net students and evaluates on the same five
polyp sets used here. This follows from the present comparison rather than competing with it: if
the advantage is concentrated out of distribution and a lightweight architecture already matches
in domain, transferring that component into the smaller model is the natural next step, and the
domain dependence found here implies that a distillation method validated on one domain should
not be assumed to transfer either.
6. Conclusion
This study compared foundation-model adaptation with task-specific training from scratch
for medical image segmentation, separating in-domain from out-of-distribution evaluation across
three domains and eight out-of-distribution test sets. Three conclusions follow.
1. Adaptation is warranted when the deployment target differs from the training source:
an adapted DINOv2 outperformed two independently tuned U-Nets on all eight out-ofdistribution sets in DSC, IoU, sensitivity and HD95, whereas within the training distribution
and at the full training set a well-configured U-Net matched it.
2. Little adaptation is needed: bias-only tuning captures most of the benefit, and LoRA at 2.7
per cent of the parameters equals or exceeds full fine-tuning in DSC on all five polyp sets.
3. How much adaptation is needed is a property of the domain rather than of the method,
varying eightfold in LoRA rank across domains and reproduced by a method with no rank
parameter, so capacity must be selected on the target domain.
These results were obtained with DINOv2, a small, general-purpose foundation model never
trained on segmentation masks or medical images; larger or medical-specific foundation models
would be expected to perform at least as well.
