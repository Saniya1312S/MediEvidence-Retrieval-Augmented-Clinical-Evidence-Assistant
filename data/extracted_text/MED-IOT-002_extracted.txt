Abstract. Smart healthcare IoT requires low-latency and reliable delivery of patient data, yet IP-based communication remains host-centric
and offers limited support for mobility, caching, and request aggregation. This paper evaluates a smartphone-centric Named Data Networking (NDN) approach in which a patient’s smartphone aggregates sensor
streams and publishes a stable “latest profile” under a consistent name,
enabling effective in-network caching. Using ndnSIM simulations on a
realistic medical IoT topology, we compare NDN and IP under identical traffic and link conditions. Results show that per-sample naming
provides little benefit, but smartphone aggregation with stable naming
allows NDN caching to reduce delay after cache warm-up and achieve
significantly higher delivery ratios under congestion compared to IP.
Keywords: Named Data Network · Smart Healthcare · IoHT · Mobile
Health.
1
Introduction
The growing use of wearable sensors, home medical devices, and mobile health
(mHealth) applications is moving healthcare from occasional, hospital-centered
visits to continuous, patient-centered monitoring. In these systems, body-worn
and ambient sensors measure vital signs and other physiological parameters and
send them, often over short-range and multi-hop wireless links, to a nearby
gateway such as a smartphone, which must then forward the data to healthcare staff with low delay and high reliability [11, 2]. Many smart-hospital and
remote-monitoring platforms already combine IoT, edge, and cloud components
to provide such continuous services [13]. However, most of these platforms are
built on the traditional TCP/IP protocol stack and therefore inherit its known
limits in terms of scalability, mobility support, and efficient, secure data delivery in dense and dynamic IoT environments [8, 3]. Beyond performance concerns,

the underlying networking substrate can also influence how efficiently data and
resources are shared across stakeholders, i.e., whether the network itself can facilitate a form of network-level collective intelligence by enabling reuse and local
sharing of previously retrieved medical information.
The traditional Internet architecture is host-centric: routers forward packets
toward IP addresses that identify endpoints rather than the data itself. This
model is not well suited for data-intensive smart environments and IoT systems, where applications are mainly interested in named content (for example,
the latest ECG of a given patient) instead of a specific server. Recent surveys
on NDN in smart environments and IoT report several limitations of the IP
model in this setting, including complex address management, limited mobility
support, and lack of pervasive in-network caching, which are especially problematic in dense and dynamic IoT healthcare environments. This paper focuses on
smartphone-centric IoT healthcare and demonstrates how NDN can overcome
these challenges in such settings by offloading much of the traffic management to
in-network caches and enabling faster, more efficient data retrieval. In this sense,
repeated clinical queries (e.g., by a physician, a clinic system, or an authorized
service) can benefit from data that the network has already fetched, effectively
turning the network into a shared pool of reusable content. By eliminating the
need for traditional IP-based address resolution and supporting mobility, NDN
proves to be a better fit for healthcare IoT systems where real-time data from
wearable sensors is crucial [8, 12, 1, 3]. These studies argue that simply running
application-layer protocols such as MQTT or CoAP over IP cannot fully solve
these structural problems; instead, the network layer itself must evolve toward
a data-centric design.
Named Data Networking has been proposed as a prominent informationcentric networking (ICN) architecture to address these limitations [14]. In NDN,
applications request content by hierarchical names rather than by host addresses;
routers forward Interest packets according to name prefixes and return Data
packets that can be cached at any hop. This leads to a stateful forwarding plane,
based on Pending Interest Tables (PITs), Forwarding Information Bases (FIBs),
and Content Stores (CSs), which natively supports in-network caching, multipath retrieval, and efficient multicast, while data-centric security is achieved by
cryptographically binding each Data packet to its producer, independent of the
delivery path [14]. As a result, the distributed CS across routers can be viewed
as a continuously updated, network-wide knowledge pool: once data is retrieved,
it becomes locally available for subsequent consumers without requiring them to
know the original producer. Moreover, the cooperative behavior of PIT aggregation and CS hits enables decentralized coordination of data retrieval, allowing
the network as a whole to efficiently satisfy repeated requests without a central
controller. Surveys on NDN for smart environments and on hierarchical naming
for NDN-IoT conclude that these properties align well with the requirements
of smart cities, buildings, and health spaces, but also emphasize open issues in
naming scalability, resource constraints, and security [8, 12].

Recent work suggests NDN can improve performance and security in healthcare IoT [13, 6, 7, 9], but many studies focus on architecture/security/caching
strategies rather than a clean baseline comparison against IP under matched conditions [3, 8, 12, 1]. This paper isolates the effect of NDN’s caching and request
aggregation in a realistic smartphone-centric healthcare topology and compares
delay and delivery reliability to IP.
This work focuses on an underexplored but highly relevant scenario in healthcare IoT, namely a smartphone-centric system, where the patient’s smartphone
serves as the data aggregation point for multiple wearable sensors, and acts as
the NDN data producer for the patient’s health profile. While the literature
has demonstrated the potential of NDN for healthcare, few studies have explicitly tested NDN in small-scale, smartphone-centric topologies that simulate
real-world, patient-centric monitoring systems. To address this limitation, we
present a comprehensive comparison of NDN and IP in a smartphone-centric
healthcare scenario, evaluating their performance under identical experimental
conditions. Our study demonstrates that NDN can improve real-time data delivery by exploiting data-centric communication and in-network caching while
requiring only minimal modifications to existing infrastructure. Experimental
results further indicate that this architecture reduces communication latency
and better accommodates user mobility, highlighting the potential of NDN as a
practical networking solution for future smart healthcare systems.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews background
on NDN and summarizes related work on NDN-based healthcare and smart
environments. Section III presents the proposed system model, including node
roles, naming scheme, and traffic types. Section IV describes the simulation setup
and performance metrics. Section V discusses the numerical results comparing
NDN and IP. Finally, Section VI concludes the paper and outlines directions for
future work.

Related Work
Early IP-based BAN/WSN healthcare systems forward sensor data via gateways over TCP/IP (often 6LoWPAN), but the host-centric model offers limited
support for mobility, in-network caching, and data-centric security.[11, 2].
Information-centric networking, and in particular NDN, has been proposed to
better match the data-oriented nature of IoT and smart environments. Amadeo
et al. review ICN for the Internet of Things and argue that name-based access,
in-network caching, and data-centric security can address many of the scalability
and mobility issues that arise under IP [1]. Zhang et al. present the NDN architecture, in which consumers issue Interests for named content and Data packets
can be served from any node that holds a copy, whether the original producer
or an intermediate cache [14]. Recent surveys extend this perspective to smart
environments and NDN-IoT deployments. Islam et al. show that NDN’s hierarchical naming, stateful forwarding, and built-in caching align well with the needs
of smart cities, buildings, and health spaces, but also highlight practical barriers

such as interoperability with IP and limited testbeds [8]. Shah et al. focus on
hierarchical and hybrid naming schemes for NDN-based IoT, including e-health
scenarios, and discuss how naming choices interact with access control, content
protection, and the resource constraints of IoT devices [12]. Azamuddin et al.
provide a broad overview of NDN architecture, applications, and open research
problems, further motivating its use in emerging IoT and edge-computing settings [3]. On the caching side, Jafari et al. propose ECLAT-LRU, a predictive
caching policy that improves NDN cache performance [10].
On top of this architectural groundwork, several NDN-based frameworks for
healthcare and related IoT domains have been proposed. Wang and Cai introduce SHNIE, a secure healthcare monitoring framework that integrates clustered
IoT devices with an NDN-based edge cloud [13]. SHNIE uses hash-based ciphertext names, encryption, and signatures to protect medical data and exploits
request aggregation and in-network caching to reduce communication latency
and message cost compared with an IP-based smart hospital system. Dudeja et
al. design an NDN-based data communication framework for IoT–edge ecosystems in which edge nodes act as cluster heads and assign priorities to periodic sensor data; these priorities are then mapped to different caching strategies
(e.g., Leave-Copy-Everywhere versus Leave-Copy-Down) in the NDN core [6].
In a follow-up work, they propose a secure and pervasive NDN communication framework for connected healthcare that supports both consumer-initiated
and producer-initiated data dissemination and evaluate its performance under
various network conditions [7]. Demir and Manap implement a Named Data
Networking of Things (NDNoT) stack on the Contiki-NG operating system and
demonstrate the feasibility of NDN-based medical data retrieval on constrained
IoT devices using Cooja simulations [5]. Islam et al. outline an NDN-aided edge–
cloud architecture for continuous smart healthcare, where NDN is used at the
edge and an NDN edge gateway bridges local NDN domains to IP-based cloud
services such as AWS IoT [9].
These efforts collectively illustrate the potential benefits of NDN in healthcare and IoT-edge settings, including improved content delivery efficiency, flexible data dissemination patterns, and stronger data-centric security. However,
most existing works either focus on security mechanisms and naming schemes,
on architectural integration with edge and cloud platforms, or on NDN-only
performance tuning. SHNIE does include an analytical and simulation-based
comparison with an IP smart-hospital baseline, but its design centers on a relatively complex edge-cloud security framework rather than on a minimal end-toend communication model [13]. Other proposals evaluate NDN-based healthcare
or IoT frameworks in isolation, without constructing a matching IP reference
system over the same topology and traffic patterns [6, 7, 5, 9]. Despite recent
advances, controlled side-by-side comparisons between NDN and IP under identical experimental conditions remain limited, particularly in smartphone-centric
medical IoT scenarios where the patient’s smartphone serves as both the edge
device and the primary data producer. In such environments, remote caregivers
retrieve up-to-date patient information directly by name, making it possible to

3
System Model and Proposed Architecture
We study the use of NDN in a smart healthcare system where patients are
equipped with wearable IoT sensors. Our goal is to evaluate whether NDN can
provide communication performance comparable to, or better than, a traditional
IP-based solution when delivering medical data from patients to remote caregivers. In addition to end-to-end performance, we are interested in whether
NDN’s data-centric mechanisms enable efficient reuse of previously retrieved
health information across multiple authorized stakeholders, effectively leveraging the network as a shared substrate for data access.
3.1
Overall Scenario and Node Types
We consider a hospital or clinic scenario in which each patient is continuously
monitored by a set of body-worn sensors. These sensors periodically generate
physiological measurements such as heart rate, SpO2, and blood pressure. A
patient’s smartphone acts as a local gateway and edge device that collects sensor
data and makes it available to remote caregivers.
In our evaluation, we model six patients, each with one smartphone. The
smartphones and caregivers are interconnected by a fixed multi-hop network.
On the caregiver side, we consider six monitoring consumers representing clinics,
physicians, or hospital monitoring stations. This multi-consumer setting naturally captures repeated access to the same patient state from different monitoring points, which is precisely where in-network caching can provide network-wide
reuse by serving subsequent requests from nearby nodes. Between smartphones
and consumers, the network includes nine routers (e.g., access routers and intermediate/core routers), resulting in a 21-node topology. The same topology, node
roles, and traffic patterns are used in both NDN and IP scenarios (Section IV
will detail link capacities, propagation delays, and traffic parameters).
The medical traffic of interest consists primarily of periodic monitoring flows
(vital signs) and time-critical updates (e.g., alarms or abrupt changes). We focus
on end-to-end delay and packet loss as the main performance metrics; all security
mechanisms (encryption, signatures) are abstracted away and not modeled in the
simulations.
3.2
Baseline NDN Design: Direct Sensor-to-Consumer
Communication
As an initial baseline, we consider a straightforward NDN design in which each
body-worn sensor acts as an NDN data producer. Every new measurement generated by the sensor (e.g., once per second, once per minute, or at any other

6
M. R. Shakournia et al.
configured sampling interval) is published as a distinct NDN Data packet with
a unique name that encodes the generation time. In this model, clinics, doctors,
or hospital servers act as NDN consumers and retrieve samples directly from
the sensors by issuing Interests for these time-stamped names over a multi-hop
topology, similar to IP-based body area network designs where sensors forward
data to a sink or gateway [11, 2]. For such time-critical flows, where each sample
is unique and must be delivered as soon as it is produced, in-network caching and
request aggregation offer little benefit, because each measurement is requested
exactly once and is not reused by other consumers. As a result, this baseline
intentionally represents a setting where the network cannot exploit reuse across
stakeholders, since there is no shared, repeatedly requested content to retain in
the forwarding plane.
To better isolate this effect, we also conduct a micro-benchmark in a minimal topology consisting of a single producer (modeling a body-worn sensor), one
intermediate router, and a single consumer. In this setup, the producer generates 1000 distinct packets, each carrying a unique name (or, in the IP case, a
unique sequence identifier), and we transmit the same 1000 packets over both
an IP and an NDN stack under identical link settings, measuring the end-to-end
request–response delay. Since all packets are strictly non-reusable, this experiment intentionally removes the main performance advantages of NDN, namely
in-network caching and request aggregation. Intermediate NDN routers still perform per-packet lookups in CS and PIT, in addition to FIB lookups, whereas
the IP baseline only maintains a routing table and forwards packets without
per-request state. In our ns-3/ndnSIM implementation, the additional CS/PIT
processing overhead is modeled at a logical level and therefore manifests as only
a small difference in simulated delay; however, in a real implementation with
non-negligible lookup and memory costs, this overhead can become more pronounced and may make NDN slower than a stateless IP forwarder under purely
time-critical, non-cacheable traffic. The numerical results of this sanity-check
experiment, summarized in Table 1, confirm that under these conditions NDN
does not provide a delay advantage over IP.

aggregates the incoming sensor readings into a personal health profile that is
updated whenever new data arrive. Instead of publishing every raw sample as a
separate NDN object, the smartphone exposes a single named NDN Data object
that always represents the latest state of the patient’s vital signs. For a patient
with identifier patientID, the current profile may be published under a stable

The smartphone therefore acts as the sole NDN producer for this profile, while

Because all monitoring systems use the same content name to retrieve the
patient’s latest profile, intermediate NDN routers and edge nodes can cache the
Data packet and satisfy subsequent Interests from nearby caches. This is particularly relevant for smart-healthcare monitoring, where multiple monitoring points
(e.g., ward dashboards, nurse stations, physician terminals) repeatedly request
the same patient’s current status. In this way, the distributed caches collectively
act as a shared pool of recently retrieved patient profiles, enabling consumers
to obtain the closest available copy without needing to target a particular host.
In our evaluation (Section IV), we consider several such monitoring consumers
that periodically retrieve the profile of each patient; once a profile is fetched the
first time, subsequent requests can be served from caches, and NDN’s in-network
caching begins to provide a clear benefit.
3.4
Consistency and Freshness Semantics
A key requirement in this architecture is that caregivers must receive up-todate information. NDN provides built-in mechanisms to express freshness requirements. Each Data packet includes a FreshnessPeriod indicating how long
cached copies should be considered valid, and consumers may set the MustBeFresh
selector in their Interests to request only fresh Data.
In our design, we align the FreshnessPeriod of the health-profile Data with
the sensor update period. As a result, cached copies of the profile automatically
expire in routers roughly when new data are produced, and Interests marked as
MustBeFresh are forwarded toward the smartphone if cached content has become
stale. This ensures that doctors and monitoring systems obtain a consistent
and up-to-date view of the patient’s vital signs while still benefiting from innetwork caching between updates. Put differently, freshness semantics help keep
the shared cached pool reliable by preventing stale profiles from being served
beyond their validity window.
A high-level representation of the proposed architecture is shown in Fig. 2.
Wearable sensors attached to each patient send data to the patient’s smartphone,
which acts as an edge device and NDN producer. A set of intermediate routers
connects the smartphones to multiple monitoring consumers (clinics, doctors,
and hospital servers). NDN runs from the edge devices to the consumers, using
hierarchical names and in-network caching, while the specific low-power protocol
between sensors and the smartphone is abstracted away in the system model.
3.5
IP Reference Architecture
For a fair comparison, we also construct an IP reference architecture that uses the
same physical topology, node roles, and traffic patterns as the NDN architecture.

In the IP baseline, each patient’s smartphone still collects sensor data via BLE
and maintains an internal health profile, but communication with caregivers
follows the conventional host-centric model.
Specifically, the smartphone transmits monitoring data to caregiver nodes
using UDP over IP. Intermediate routers maintain IP routing tables and forward packets based on destination IP addresses. There is no in-network content
caching in the network: routers do not store application-layer data, and each
monitoring request must be served by the smartphone or a backend server every
time. Consequently, the network does not accumulate reusable content state on
the path, and repeated monitoring requests cannot benefit from previously retrieved data at intermediate nodes. No NDN-specific state (such as PIT or CS) is
present. This IP baseline allows us to quantify the impact of NDN’s name-based
forwarding and in-network caching on end-to-end delay and packet loss under
identical network and traffic conditions.
4
Simulation Setup and Metrics
We compare the proposed smartphone-centric NDN architecture with an IP
baseline in ns-3, using the standard Internet stack for IP and ndnSIM for NDN.
Both scenarios share the same physical topology, node roles, and traffic patterns;
only the network layer (IP vs. NDN with name-based forwarding and caching)
differs.
4.1
Topology and Traffic Overview
We use the 21-node topology from Section III: six monitoring consumers, six
patient-side producer/server nodes (smartphones), and nine routers connected
through a constrained core. All consumer–producer traffic traverses the same
core bottleneck, enabling controlled congestion and loss experiments.
Each consumer is associated with one patient. In IP, consumers send UDP
traffic to the corresponding patient-side server; in NDN, they issue Interests to
the corresponding producer. Applications run over the same time interval in both
cases, and packet/payload sizes are matched for fairness. Table 2 summarizes the
main simulation parameters.
Unless otherwise stated, NDN uses ndnSIM’s MulticastStrategy; we also
tested BestRoute. In this static, tree-like topology, both yield nearly identical
RTT and delivery ratio, indicating that the observed gains mainly come from
in-network caching rather than path diversity.

5.2
Short-Term Cache Warm-Up Behavior
Figure 3 shows a 10-packet rolling mean RTT over the first 2500 packets. In both
loss and no-loss runs, NDN exhibits a short initial RTT spike corresponding to
cache warm-up, when early Interests traverse the full multi-hop path to the
smartphone.
After a few requests, the profile Data are cached near the monitoring stations,
and subsequent Interests are satisfied locally, causing NDN RTT to drop quickly
to a low, nearly constant value. IP shows no such warm-up effect: RTT stays
roughly constant without loss, but grows and oscillates under congestion. These
results confirm that NDN gains here are driven by caching profiles close to
consumers.
5.3
Delay Under Packet Loss
To induce congestion and loss, core links are set to 200 kbps, making the core
the dominant bottleneck and causing frequent queue drops. As shown in the
upper plots of Figs. 3 and 4, IP exhibits unstable RTT with pronounced oscillations and values often exceeding 103 ms, due to repeated queue overflows and
retransmissions in the request–response exchange.

NDN remains consistently low, with chunk-averaged RTT on the order of
102 ms under the same conditions. Because requests target the same named
profile, fetched Data are cached on the consumer side of the bottleneck, so subsequent Interests are satisfied locally without crossing the congested core. As a
result, the rolling-mean curve flattens after warm-up and Fig. 4 shows a narrow
RTT band throughout the one-million-packet run.
5.4
Delay Without Packet Loss
Without loss, both architectures are stable but differ in absolute RTT. In the
lower plots of Figs. 3 and 4, IP remains near 10–12 ms, reflecting fixed queuing
and propagation along the multi-hop path.
NDN converges to about 5–6 ms after a brief cache warm-up. Once the profile
is cached near consumers, Interests are satisfied over shorter effective paths and
repeated upstream traversal through the core is avoided, yielding persistently
lower RTT over the one-million-packet run.
5.5
Delivery Ratio
Table 3 reports end-to-end delivery. In the no-loss scenario, both IP and NDN
achieve near-perfect delivery. Under packet loss on the 200 kbps core, IP delivery
drops sharply to about 3%, whereas NDN remains close to 99%, because caching
reduces the amount of traffic that must traverse the lossy core. This shows that
NDN caching improves both delay and robustness under congestion.
Overall, NDN is not inherently faster than IP for per-packet, time-critical
sensor traffic because unique names prevent cache reuse and PIT/CS processing
adds overhead. In contrast, the smartphone-centric, profile-based model maps
repeated monitoring to a stable name, enabling routers near monitoring stations
to serve requests from cache. This yields lower and more stable RTT and much
higher delivery ratios than IP in lossy or resource-limited settings, consistent
with continuous remote monitoring where caregivers primarily require an up-todate patient profile rather than every raw sample.
