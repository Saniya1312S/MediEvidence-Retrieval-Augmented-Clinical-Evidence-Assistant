Abstract 
AI-enabled remote patient monitoring is evolving beyond passive data collection toward agentic 
AI systems that interpret patient states, coordinate care, and initiate action across domestic and 
clinical settings. This structured scoping review finds that mature agentic care has not yet emerged: 
Most systems remain human led, while stronger decision–action coupling and embodied AI remain 
largely confined to prototypes and pilots. Healthcare robotics makes the transition from monitoring 
to action especially visible by translating AI-generated recommendations and decisions into 
speech, movement, prompting, physical assistance, escalation, and care coordination, making 
embodiment, clinical integration, human oversight, and governance fundamental design 
requirements. Further progress requires safe and interoperable care architectures in which 
intelligent components operate through clinically validated and accountable workflows. We 
propose agentic robotics as a future direction for supervised systems embedded in the care 
ecosystem, connecting AI agents and robots with clinicians, caregivers, and patients through 
auditable workflows that can be interrupted and remain subject to human oversight and explicit AI 
governance.

1. INTRODUCTION 
Global aging and the expansion of care beyond institutional settings are creating sustained demand 
for technologies that can support independent living while reducing the burden on clinicians, 
caregivers, and families (1). Over the last few decades, remote care technologies have 
progressively evolved from domotics (referring to the use of automated and networked 
technologies to monitor and control functions within the home) and early smart environments 
(which focused mainly on environmental control, safety, and remote monitoring) toward more 
integrated ecosystems built on the Internet of Things (IoT), wearable sensors, ambient intelligence, 
and AI (2). These systems have expanded the possibilities of home-based care by enabling 
continuous sensing, context awareness, data-driven prediction, and more personalized forms of 
assistance. However, monitoring alone is often insufficient, since in a caring context, sensing must 
be also translated into safe, meaningful, and timely support within complex domestic 
environments. 
Robotic technologies represent a further step in this evolution. By providing AI-based services 
with physical and social embodiment, robots can make digital intelligence concrete and actionable 
in everyday life (3). In domestic environment, they can support daily routines through navigation 
and object manipulation. Their verbal and nonverbal communication capabilities also allow them 
to provide companionship and mediate care interactions among users, caregivers, and clinicians. 
Together, these functions support a broad range of use cases and scenarios, from physical 
assistance with feeding, dressing, mobility and household activities to cognitive and social support, 
health monitoring, medication management and emergency detection (4). Beyond the diversity of 
applications, robotics for home-based care poses distinctive control challenges. Autonomous 
systems must adapt their behavior to changes in users’ needs and conditions under partial 
observability, while coordinating with multiple care actors and operating within strict safety and 
ethical constraints. These demands make remote care a particularly rich testbed for integrating 
perception and planning with safe control and effective human–robot interaction. 
In particular, personalized care robots designed for older adults and other vulnerable 
populations, including people living with frailty, cognitive or physical impairments, or 
neurodegenerative conditions, illustrate how assistive technologies can operate in home and 
community settings, and adapt to individual needs, cognitive profiles, preferences, and changing 
conditions while remaining acceptable in real-world domestic use (5). In this context, autonomous

systems must implement a full perception–inference–decision–action loop under strict safety and 
reliability requirements. Remote care is one of the most demanding instantiations of this problem 
because it addresses adverse events such as falls, nonadherence medication, dementia and 
cognitive decline, chronic disease progression, mental health crisis, and social isolation. These 
situations unfold in homes where sensory and contextual information is often incomplete and 
responsibility is distributed across patients, caregivers, clinicians, and devices. 
With the maturation of IoT and AI, the focus progressively shifted toward more complex care 
architectures that integrate data through interoperable and standardized systems, enabling context 
awareness and decision support. More recently, advances in robotic technologies have introduced 
the Internet of Robotic Things (IoRT) paradigm (6), which connects robots with distributed sensing 
and AI-enabled infrastructures allowing monitoring to lead to situated interaction and action (7). 
This evolution is further reinforced by emerging agentic AI approaches, which increase the 
potential for autonomous planning, adaptation, and coordination across home care services (8). 
From this perspective, robotics becomes a key enabling technology when AI-enabled remote 
healthcare must move beyond digital inference to act directly in the physical environment. Robots 
provide AI with an embodied presence, enabling sensing and reasoning to guide situated acting in 
domestic environments. This embodiment creates technical, architectural and governance 
challenges for remote setting, since safe and meaningful deployment depends on interoperable and 
accountable systems that are clinically relevant, and acceptable to users, caregivers, and healthcare 
organizations. This article therefore examines how robotic embodiment is incorporated into AIenabled remote patient monitoring (RPM), ambient assisted living (AAL) and remote or 
distributed care architectures, with particular attention to how architecture and governance shape 
the transition from monitoring to real-world care action. 
Robotic prototypes were included in the core corpus only when embedded in, or explicitly 
specified as part of, an architecture connecting sensing and inference with monitoring, decision 
support, actuation, escalation, or governance. Accordingly, the observed prevalence of 
embodiment refers in particular to architecturally integrated robotics within AI-enabled 
RPM/AAL corpus and should not be generalized to healthcare robotics as a whole. Complete core 
and background corpus registries, including the embodied robotic subset, are provided in 
Supplemental Tables 19–21. 
Addressing the growing interest in agentic AI for healthcare , this structured scoping review, 
treating agency as a system-level property of the care architecture, maps how sensing, inference,

examines whether embodiment is associated with stronger decision–action coupling and identifies 
barriers to sustained deployment related to system maturity, governance, and ecosystem 
integration. The review provides an evidence map of concrete system architectures, while clinical 
effectiveness lies out of its scope. 
2. METHODOLOGY 
2.1. Review Aims and Evidence-Mapping Design 
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping 
Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (9) was used as transparency guidance for reporting search, source 
selection, and evidence charting, while the corpus itself was used to support conceptual synthesis 
rather than statistical meta-analysis. We therefore prioritized papers that described implemented 
or clearly specified systems over papers that made only abstract claims about autonomy, robotics, 
agentic AI, or digital health. The review uses the broader RPM/AAL corpus as a baseline and 
robotic embodiment as an analytical lens for identifying when monitoring begins to become 
situated care action. The review was organized around three evidence-mapping questions: 
◼ 
EQ1: How are AI-enabled RPM, AAL, and robotic care systems architecturally configured, 
and where do they move beyond monitoring toward care action? 
◼ 
EQ2: How are embodied robotic systems positioned across decision–action coupling and 
maturity levels compared with nonembodied RPM systems? 
◼ 
EQ3: Which architectural patterns characterize action-capable systems with real-user 
validation, and what systematic gaps prevent wider real-world deployment? 
These questions were operationalized in the search strategy through four query blocks, 
summarized in Table 1.

2.2. Coding Framework and Terminology 
Included records were charted using a prespecified coding framework designed to align data 
extraction with the three evidence-mapping questions. The framework combined the SIDAG 
architectural layers with two study-specific scales developed a priori for this review. Decision–
action coupling was coded at four levels (numbered A0–A3) and validation maturity was coded at 
four levels (numbered M0–M3). These dimensions were defined before aggregate synthesis and 
were applied during independent full-text screening and preliminary extraction; disagreements or

--- PAGE 7 [pdf_text] ---
7
uncertain classifications were subsequently merged and resolved during consensus under senior 
reviewer supervision. 
SIDAG formalizes and organizes functional elements recurrent across intelligent agent 
architectures, agentic healthcare AI, robotic sense–think–act models, RPM systems, shared 
autonomy, and healthcare AI governance into a healthcare-specific evidence-charting framework 
(10–13). It traces how systems acquire information about the patient and care context, interpret its 
clinical or operational relevance, select a response, and execute or trigger interventions. In highrisk healthcare and assistive robotics, this sequence requires governance mechanisms that 
constrain how data are handled, decisions are made, and care-relevant actions are performed (14). 
Data protection mechanisms alone were not considered evidence that system decisions or actions 
were governed. This perspective shifts the question from whether a system contains an AI agent 
to whether it participates in a supervised sensing-to-action pathway and, where implemented, 
closes the loop through outcome monitoring and feedback. For healthcare deployment, agency 
becomes meaningful only when sensing, inference, decision, action, outcome monitoring, and 
governance are linked in a way that makes system behavior traceable, interruptible, and 
accountable. Governance domains are reported in Supplemental Table 15, and paper-level 
governance coding is provided in Supplemental Table 19. 
SIDAG therefore allowed us to map whether each system remained monitoring oriented or 
progressed toward an action-capable care architecture that linked the five SIDAG dimensions 
(sensing, inference, decision, actuation, and governance). These dimensions and their operational 
definitions are summarized in the sidebar titled The SIDAG Evidence-Charting Framework. 
[SIDEBAR] - THE SIDAG EVIDENCE-CHARTING FRAMEWORK 
SIDAG was used to code where each system stopped along the sensing-to-action chain: 
• 
Sensing: acquisition of patient, user, robot, or environmental state 
• 
Inference: analysis, prediction, classification, or interpretation 
• 
Decision: recommendation, prioritization, planning, or authority allocation 
• 
Actuation: alerting, escalation, device action, robotic movement, prompting, or intervention 
• 
Governance: oversight, override, auditability, safety constraints, privacy, accountability, or regulatory alignment 
 
Detailed SIDAG coding questions and examples are provided in Supplemental Table 12 and 
Section 7.1 of the Supplemental Material.

Because the term “agency” is used inconsistently across AI, robotics, and healthcare literatures, 
we treated it as an operational property rather than as a broad philosophical or marketing term. 
Following the distinction between AI agents and agentic AI proposed by Sapkota et al. (8), we 
distinguish an AI agent, understood as a software or robotic actor capable of perceiving, reasoning, 
planning, using tools, or executing actions, from agentic AI, understood as a broader system-level 
capacity for goal-directed, multistep decision-making and action under constraints. Accordingly, 
“AI agents” refers to individual software or robotic actors; “agentic AI” refers to system-level, 
goal-directed decision and action; and “agentic robotics” refers to the embodied participation of 
robotic actors within supervised, distributed, and governed care architectures. In this review, 
however, agency was operationalized more narrowly as the demonstrated authority of a system to 
select, prepare, coordinate, trigger, or execute care-relevant action. This operational definition 
prevented systems from being classified as agentic merely because they used machine learning, 
conversational AI, or robotic embodiment. Agency had to be visible in the architecture through 
the coupling of decision and actuation and was assessed using the A0–A3 scale. 
The A0–A3 scale captured the highest demonstrated level of decision–action coupling in each 
system, ranging from passive sensing, prediction, or data relay to bounded, system-governed care 
action. The M0–M3 scale captured validation maturity, ranging from conceptual architecture to 
sustained real-world deployment. Operational definitions of both scales are provided in the sidebar 
titled Decision–Action Coupling and Validation Maturity. 
[SIDEBAR] - DECISION–ACTION COUPLING AND VALIDATION MATURITY 
The decision–action scale used in this review is as follows: 
• 
A0: Passive sensing, prediction, or data relay is used without decision support. 
• 
A1: The system analyzes, alerts, or recommends; humans retain care action authority. 
• 
A2: The system proposes, prepares, coordinates, or triggers an action that requires human authorization or supervision. 
• 
A3: The system executes care-relevant action without immediate human confirmation, within bounded system rules. 
The validation maturity scale used in this review is as follows: 
• 
M0: The system consists only of a conceptual architecture or design proposal. 
• 
M1: The system is a prototype or has been used in a laboratory test, simulation, or technical validation. 
• 
M2: The system has been part of a pilot study, real-user evaluation, or deployment in a naturalistic care setting. 
• 
M3: The system has seen sustained real-world deployment or routine clinical/service use. 
 
The A and M levels were paired a priori because action capability and deployment maturity 
are analytically distinct: A system may demonstrate high decision–action coupling in a laboratory

--- PAGE 9 [pdf_text] ---
9
while remaining far from deployable in real care settings. Associations between embodiment and 
higher decision–action coupling were explored using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test, comparing 
A0–A1 with A2–A3 systems. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were reported. 
Together, SIDAG, A levels, and M levels allowed us to apply the same analytical question 
across dashboards, AAL platforms, telecare systems, IoRT configurations, and embodied robots: 
Where does the system stop along the sensing-to-action chain, and what happens when inferred 
patient state is translated into care-relevant action? Robotic embodiment was therefore treated not 
as a topic separate from RPM but rather as a particularly demanding case of agentic remote care, 
because physical or social action requires architectural integration, human oversight, safety 
constraints, auditability, and accountability. 
Bibliographic and conceptual information was extracted from all 113 included records using a 
61-field extraction form covering bibliographic metadata, screening decisions, study 
characteristics, technology details, governance, limitations, gaps, and provenance. System-level 
SIDAG, A-level, M-level, embodiment, architecture, and governance coding was applied to the 
93 core system papers used for corpus-level synthesis. AI-assisted extraction was introduced only 
after human consensus had finalized eligibility decisions and preliminary classifications and was 
limited to the postconsensus enrichment of empty or incomplete extraction fields. Human-entered 
values were preserved as authoritative, and all AI-generated fields were flagged for human 
verification before analysis. 
3. EVIDENCE MAP: EMBODIMENT IN THE TRANSITION FROM MONITORING TO 
CARE ACTION 
3.1. The Remote Patient Monitoring Baseline: Monitoring Matures Before Agency 
The broader RPM/AAL corpus establishes the baseline against which the robotic signal becomes 
meaningful. Across 93 core systems, decision–action coupling was concentrated at A1. 67 papers 
(72.0%) described systems that analyze, alert, or recommend while humans retain authority over 
care action. Only 22 systems (23.7%) reached A2 or A3, where the system proposes, coordinates, 
prepares, triggers, or executes care-relevant action. Validation maturity shows a parallel 
concentration before deployment. 52 systems (55.9%) remained at prototype or laboratory level 
(M1), 30 (32.3%) reached pilot or real-user validation (M2), and only 4 (4.3%) reached sustained 
real-world deployment (M3).

descriptive and conceptual, not causal. Nevertheless, it is structurally important. One plausible

final decision and action channel. A2 and A3 systems must also meet the broader governance 
requirements associated with action-capable care and integrate them into clinical workflows. Once

3.2. Embodiment as a Low-Prevalence, High-Signal Marker of Agency 
Robotic embodiment is uncommon in the corpus, but it is disproportionately associated with 
agency. Physically embodied systems represent only 16 of the 93 papers (17.2%), reflecting the 
corpus’s broader emphasis on digital and sensor-based monitoring. Their analytical importance 
derives from their association with higher decision–action. As shown in Table 2, nonembodied 
systems are overwhelmingly concentrated at A1 (61/77, 79.2%), whereas embodied systems are 
more evenly distributed across A1–A3. More than half of embodied systems reach A2 or A3 (9/16, 
56.3%), compared with only 13 of the 77 nonembodied systems (16.9%). The relationship between 
embodiment and higher decision–action coupling was the clearest quantitative association 
observed in the corpus. Embodied systems were more frequently classified at A2 or A3 than 
nonembodied systems (Fisher’s exact odds ratio = 6.33, p = 0.002).

The observed association does not make physical embodiment a prerequisite for higher 
decision–action coupling, nor does embodiment alone make a robotic system capable of deciding 
and acting. The association shows that when inference, decision, and action acquire physical 
expression, the meaning and risks of agency change substantially. A nonembodied RPM system 
may misclassify patient state, trigger a false alert, or miss deterioration. An embodied system may 
move in a home, approach a person, mediate a care interaction, prompt medication or 
rehabilitation, support mobility, manipulate objects, or trigger a physical or social response. Errors

--- PAGE 12 [pdf_text] ---
12 
can therefore extend beyond informational harm and produce physical, relational, and clinical 
consequences. 
Embodiment agency therefore requires carefully orchestrated architectures that couple 
perception and reasoning with physical and social capabilities, because each function affects the 
operation and safety of the others. Consistent with robotics background literature, physical 
actuation must remain subject to safety-critical constraints throughout this integrated process to 
support situated intelligence and action (26). Wearable and soft robotic assistance further 
illustrates why embodied agency must adapt in real-time to patient-specific biomechanics while 
maintaining comfort and safe force transmission (27). For this reason, embodiment marks the point 
at which care action acquires materially and socially consequential. 
The heterogeneity of the robot subset is analytically useful, because it reveals distinct pathways 
from monitoring to embodied action. Social and dialogue-oriented robotic systems translate 
inference into interaction or prompting (19, 28, 29)., while telepresence systems connect 
monitoring with navigation and remote presence (20, 30, 31). In IoRT and multirobot 
configuration, coordination becomes part of the care action (11, 32, 33), whereas humanoid and 
rehabilitation-oriented systems combine monitoring, interaction, and embodied support (23, 34, 
35). Figure 3 provides a visual anchor for these pathways, spanning social robotic interfaces, 
assisted telepresence, and modular healthcare robotics platforms.

3.3. The Robotic Deployment Gap: From Stand-Alone Capability to Governed Care 
Architecture 
The same evidence that makes robots analytically important also exposes the deployment gap. No 
embodied system reaches M3 sustained deployment. The small number of M3 systems limits 
statistical inference, but this pattern remains central to the review’s argument. Embodied systems 
are associated with higher decision-action coupling without a corresponding increase in 
deployment maturity. Mature deployment is currently confined to conservative A1 tools, while 
action-capable systems remain prototypes or pilots. This ceiling concerns sustained deployment

--- PAGE 14 [pdf_text] ---
14 
within the care architectures captured by the review and does not preclude embodied social robots 
from reaching users through consumer or other nonregulated care pathways. 
ElliQ (36) provides an instructive boundary case. It has reached commercial and public sector 
deployment as a proactive companion robot for older adults, exhibiting social-assistive agency 
through self-initiated interaction and the personalization of reminders, wellness support, and 
communication based on users’ routines and preferences. ElliQ nevertheless remains positioned 
as a consumer device and it is not intended to detect, diagnose, treat, triage, or manage clinical 
deterioration. Even with this bounded action-space, deployment requires careful calibration and 
support. Users may need guidance about the robot’s role and technical assistance, and some may 
perceive proactive interactions as intrusive. The study also describes an optional weekly report 
that users may share with clinicians, while clinical interpretation and any resulting action remain 
external to the system (36). Social-assistive agency can therefore mature commercially when its 
action space focuses on companionship and wellness support and clinical authority stays external. 
Viewed against this boundary case, the deployment gap appears to concern the transition from 
local robotic capability to system-level readiness. Adjacent AAL and robotics-control studies 
identify complementary mechanisms for supporting this transition through distributed service 
coordination (37), simulation-based validation in inhabited environments (38), and formal 
verification of safety and coordination in multirobot settings (39). 
As shown in Table 3, only seven systems combine A ≥ 2 with M ≥ 2. The embodied subset 
comprises a socially assistive robot (19), a telepresence robot (20), and a humanoid/IoT platform 
(23). Across these systems, actions remain bounded and the care authority is shared through human 
supervision or explicit governance. Within the reviewed corpus, governed and ecosystemembedded action therefore represents the current empirical frontier of embodied agentic RPM.

The evidence map identifies the structural tension of the field: Deployment maturity 
accumulates around conservative monitoring systems, whereas embodied and agentic systems 
concentrate action capability but remain below sustained deployment. The next section examines 
how this tension emerged over time by tracing the evolution from cloud-based, rule-driven 
monitoring to edge–hybrid inference and more recent forms of agentic and embodied care systems 
facing unresolved governance requirements. 
4. FROM PREDICTIVE MONITORING TO EMBODIED ACTION 
RPM and AAL systems have not evolved along a single axis. The corpus shows neither a simple 
replacement of cloud dashboards by robots nor a linear progression from rules to machine learning 
and autonomy. The clearest temporal change concerns the location and distribution of 
computation. Hybrid platforms increased from 9 of 27 systems (33.3%) in 2018–2020 to 10 of 22 
(45.5%) in 2021–2023 and 22 of 44 (50.0%) in 2024–2026, while dedicated edge platforms 
appeared after 2020. Decision–action coupling did not follow the same trajectory: Systems

--- PAGE 16 [pdf_text] ---
16 
classified at A2 or A3 represented 7 of 27 (25.9%), 3 of 22 (13.6%), and 12 of 44 (27.3%) across 
the three intervals, respectively. The corpus therefore indicates changing architectural emphases 
rather than successive stages toward autonomy. 
Embodiment is distributed across all three intervals and is treated as the cross-cutting analytical 
dimension defined in Section 2 and examined empirically in Section 3. Its relevance here lies in 
how different architectural configurations accommodate embodied action and the associated 
requirements for supervision and governance.

4.1. Instrumented Visibility and Centralized Monitoring

--- PAGE 17 [pdf_text] ---

The first period, 2018–2020, is best understood as the era of instrumented visibility. The dominant 
technological problem was how to acquire the patient, user, and environmental state and make it 
available to clinicians or caregivers. Cloud and hybrid platforms were equally common (9/27 each, 
33.3%), while dedicated edge processing was absent. Across these configurations wearable, 
ambient, or device-level signals were typically routed to cloud services, dashboards, or local 
monitoring platforms for humans’ interpretation (40, 41). The corresponding AI profile was 
conservative. Rule-based and threshold mechanisms accounted for 17 of the 27 systems (63.0%), 
and 19 of the 27 systems (70.4%) were classified as A1. Their contribution lay primarily in 
improving detection and clinical visibility while care authority remained with humans. 
Wearable AI monitoring in assisted-living communities (16) illustrates this A1 pattern within 
the period. The same human-led configuration subsequently reached sustained deployment in 
oncology symptom monitoring (15), chronic heart failure telemonitoring (17), and electronic 
health record (EHR)–integrated home spirometry after lung transplant (18), indicating the 
persistence and maturity of this architectural model beyond the first interval. Embodiment also 
appeared early, although not as mature care infrastructure. Robot-related systems such as the 
elderly AAL robot sensor architecture (30) and wearable-triggered tele controlled assistance (31) 
already connected robotic action to sensing, servers, planning, or teleoperation. These systems 
revealed an architectural tension already present at the beginning of the review period: Physical 
action could be introduced before the surrounding care ecosystem was ready to govern it. The 
robot could move, mediate, or respond, while the broader RPM/AAL pathway remained prototype 
level, partially validated, or weakly connected to clinical workflows and accountability. 
The same distinction is important for interpreting locally bounded robotic systems. An assistive 
device may combine sensing, control, and actuation while remaining only weakly integrated with 
a broader monitored care pathway. The cane-type fall prevention robot (42) provides a later 
example of this boundary: It has clinically meaningful physical embodiment, including motion 
sensing, vibrotactile feedback, lighting response, and onboard actuation, but its contribution 
remains local and technical. Such systems should show what embodied capability looks like before 
ecosystem integration and why embodiment alone is not equivalent to agentic care.

4.2. Edge–Hybrid Inference and Local Processing 
Between 2021 and 2023, computation increasingly moved closer to the point of care. Hybrid edge–
fog–cloud architectures rose to 10 of 22 systems (45.5%), and dedicated edge platforms appeared 
(3/22, 13.6%). Papers increasingly addressed the limitations of centralized cloud monitoring for 
timely, bandwidth-efficient, and privacy-sensitive processing. Inference consequently moved 
toward the home, gateway, device, or robot, as illustrated by edge–cloud clinical prediction and 
autonomous telepresence systems developed during this period (20, 43). 
The AI profile changed accordingly. Machine learning prediction rose to 8 of 22 systems 
(36.4%), becoming more prominent than in the first period. This shift is important but should not 
be overread. Predictive machine learning increased inferential sophistication and the systems could 
model deterioration, classify activity, identify anomalies, or detect patient-specific risk more 
flexibly than threshold logic alone. Yet the A-level distribution remains conservative: 19 of 22 
systems (86.4%) were classified as A1, while only 3 of 22 (13.6%) reached A2 or A3. Systems 
increasingly incorporated predictive methods without a corresponding increase in the authority 
delegated to them; most outputs remained directed to humans for interpretation and response. 
Better models did not automatically close the care loop. A patient-criticality-aware edge–cloud 
architecture could perform clinical prediction closer to the data source while still routing its output 
to human decision-makers (43). Similarly, EHR-integrated home spirometry combined automated 
prompting and data collection with threshold-based alerts displayed on clinical dashboards, 
leaving care authority with clinicians (18). A predictive RPM platform may therefore be 
technically advanced but architecturally conservative if its outputs stop at a dashboard or alert. 
Conversely, a simpler rule-based or ontology-driven system may be more agentic if it coordinates 
an action pathway under defined supervision, as illustrated by the bounded domestic actuation 
implemented in DOMUS (domestic ontology managed ubiquitous system) (25). 
The question for this review is therefore where AI output goes and what authority it carries. 
Does it remain informational, or does it become coupled to decision, actuation, escalation, or 
embodied support? 
The telepresence robot (20) is the hinge case for this by translating local computation into 
supervised embodied action. The user or operator defines the goal, and the robot executes bounded 
movement while retaining fallback control pathways. Its validation in a real home demonstrates a 
shared autonomy configuration with an explicit allocation of human and robotic authority. 
Adaptivity-as-a-service (37) extends this principle by modelling assistance through services

--- PAGE 19 [pdf_text] ---
19 
coordinated across a distributed AAL architecture. Formal and stochastic modeling of multirobot 
service settings (39) similarly treats human behavior and coordination as part of the system. 
Together, these studies locate intelligence at the level of the distributed care loop, where local 
computation and robotic action remain coordinated with human supervision and fallback. 
4.3. Consolidation of Hybrid Ecosystems and Heterogeneous Decision Logic 
During the third period, 2024–2026, hybrid configurations accounted for 22 of 44 systems 
(50.0%), while dedicated edge platforms accounted for 5 of 44 (11.4%). Decision logic remained 
heterogenous. Rules and thresholds were used in 24 of 44 systems, (54.5%), machine learning and 
adaptive methods in 13 of 44 systems (29.5%), and smaller groups incorporated clinical decision 
support systems (CDSS) or autonomous and multiagent logic. This coexistence indicates 
continued methodological plurality across the field. Systems combine rule-based and statistical 
methods with clinical decision support, planning, or coordination according to the function of their 
outputs and where action is expected to occur. 
Recent systems illustrate this plural AI ecology: TeleXAI-R combines transformer-based risk 
stratification with explainability and confidence-aware alerting (44), while edge–AI enables local 
anomaly detection with clinician-facing dashboard alerts (45); fog–edge Internet of Medical 
Things (IoMT) architectures optimize latency and threat resilience (46). Other systems connect 
decision logic more directly to clinical workflows. Chronic heart failure platforms integrate CDSS, 
Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) workflows, alerts, and care plans (47); 
conversational AI conducts heart failure follow-up while nurses retain treatment authority (48); 
and local large language model (LLM) and multiagent elder care systems add voice, emotion 
recognition, reminders, and caregiver alerts without assuming care authority (49). 
The observed diversity can be interpretated in light of the different computational and control 
requirements of risk estimation. A monitoring system can tolerate a probabilistic prediction if a 
human interprets the result. An embodied or semiautonomous system must also decide when to 
interrupt, prompt, move, escalate, hand over, or remain silent. Coupling AI outputs to care-relevant 
action shifts the design problem from prediction to authority allocation and control under 
uncertainty, making decision logic inseparable from the architecture question. 
The robotic system from this period should be read as architectural roles associated with 
specific care functions. The socially assistive robot used to deliver FHIR-integrated clinical 
decision support during hospital rounds (19) serves as a clinical interface connecting patient-

--- PAGE 20 [pdf_text] ---
20 
specific information with clinician workflow. The Smart Care diabetes platform (23) uses a 
humanoid robot as a home feedback node that collects biometric and self-reported information, 
links it to a disease-management hub, and returns personalized feedback to the patient, supporting 
day-to-day diabetes self-management . The human–robot–robot assistance system (32) extends 
this configuration toward IoRT orchestration. In this system, bed and privacy-preserving thermal 
sensing detect the patient’s intention to exit the bed, while a mobile robot interacts with the patient 
and manipulator-based assistance enables object handover, aiming to reduce unsafe bed-exit 
events and support activities of daily living. Together, these systems address the shared 
architectural and clinical challenge of moving intelligence beyond the dashboard and into the care 
environment while maintaining human supervision. 
Agentic AI becomes relevant when heterogeneous decision logic is coupled with care-relevant 
action. Open-ended autonomous agents have not reached broad deployment within the corpus, and 
the observed forms of agency remain bounded to informational support, care coordination, and 
supervised embodied action As established in Sections 2 and 3 and illustrated in Section 4.2, the 
degree of agency depends on where system outputs are directed and what authority they carry. 
Embodiment makes this coupling particularly consequential because digital outputs acquire 
physical or social expression within the patient’s environment. 
Earlier and adjacent robotics research identifies additional capabilities relevant to this 
transition. Digital-twin validation of Pepper in a simulated smart-home environment (38) shows 
how integrated robotic subsystems can be evaluated before physical deployment, while accounting 
for computational, environmental and interactional failures. Advanced soft robotics work (50) 
highlights the need for adaptive actuation and human–robot interaction mechanisms when 
assistance has physical consequences. Artificial theory-of-mind trajectory prediction (51) shows 
that implicit human intent must be inferred when people and robots share space without explicit 
verbal commands, while robotic walking assistance illustrates real-time adaptation to kinematic 
and physiological constraints (52). The Healthcare Robotics’ Ontology (HERON) (35) provides 
the semantic layer needed for communication, collaboration, and safety across healthcare robotics. 
Together, these examples characterize embodied care intelligence as multimodal, anticipatory, 
interactive, and safety constrained.

4.4. Clinical Use Cases: From Remote Monitoring to Situated Support 
The clinical implications of these architectural shifts become clearer in home care use cases. 
Conventional RPM, primarily supports earlier recognition of heart failure instability, chronic 
disease instability, abnormal activity, and other signs of clinical deterioration (15, 17, 18, 53). In 
embodied RPM/AAL, the expected benefit shifts from earlier detection to situated support. The 
system is no longer only asking whether something is wrong; it begins to support what should 
happen next. Figure 5 provides a visual overview of this shift, showing how embodied RPM/AAL 
extends clinical visibility toward situated support across key home care use cases.

Fall prevention and mobility support illustrate this shift clearly. Wearable and ambient system 
can detect instability, inactivity, or a fall and notify caregiver (54). Embodied system can also 
provide local assistance. A cane-type robot can provide vibrotactile feedback, lighting support, or 
motion stabilization (42). Adaptive assistance system can modulate robotic support in response to 
fatigue, instability, or movement state estimates (29), while walking-assistance work in the 
robotics background literature shows that safe physical cooperation requires real-time adaptation

to the user’s gait and kinematic limits (52). These capabilities support the clinical objectives of 
reducing fall risk, preserving mobility, and sustaining activities of daily living. 
Medication adherence and self-management provide another pathway toward situated support. 
Nonembodied RPM platforms can remind, alert, or display adherence data. Social and humanoid 
robots make this loop more interactive by asking questions, collecting self-reported symptoms or 
behaviors, and returning feedback in a familiar home context. In the Smart Care diabetes IoT 
platform (23), the robot serves as the patient-facing interface connecting biometric measurement 
and self-reported behaviors with a disease management hub and personalized feedback. Similarly, 
medication-reminder dialogue systems (28) show how social embodiment can support adherence 
routines. Integration with clinical oversight and escalation pathways determines how these systems 
participate in the broader care architecture. 
Telepresence and social assistance address isolation, caregiver burden, and access to remote 
support alongside clinical monitoring. The telepresence robot described by Laniel et al. (20) 
combines remote presence with bounded autonomous navigation under human command. Its 
mobile interface allows caregivers or clinicians to interact with and observe the home environment, 
supporting remote check-ins and situational awareness. This configuration depends on clearly 
defined privacy and control boundaries, including fallback control and human authority. 
Domestic assistance and emergency coordination are the most action-oriented use cases. AAL 
robot sensor architectures (30) and IoRT systems (32) connect environmental sensing, intent 
recognition, dialogue, mobile robot action, and sometimes manipulation. They can detect unsafe 
attempts to leave the bed and ask whether help is needed. Robotic components may then provide 
object assistance or notify staff or relatives. These examples show how embodiment changes the 
risk profile, intervening in a vulnerable moment in the patient’s environment. 
Finally, adaptive and cognitive support illustrates how clinical value also depends on 
personalization and anticipation. Adaptive robotic dialogue systems can tailor medication 
reminder interactions to user responses and changing conversational conditions (28), while 
multimodal AI and augmented reality systems can provide personalized remote cognitive 
assistance for aging-in-place (21). Artificial theory-of-mind trajectory prediction illustrates how 
safe interaction may also require anticipating what a person is about to do when humans and robots 
share space (51). Together, these approaches aim to preserve autonomy through assistance that 
responds to the user’s behavior and context.

5. GOVERNANCE READINESS: FROM CARE ACTION TO ACCOUNTABLE 
AUTONOMY 
The clinical use cases discussed above create a natural transition from architecture to governance. 
Once RPM/AAL systems move from monitoring to situated support, the key question is whether 
care-relevant action is authorized, supervised, explainable, interruptible, and accountable. A 
system that displays an alert can leave responsibility with clinicians or caregivers. When system 
prompts medication, actuates a domestic device, escalates a crisis, or coordinates robotic 
assistance, it redistributes authority within the care loop. Across the 93 core systems, 24 papers 
(25.8%) either had no identifiable governance mechanism or provided insufficient information for 
governance coding. The absence or nondetermination of reported governance mechanisms should 
be interpreted as a governance readiness and reporting gap, not as proof that safeguards were 
absent in the prototypes. Nondocumentation nevertheless consequential because deployment 
readiness cannot be assessed when the relevant SIDAG safeguards are unspecified. 
A1 systems, although technically conservative, have the largest governance gap. 20 of 67 
systems (29.9%) had no identifiable governance mechanism or insufficient governance 
information. A2 systems showed the lowest gap, with only 1 of 14 (7.1%) lacking governance 
documentation. This lower proportion may reflect the need for systems coordinating executable 
actions to make authorization workflows visible. 2 of 8 A3 systems (25.0%) had no identifiable or 
determinable governance mechanisms despite system-governed action, although the small 
denominator limits interpretation. Governance therefore becomes relevant whenever AI output 
influences care decisions, escalation, workload, or patient behavior. Figure 6 summarizes how the 
identified governance features are distributed across autonomy levels and between embodied and 
nonembodied systems.

The European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act gives this argument regulatory urgency and 
provides a readiness lens for the analysis, while formal legal classification remains outside the 
scope of the review. Depending on the intended use, product status, and deployment context, AI 
systems affecting health, safety, vulnerable users, or regulated products may fall under high-risk 
obligations. Relevant requirements correspond closely to the gaps observed here and include risk 
management, technical documentation, logging, information to deployers, human oversight, 
robustness, cybersecurity, and accuracy (57). The review does not assess legal compliance. Its 
findings show that many papers do not yet report the evidence needed to assess compliance 
readiness. 
The governance implications become especially visible in embodied RPM/AAL because 
physical and social actions can turn weakly documented authority into a direct safety issue: Who 
can stop the robot, reverse an automated action, interpret an escalation, or review what happened 
afterward? Progress toward accountable autonomy therefore requires systems that close the loop 
from patient state to care action while making authority, traceability, and responsibility explicit. 
These findings support agentic robotics as the prospective design horizon developed in the 
concluding section, with governed, ecosystem-embedded care robots whose actions are selected 
through clinically validated policies and remain subject to explicit human authority. 
6. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 
This review was guided by three evidence-mapping questions concerning how AI-enabled RPM, 
AAL, telecare, and robotic care systems are architecturally configured; how embodied systems are 
positioned across decision–action coupling and maturity levels; and which patterns characterize 
systems that approach deployable care action. Taken together, the findings show a field moving 
from monitoring toward action but not yet toward fully autonomous care. Some systems reach 
bounded A3 action, but none demonstrate open-ended autonomous care in sustained real-world 
deployment. Current maturity remains concentrated in conservative A1 architectures, where AI

--- PAGE 26 [pdf_text] ---
26 
supports monitoring, prediction, alerting, or decision support while humans retain responsibility 
for clinical action. Findings from the small embodied subset require cautious interpretation, 
physical embodiment is neither a prerequisite for higher decision–action coupling nor sufficient to 
make a system deployable. More specifically, the evidence shows that embodied systems are 
disproportionately associated with higher decision–action coupling. Unlike a dashboard, a robot 
can turn AI output into situated physical or social behavior through speech, movement, prompting, 
navigation, manipulation, and interaction with patients or caregivers. Embodiment therefore 
makes the transition from monitoring to action visible while raising the burden of safety, oversight, 
validation, and accountability. 
The temporal trend supports this interpretation. Earlier RPM/AAL systems made care more 
visible through cloud monitoring, dashboards, and rule-based alerts. Edge–hybrid architectures 
then moved inference closer to patients and homes, while predictive machine learning improved 
detection without necessarily changing authority. More recent systems have explored distributed 
and decision-capable ecosystems in which AI models, sensors, devices, robots, clinicians, 
caregivers, and users coordinate bounded care action. The field is therefore redistributing 
intelligence across a care ecosystem rather than progressing linearly from rules to machine learning 
and autonomous control. 
The evidence map supports the governed, ecosystem-embedded care robot as a prospective 
design horizon. We use the term “agentic robotics” to describe this prospective horizon beyond 
the maturity levels observed across the corpus. Agentic robotics refers to a multiagent care 
architecture in which artificial agents, robotic embodiments, clinicians, caregivers, and patients 
share perception, decision support, action, and accountability across a supervised care loop. Within 
this architecture, robots operate through clinically validated action policies appropriate to their 
intended use and level of risk, with explicit human authority over clinically consequential 
decisions. Their agency derives from participation in a context that makes action informed, 
constrained, traceable, and accountable. By integrating patient and environmental sensing, 
distributed inference, clinical and caregiver knowledge, local interaction, and governance, such 
architectures could strengthen perception and adaptation in unstructured home environments. In 
this model, embodiment provides AI with a situated interface to daily life, while ecosystem 
integration prevents situated action from becoming isolated, opaque, or unsafe. 
This design horizon extends beyond the maturity demonstrated by the reviewed systems. Those 
with the strongest decision–action coupling remain below sustained deployment, while

--- PAGE 27 [pdf_text] ---
27 
background reviews identify continuing translational barriers involving data quality, 
interoperability, clinical workflow, digital inclusion, cybersecurity, scalability, and multicenter 
validation (55, 58–60). 
Progress toward agentic robotics requires governance to be designed and reported as an 
architectural component using the safeguards formalized in SIDAG. Explainability must also 
support interpretation of system output by clinicians, caregivers, and users. Validation should 
advance from short-term pilots to longitudinal and multisite evaluation of A2 and A3 systems, 
with embodied technologies assessed as a component of care ecosystems instead of isolated robot 
prototypes. Technical performance must ultimately be linked to clinical meaningful outcomes, 
including preserved mobility, improved adherence and self-management, reduced caregiver 
burden, stronger social connection, and timely escalation. 
The strongest conclusion of this review is that embodied AI becomes clinically credible only 
when it is connected, supervised, and accountable. Accountable autonomy therefore defines the 
most promising horizon for agentic robotics. Ecosystem-embedded robotic and RPM/AAL 
systems should act through context aware and governed pathways, closing the loop from patient 
state to care action while keeping human authority, traceability, and responsibility explicit.
