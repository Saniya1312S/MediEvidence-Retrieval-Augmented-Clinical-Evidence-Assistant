MedJev: Evaluating Zero-Shot System-One
Decision Making in Medicine
Simon A. Lee1, Jiahang Sha1, Moonseong Jeong1, Yunbei Pan1 and Jeffrey N. Chiang1,2
1UCLA Computational Medicine, 2UCLA Neurosurgery
System-One decision models such as Jev answer structured questions about a given state, including
option selection and probability estimation, in a single forward pass without generating text. Their speed,
low computational cost, and ability to produce calibrated probabilities make them promising for clinical
decision support. We investigate the zero-shot performance of MedJev, an open System-One model that
runs locally on a laptop, across ten medical benchmarks. Four benchmarks assess medical knowledge
and diagnosis (MedQA, USMLE, NEJM, and DiagnosisArena), while six evaluate patient-level decisions
using cohorts from MIMIC-IV (In-Hospital Mortality, Length of Stay > 7 Days, 30-Day Readmission, and
ED Admission at 2 h) and MIMIC-IV-ED (ED Triage Admission and ED Triage Disposition). We compare
MedJev against zero-shot LLM baselines, including Qwen3.5-9B, MedGemma-1.5-4B, Gemma-3-4B,
Qwen3.5-2B, and Pythia-2.8B. We also compare against published results for GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o,
and o1 on the same benchmarks, as well as XGBoost and TF-IDF models. In the zero-shot setting, MedJev
achieves accuracies of 72.5% on MedQA, 75.4% on USMLE, and 80.1% on NEJM, with well-calibrated
confidence estimates (ECE 0.028–0.063). Its accuracy is within 2–4 percentage points of published
zero-shot GPT-4 results on MedQA and USMLE (74.7% and 78.9%, respectively) and substantially
exceeds that of GPT-3.5 (44.6% and 50.8%). On patient-level tasks, MedJev achieves an AUROC of 0.926
for In-Hospital Mortality, matching or exceeding supervised models (XGBoost 0.902), and performs
comparably on 30-Day Readmission. However, it underperforms supervised models on operational
outcomes such as ED Triage Admission (0.745 vs. 0.809). Moreover, its patient-level probability
estimates do not account for local outcome prevalence (In-Hospital Mortality ECE 0.286). To address
this limitation, we introduce a lightweight, two-parameter recalibration method that adapts predicted
probabilities to local patient populations using 50–200 labeled examples, reducing the In-Hospital
Mortality ECE to 0.007. These findings highlight the potential of open System-One models as a new
baseline for medical AI, offering a competitive zero-shot alternative to general-purpose LLMs and
EHR-specific predictive models. We release our code and evaluation harness to support future research.

1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly evaluated on medical tasks, including clinical question
answering, diagnosis, and patient outcome prediction [1]. Most evaluations use generative models
that produce textual answers, which are subsequently parsed into decisions. However, many medical
prediction tasks require a structured output rather than a generated response. For example, predicting
in-hospital mortality requires estimating the probability of death given the information available at a
particular point in a patient’s hospitalization, while emergency department triage requires selecting
among possible dispositions. These tasks can be expressed as decisions over a fixed set of outcomes,
making them suitable for models that directly estimate probabilities without generating text.
System-One models provide an alternative to generative inference for such tasks. Inspired by the

distinction between fast and deliberative reasoning [2], System-One models directly answer structured
questions about a given state in a single forward pass. Jev [3] implements this approach through
typed requests that pair an input state, represented as text or JSON, with questions specifying
the desired output. A CHOICE request returns a probability distribution over candidate answers,
while a NOUL request estimates the probability of a binary outcome. Unlike conventional generative
evaluation, which relies on decoding and parsing model responses, this interface produces structured
probabilities directly. It therefore provides a common representation for evaluating multiple-choice
medical questions and patient-level predictions from electronic health records (EHRs).
Despite this interface, the applicability of System-One models to medical decision tasks remains
unclear. Previous evaluations of Jev have demonstrated competitive zero-shot performance across
general-purpose benchmarks [4], but medical knowledge, diagnostic reasoning, and EHR-based
prediction present different challenges. Medical knowledge benchmarks require selecting correct
answers from clinical vignettes that mimic differential diagnosis, whereas patient-level prediction
requires interpreting heterogeneous longitudinal records and estimating outcomes whose prevalence
may vary across clinical populations. Furthermore, a model that accurately ranks patients by risk
does not necessarily produce probabilities that reflect observed outcome frequencies. Evaluating
System-One models in medicine therefore requires examining both their predictive performance and
probability calibration across different task types.
In this work, we study how an open System-One model can be incorporated into a medical evaluation
pipeline. We release MedJev which builds on OpenJev [5] and run a suite of evaluations. Our
evaluation represents each benchmark as a Jev request consisting of an input state and a typed
question. Medical question-answering and diagnosis benchmarks are formulated as CHOICE requests,
while patient-level outcomes are primarily formulated as NOUL requests. For EHR-based tasks,
we construct patient states using only information available at a predefined prediction time. This
formulation allows the same inference interface to be applied across medical knowledge questions,
longitudinal patient records, and emergency department triage data.
We evaluate MedJev on ten medical benchmarks (expanding on the work of [6]) spanning medical
knowledge, differential diagnosis, and patient-specific prediction. These include MedQA [7], USMLE
[7], NEJM Case Challenges, and DiagnosisArena [8], together with six outcomes derived from MIMICIV [9] and MIMIC-IV-ED [9]. To assess the effect of System-One training, we compare MedJev against
its underlying language model and other open-weight LLMs using identical prompts and probability
readouts. We additionally consider published results from frontier LLMs and train supervised XGBoost
and TF-IDF baselines for EHR specific tasks. Beyond predictive accuracy and discrimination, we
evaluate probability calibration and examine whether lightweight post-hoc recalibration can adapt
zero-shot predictions to local outcome prevalence. We also vary the number of labeled examples
available to supervised models to characterize when task-specific training becomes preferable to
zero-shot inference.
Our results show that System-One models can serve as competitive zero-shot predictors across several
medical tasks, although their effectiveness depends on the prediction target. MedJev achieves strong
performance on medical knowledge benchmarks and competitive risk discrimination for outcomes
such as in-hospital mortality, while supervised models perform better on operational outcomes
such as emergency department admission. Although its confidence estimates are well calibrated on
medical question-answering benchmarks, its patient-level probabilities require adjustment to local
outcome frequencies. A lightweight recalibration procedure using a small number of labeled examples
substantially reduces this error without changing patient risk rankings. These findings illustrate how
System-One models can be evaluated within a unified medical prediction pipeline and clarify the role
of zero-shot inference, local calibration, and supervised learning in clinical prediction settings.

2. Related Work
System-One decision models.
Most language models perform decision-making tasks through
autoregressive generation, producing textual responses that must be interpreted or parsed to recover
a final prediction. System-One decision models instead formulate inference as a direct mapping from
an observed state and a structured question to a probability distribution over possible outcomes. Jev
[3] implements this paradigm through three typed primitives: Choice, which returns a distribution
over a finite set of alternatives; Noul, which estimates the probability of a binary outcome; and
Score, which represents judgments on an ordinal scale. These primitives allow classification,
probability estimation, and ordinal assessment to share a common inference interface without
requiring autoregressive decoding. Deußer et. al, [4] systematically evaluate Jev in the zero-shot
setting across 37 public datasets, comparing its predictive performance and computational efficiency
against open-weight LLMs evaluated using exact option probabilities. Their results establish the
feasibility of System-One inference for general-purpose classification and decision tasks. However,
their evaluation includes only one biomedical dataset, PubMedQA [10], and does not examine patientlevel prediction from EHRs. More recently, OpenJev [5] provides an open-weight implementation
of the Jev interface by fine-tuning Qwen3.5-9B [11]. OpenJev obtains structured predictions from
the probability distribution over option-letter tokens, followed by temperature calibration. Our study
extends prior System-One evaluations to medical knowledge, diagnosis, and longitudinal patient
prediction, while examining both zero-shot predictive performance and the reliability of the resulting
probabilities.
Medical question answering and diagnosis.
Medical question answering has become a standard
approach for evaluating the clinical knowledge and reasoning capabilities of language models [12–16].
MedQA [17] evaluates medical reasoning using multiple-choice questions derived from professional
medical examinations, including the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE). MedMCQA [18] provides a large collection of multiple-choice questions spanning medical subjects, while
PubMedQA [10] evaluates biomedical question answering using research abstracts. These benchmarks
have been widely used to measure the medical knowledge encoded in pretrained and instructiontuned language models. Work on medical LLMs, including Med-PaLM [1], demonstrates that large
generative models can achieve strong performance on medical examination questions, motivating
their use as reference models for medical AI evaluation. However, performance on examination-style
questions does not necessarily characterize a model’s ability to interpret complex clinical presentations
or make patient-specific predictions.
Diagnostic benchmarks address this limitation by evaluating models on clinical cases rather than
isolated knowledge questions. DiagnosisArena [8] constructs diagnostic tasks from published case
reports, requiring models to distinguish among candidate diagnoses using clinical histories, examinations, and investigation findings. [19] investigate the diagnostic performance of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4
on clinical vignettes using different reasoning prompts and clinician-graded open-ended responses.
These studies predominantly evaluate generative models through textual answers, often allowing
reasoning or explanation before a final diagnosis. In contrast, our evaluation formulates medical
knowledge and diagnostic tasks as structured multiple-choice decisions, enabling direct probabilitybased comparisons among System-One models and conventional LLMs. Because multiple-choice and
open-ended diagnosis differ in difficulty, previously reported generative results are treated as external
reference points rather than strictly equivalent evaluations.
Clinical prediction from electronic health records.

data [20–27] and EHRs [28–30] differs from medical question answering because models must

reason over heterogeneous patient information collected across time. Established prediction tasks
include in-hospital mortality, length of stay, and hospital readmission, which have been widely studied
using critical care datasets such as MIMIC [31–34]. These tasks require predictions at clinically
meaningful decision times, making the availability and temporal ordering of patient information
important aspects of benchmark construction. The Medical Event Data Standard (MEDS) [35–37]
provides a standardized representation of longitudinal patient records as timestamped clinical events,
facilitating the development and evaluation of models across EHR-derived tasks [28, 29, 38, 39].
Emergency department prediction presents a related but distinct setting in which decisions must
be made using information available early in the patient encounter. [40] study hospital admission
prediction at emergency department triage using routinely collected patient characteristics, vital
signs, and chief complaints. Gradient-boosted trees and other supervised tabular models provide
strong baselines for such structured clinical prediction tasks. Language models offer an alternative by
representing patient records as text and applying pretrained language understanding capabilities
to the resulting sequences. [41] investigate this approach using serialized emergency department
records, while EHRSHOT [42] introduces a framework for evaluating foundation models on EHR
prediction tasks under limited-label settings. These studies motivate the evaluation of pretrained
models on patient-level outcomes, but their settings generally involve either supervised adaptation,
learned patient representations, or conventional language-model inference. We instead examine
whether a System-One model can directly estimate clinical outcome probabilities from serialized EHR
states without task-specific training.
Language models for structured data and probability calibration.
Applying language models to
structured clinical records is closely related to research on language-based representations of tabular
data [41, 43–45]. TabLLM [46] demonstrates that serializing tabular features into natural-language
descriptions enables pretrained language models to perform classification with relatively few labeled
examples. This approach is particularly relevant when task-specific training data are scarce, although
conventional supervised methods often become more competitive as the number of labeled examples
increases. [47] further demonstrate the strong performance of tree-based methods on typical tabular
prediction tasks, motivating their inclusion as supervised baselines. For clinical prediction, this
comparison is important because pretrained models may provide useful predictions without local
labels, whereas models trained directly on EHR data can learn relationships specific to the target
population and outcome.
Beyond discrimination and accuracy, clinical prediction requires an assessment of probability calibration. A model may correctly rank patients by risk while systematically overestimating or underestimating the frequency of an outcome. This distinction is particularly important when a model is
evaluated zero-shot on a patient population whose outcome prevalence differs from the distributions
encountered during pretraining. Post-hoc calibration methods provide a lightweight mechanism for
correcting predicted probabilities without retraining the underlying model. Platt scaling [48] learns a
logistic transformation of model scores, while temperature scaling [49] adjusts the sharpness of a
predicted probability distribution. These approaches preserve binary risk rankings or multiclass predicted labels, respectively, while modifying the reported confidence. In our evaluation, we distinguish
the zero-shot predictive capabilities of System-One models from the calibration of their probabilities
on local patient populations. We investigate whether a small number of labeled examples is sufficient
for post-hoc recalibration and compare this adaptation strategy against supervised models trained
under the same label budgets.

3. Methodology
We evaluate MedJev across ten medical benchmarks using a common pipeline that converts each task
into a structured decision request. The pipeline consists of constructing an input state, specifying a
question and its possible outcomes, obtaining a probability distribution from the model, and evaluating
the prediction against the ground-truth outcome. All models are evaluated on the same test examples,
with additional comparisons against supervised baselines and locally recalibrated predictions.

3.2. Medical Benchmarks
We evaluate four medical knowledge and diagnosis benchmarks and six patient-level prediction
benchmarks. All tasks are formulated using the same Jev request interface, with Choice for
categorical outcomes and Noul for binary outcomes. Table 1 summarizes the dataset sizes, prediction
units, and label distributions.
Medical knowledge and diagnosis.
We use MedQA, USMLE, NEJM, and DiagnosisArena to evaluate
medical knowledge and diagnostic decision-making. MedQA and USMLE correspond to the fiveoption BigBio and four-option MedQA-USMLE releases of USMLE-style examination questions [17].
DiagnosisArena [8] contains 915 publicly available case reports with four candidate diagnoses. NEJM
[19] contains 512 diagnostic vignettes originally evaluated using open-ended responses from GPT-3.5
and GPT-4.
All four benchmarks are represented as Choice requests. For NEJM, vignettes overlapping with
MedQA retain their original five answer options. For the remaining cases, we construct five-option
questions using the correct diagnosis, up to four clinician-graded incorrect GPT responses, and

from the original open-ended evaluation, published GPT results are used only as external references.
Longitudinal EHR prediction.
We construct four patient-level benchmarks using MIMIC-IV [31]
represented in the Medical Event Data Standard (MEDS) [37]. The tasks are In-Hospital Mortality,
Length of Stay > 7 Days, 30-Day Readmission, and ED Admission at 2 h. Each task is defined by an
outcome and a fixed prediction time 𝑡∗.
In-Hospital Mortality and Length of Stay > 7 Days are predicted 24 h after hospital admission among
adult patients who remain hospitalized at that time. 30-Day Readmission is predicted at discharge,
and ED Admission at 2 h is predicted two hours after emergency department arrival. For each
prediction, the model receives only events within (𝑡∗−365 d, 𝑡∗]. The available events are serialized
into a compact JSON state containing patient demographics, clinical history, care units, laboratory
measurements, and medications when available.
We exclude information that would not be available at the specified prediction time. In particular,
discharge-stamped diagnoses and diagnosis-related groups are excluded from the 24 h prediction
tasks. For ED Admission at 2 h, we additionally remove admission-only demographic fields, ED and
admission bookkeeping codes beginning 24 h before arrival, and nonlaboratory events recorded after
ED arrival. These restrictions prevent outcome-related information from entering the input state.
Emergency department triage.
We construct two additional benchmarks from MIMIC-IV-ED [50]:
ED Triage Admission and ED Triage Disposition. Both are evaluated at the time of triage using
information available at that point in the encounter. The input state includes age, sex, race, arrival
transport, triage vital signs, pain score, Emergency Severity Index acuity, chief complaint, and
emergency department visits within the preceding year. Subsequent medications, vital signs, diagnoses,
and medication reconciliation records are excluded.
ED Triage Admission is a binary Noul task predicting whether the visit results in hospital admission.
ED Triage Disposition is a six-option Choice task predicting discharge home, admission, transfer,
departure before completion, death, or another disposition. Figure 1 provides an example of the
serialized input and corresponding question.
Dataset preparation.
The longitudinal benchmarks use a MEDS-Transforms extract [51] of MIMICIV v2.2 containing 299,712 patients distributed across five training shards, one tuning shard, and
one held-out shard. Training examples are drawn from the training shards and test examples from
the held-out shard. MIMIC-IV-ED v2.2 contains 425,087 emergency department stays. We retain
adult patients with a corresponding MEDS birth record and assign each patient to the MEDS split
associated with their subject_id. This ensures patient-disjoint training and test partitions across
the EHR benchmarks.

Supervised EHR baselines.
We evaluate XGBoost [56] and TF-IDF with logistic regression as
supervised baselines for the six patient-level tasks. For longitudinal MEDS records, XGBoost uses
bag-of-codes features, including event counts and last observed values. For emergency department

approaches [40]. TF-IDF with logistic regression operates directly on the serialized patient state.
Both supervised models are trained using locally labeled examples from the corresponding training
partitions. XGBoost tree depth, column subsampling, minimum child weight, and number of trees are
selected by five-fold cross-validation. TF-IDF 𝑛-gram ranges and logistic regression regularization
are selected using the same procedure. The selected models are refitted using all available training
examples. We evaluate supervised performance with up to 1,000 labeled examples per task.
3.4. Calibration and Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate MedJev in the zero-shot setting without task-specific training or local labels. To examine
the calibration of patient-level probabilities, we additionally consider post-hoc recalibration using
labeled examples from the corresponding training partitions. For binary Noul tasks, we apply Platt
scaling [48] to the predicted logits. For multiclass Choice tasks, we apply temperature scaling [49]
to the predicted distributions. These transformations use at most two fitted parameters and do not
update the underlying language model. For medical knowledge benchmarks, a shared temperature is
fitted using MedQA training questions. For patient-level tasks, recalibration is fitted separately for
each outcome using local labels.
All models are evaluated on identical test examples. Models requiring labeled data, including
supervised baselines and post-hoc recalibration, use the same patient-disjoint training partitions.
Model selection and calibration fitting do not use test labels. We additionally vary the available label
budget from 25 to 1,000 examples to compare the label efficiency of supervised learning and post-hoc
recalibration.
For Choice tasks, we report top-1 accuracy and expected calibration error (ECE) computed from
the probability assigned to the predicted class using 15 equal-width bins [49]. For binary Noul
tasks, we report the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under
the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), ECE, and Brier score. We compute 95% percentile bootstrap
confidence intervals over test examples [57]. Differences between MedJev and directly evaluated
baselines are assessed using paired bootstrap resampling of the same test examples.
Implementation.
All language models are evaluated locally using 8-bit MLX builds [58] on an
Apple silicon laptop with 48 GB of unified memory. Our inference engine uses the frozen OpenJev
helper and replaces only the underlying model client. We verify that local inference reproduces the
outputs of the MedJev HTTP server on a verification sample, with a maximum absolute probability
difference of zero. MedJev requires approximately 0.5 s for a MedQA question and 2 s for a serialized
MEDS state. All EHR processing and model inference are performed locally, without transmitting
patient records to external services.

4.3. Probability Calibration
We evaluate whether the probabilities produced by MedJev correspond to observed outcome frequencies. Figures 4 and 5 present expected calibration error and calibration curves across the benchmarks.
MedJev is well calibrated on medical knowledge benchmarks, with ECE values of 0.039 on MedQA,
0.028 on USMLE, and 0.063 on NEJM. In contrast, its zero-shot probabilities are poorly calibrated on
patient-level outcomes. For In-Hospital Mortality, the mean predicted probability is 0.312, compared
with an observed mortality rate of 2.6%, resulting in an ECE of 0.286. The corresponding ECE values
are 0.191 for Length of Stay > 7 Days, 0.272 for 30-Day Readmission, and 0.147 for ED Triage
Admission. These results indicate that good risk discrimination does not necessarily imply calibrated
outcome probabilities in the zero-shot setting.
We apply post-hoc recalibration using labeled examples from the corresponding training partitions.
Recalibration reduces ECE to 0.007 on In-Hospital Mortality, 0.027 on Length of Stay > 7 Days, 0.024
on 30-Day Readmission, and 0.041 on ED Triage Admission. These values are comparable to those
obtained by XGBoost, which achieves ECE values of 0.014, 0.059, 0.046, and 0.042 on the same tasks.
Recalibration changes the probability estimates without changing the reported AUROC.

5. Discussion
Our results show that System-One models can perform medical decision tasks without autoregressive
generation. MedJev achieves competitive performance on medical knowledge and diagnosis benchmarks and provides useful discrimination for several patient-level outcomes. However, its performance
varies across clinical tasks, and its zero-shot probability estimates do not consistently reflect local
outcome frequencies. These findings suggest that System-One models can serve as zero-shot baselines
for medical prediction, with local recalibration or supervised training required depending on the task.
Zero-shot predictive performance.
MedJev achieves strong accuracy and calibration on medical
knowledge benchmarks using a single forward pass. Its performance on MedQA and USMLE is within
a few percentage points of published zero-shot GPT-4 results and substantially exceeds reported
GPT-3.5 accuracy. On DiagnosisArena, MedJev outperforms the reported GPT-4o result but remains
below o1. These comparisons are limited by differences in evaluation protocols, particularly for
benchmarks originally evaluated using open-ended responses. Nevertheless, the results demonstrate
that structured probability estimation can provide competitive performance on medical questionanswering tasks without generating textual answers.

On patient-level prediction, MedJev achieves an AUROC of 0.926 for In-Hospital Mortality, comparable
to supervised models trained on labeled examples. Performance is also comparable to supervised
baselines on 30-Day Readmission. In contrast, supervised models perform better on ED admission
and length-of-stay prediction. These differences may reflect the extent to which outcomes depend on
patient characteristics and local clinical practices. Mortality is closely associated with clinical severity,
whereas admission and discharge decisions are also influenced by institutional policies, resource
availability, and care processes. Consequently, the relative performance of zero-shot and supervised
models depends on the prediction target.
Comparisons with Qwen3.5-9B further indicate that System-One training contributes to predictive
performance. MedJev improves accuracy on MedQA and USMLE and discrimination on In-Hospital
Mortality and Length of Stay > 7 Days under the same prompting and readout procedure. It also
produces better-calibrated probabilities than its base model across several benchmarks. However, these
improvements are not consistent across all tasks, as Qwen3.5-9B performs better on ED admission
prediction. System-One training therefore improves performance on selected medical tasks but does
not uniformly outperform the underlying language model.
Probability calibration and local adaptation.
Although MedJev produces well-calibrated confidence estimates on medical knowledge benchmarks, its patient-level predictions exhibit substantial
calibration error. In particular, the model overestimates the probability of in-hospital mortality relative
to the observed event rate. This distinction is important because discrimination measures whether a
model correctly ranks patients by risk, whereas calibration measures whether predicted probabilities
correspond to observed outcome frequencies. A model can achieve high AUROC while producing
probabilities that are unsuitable for direct interpretation as patient risk.
Post-hoc recalibration substantially reduces this error without retraining MedJev. Using Platt scaling
fitted on local labeled examples, we obtain calibration performance comparable to supervised baselines
while preserving the original risk ranking. The label-efficiency experiments show that 50–200 labeled
examples can substantially improve calibration across patient-level tasks. In comparison, supervised
models generally require more labeled examples to match the discrimination of zero-shot MedJev,
although this varies by outcome. Calibration parameters fitted on one medical knowledge benchmark

These results support separating zero-shot prediction from local probability calibration in the evaluation pipeline. The System-One model provides an initial prediction without task-specific training,
while a small labeled dataset can be used to adjust the probability estimates to the local population.
However, recalibration does not improve discrimination and cannot address limitations in the underlying risk ranking. For tasks where supervised models achieve higher AUROC, additional local
training remains beneficial.
Implications for medical evaluation.
The structured request interface allows medical knowledge
questions and patient-level prediction tasks to be evaluated using the same inference procedure. Each
task is represented by an input state, a question, and a set of possible outcomes, and the model
returns probabilities that can be evaluated for accuracy, discrimination, and calibration. This removes
the need to generate and parse textual responses and allows direct comparisons with other language
models using the same probability readout.
The results also indicate that evaluation of System-One models should consider both zero-shot
performance and the effect of local adaptation. Zero-shot evaluation measures predictive performance
without task-specific labels, while recalibration measures whether the predicted probabilities can
be adjusted using limited local data. Supervised baselines provide a reference for the performance
achievable through task-specific training. Considering these settings together helps determine when a
System-One model provides a useful alternative to conventional predictive models. The local inference
setup further allows these evaluations to be conducted without transmitting patient records to external
model services.
Limitations.
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, the
number of positive outcomes is small for some tasks. In-Hospital Mortality contains only 2.6% deaths
in the test set, resulting in wide confidence intervals for AUROC. Second, we evaluate a single prompt
and serialization format for each task. The questions, input representations, and event limits are fixed
rather than optimized, although prior work has shown that serialization choices can affect languagemodel performance on structured data [46]. Third, the NEJM benchmark was originally evaluated
using open-ended diagnoses, whereas our evaluation uses multiple-choice questions. Published GPT
results therefore provide contextual references rather than directly comparable estimates.
The medical knowledge benchmarks also have potential overlap with model pretraining data. MedQA
and USMLE share question stems, and MedQA has been publicly available since 2020. We cannot
exclude the possibility that some evaluation questions appeared during pretraining. For the EHR
benchmarks, the MEDS extract does not provide a complete ICU item dictionary. We identify bedside
measurements using standard MIMIC-IV item identifiers, but omit other chart events that cannot be
reliably interpreted. Finally, MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-IV-ED originate from a single academic medical
center. The results may not generalize to institutions with different patient populations, clinical
practices, or outcome prevalence. Evaluation on additional clinical datasets is needed to assess
generalizability.
Data use and ethics.
MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-IV-ED were accessed under the PhysioNet credentialed
data use agreement. All data processing and model inference were performed locally, with the model
server restricted to the loopback interface. No individual-level patient records are included in this
paper, and calibration parameters fitted on credentialed data are not publicly released. The evaluated

A. Question Formats
All benchmarks are represented as Jev requests consisting of a state and a typed question. Choice
questions return a probability distribution over candidate answers, while Noul questions return the
probability of a binary outcome. Table 1 summarizes the questions used for each benchmark.

Prompt construction.
Each question is rendered as a prompt containing the serialized state, question
instructions, and candidate options. For Choice questions, the options are assigned single-token
letter labels. The prompt follows the format below.
State:
<state JSON>
Question:
<instructions>
Options:
[A] <option 1>: <description>
[B] <option 2>: <description>
...
Answer with the letter of the best option only.
For Noul questions, the candidate outcomes correspond to true and false, with optional descriptions
specifying each outcome. MedJev reads the option-token probabilities at the first answer position
and applies the transformations in eqs. (1) and (2) to obtain the final probability distribution.

C. Supervised references
Hyperparameters chosen by five-fold cross-validation on the training examples. XGBoost: learning
rate 0.05, subsampling 0.8, a grid over depth {2, 3, 5}, minimum child weight {1, 5} and column
subsampling {0.3, 0.7}, with the number of trees set by early stopping inside each fold. TF-IDF: word
𝑛-grams {(1, 1), (1, 2)}, 50k features, sublinear term frequency, ℓ2 strength 𝐶∈{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10}. For
ED Triage Disposition, classes with fewer than 10 training stays (e.g. expired) are merged into “other”
during training.
D. Reproducibility
Everything is produced by the scripts in src/scripts/: prepare_data.py (Jev requests and training sets), extract_jev.py and run_variants_gpu.sh (zero-shot answers for every language
model; MLX), train_probes.py (recalibration), run_baselines.py (supervised references),
label_efficiency.py (label budgets, from cached outputs), evaluate.py (scores and metrics),
make_paper_assets.py (tables and every number in this paper) and make_figures.py (figures).
Every model output is cached on disk, keyed by the exact request.
