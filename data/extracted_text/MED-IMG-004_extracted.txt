A B S T R A C T
Deep learning (DL) has achieved remarkable success in medical image segmentation by learning
data-driven representations from large-scale annotated datasets. However, medical images are often
collected independently across clinical sites, resulting in substantial domain shifts due to differences
in imaging modalities, acquisition protocols, and intensity distributions. Meanwhile, segmentation
classes may vary across sites due to differences in annotation standards, leading to class shifts.
These evolving domains and class definitions can substantially degrade segmentation performance on
newly encountered data, while sequential adaptation may cause catastrophic forgetting of knowledge
acquired from previously encountered domains and classes. Existing incremental learning methods
for medical image segmentation primarily address either domain shifts or class shifts, with these
two settings largely investigated independently. This separation leaves a critical gap in developing a
unified approach that can simultaneously adapt to evolving domains and newly introduced classes
while retaining previously acquired knowledge. To address this challenge, we propose FisherGuided Continuously Adaptive Representation Learning (FGCARL), a unified framework for
domain- and class-incremental 3D volumetric medical image segmentation. FGCARL comprises
two key components: Mutual Adaptive Instance Representation (MAIR), which adaptively aligns
feature distributions across sequentially encountered domains through bidirectional normalization and
denormalization, and Fisher-Guided Knowledge Retention (FGKR), which selectively preserves
parameters important for previously learned domains and classes through Fisher-guided regularization. We conducted extensive experiments across six cross-institutional segmentation tasks using
diverse segmentation architectures. Experimental results demonstrate that FGCARL consistently
outperformed state-of-the-art continual learning methods, effectively adapting to domain and class
shifts while mitigating catastrophic forgetting and maintaining strong segmentation performance on
both previously learned and newly encountered domains.
1. Introduction
Segmentation of anatomical structures and pathological
regions in medical images plays a critical role in supporting diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning. It has
markedly progressed by the development in deep learning
(DL)-based methods [58, 11, 1, 69, 71]. DL models achieve
impressive segmentation performance due to their ability to
capture rich, data-driven features [26, 79, 70, 56]. Typically,
these models are trained on a specific domain to effectively
learn features from domain-specific data. However, when
these models are employed to make zero-shot predictions
on new domains, they fail to capture domain-specific patterns, thus limiting their generalizability and applicability
to unseen datasets or tasks [46, 72]. Moreover, in clinical
practice, DL models are generally developed for specific
tasks. However, clinical datasets are often constructed and
developed incrementally across multiple sites, each using
different imaging modalities and acquisition protocols or
parameters [40, 44, 41, 43]. Additionally, the set of annotated anatomical structures may expand over time as new
lesions or structures of interest are introduced, driven by the

Although these approaches have advanced continual adaptation for medical image segmentation, several important
challenges remain.
First, existing methods predominantly consider domain and class shifts independently. Most DIL and CIL
approaches are specifically designed for either evolving data
distributions or expanding label spaces, while real-world
clinical deployment may involve both changes simultaneously. For example, a model may be sequentially deployed
across institutions that use different imaging modalities
while also requiring segmentation of newly introduced
anatomical structures or lesion types. Although a few classdomain-incremental learning approaches have been explored in natural image recognition [31, 55], their underlying assumptions and learning mechanisms do not directly
translate to medical image segmentation. In particular, many
natural-image approaches rely on image-level class representations, prototypes, or classification-oriented objectives,
whereas medical image segmentation requires dense voxelwise predictions and preservation of spatially structured
anatomical representations. Consequently, a unified framework specifically designed for simultaneous domain and
class shifts in volumetric medical image segmentation remains underexplored.
Second, rehearsal-based strategies for mitigating catastrophic forgetting raise important challenges in clinical
applications. Rehearsal methods preserve knowledge from
previous tasks by storing and replaying representative samples or exemplars [19]. However, retaining patient imaging
data for continual model training can introduce privacy, security, and data-governance concerns, particularly in multiinstitutional clinical settings where patient data may be
subject to strict access and retention policies [33]. This motivates rehearsal-free approaches that can preserve previously
acquired knowledge without retaining sensitive patient data.
Third, existing adaptation mechanisms may be closely
coupled to specific segmentation architectures and normalization strategies. This issue becomes particularly relevant as modern medical image segmentation increasingly
incorporates diverse architectures, including convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs).
For example, ViT-based models commonly employ layer
normalization (LN) [2], which normalizes feature statistics
within individual samples and may not explicitly account for
domain-level distribution differences. Such normalization
can therefore limit the model’s ability to explicitly adapt its
feature representations to substantial domain shifts. More
broadly, architecture-specific adaptation mechanisms may
hinder the development of a general continual learning strategy that can be consistently applied across heterogeneous
segmentation architectures.
These limitations limit the applicability and generalizability of current IL methods on medical image segmentation, and highlight the need for a unified, rehearsal-free, and
architecture-agnostic framework that can simultaneously accommodate evolving domains and class spaces while preserving previously acquired knowledge. Such a framework
is particularly important for clinical scenarios in which
segmentation models must continuously adapt to new institutions, imaging protocols, and anatomical or pathological
targets without access to historical patient data.
To address these limitations, we propose Fisher-Guided
Continuously Adaptive Representation Learning (FGCARL), a unified framework for domain- and class-incremental
learning in 3D volumetric medical image segmentation. FGCARL is designed to simultaneously accommodate evolving imaging domains, including differences in modalities,
acquisition protocols, and institutional distributions, and
expanding or changing segmentation label spaces, without
requiring access to previously observed patient data. The
framework consists of two complementary components:
Mutual Adaptive Instance Representation (MAIR) and
Fisher-Guided Knowledge Retention (FGKR).
The MAIR module addresses the representation-level
challenges arising from domain shifts. It performs bidirectional feature transformation through adaptive normalization and denormalization, enabling the model to establish
consistent feature representations across previously encountered and newly observed domains. Specifically, MAIR normalizes feature representations during the forward propagation to reduce domain-specific distribution discrepancies and subsequently restores domain-relevant statistics
through denormalization during backward propagation, allowing domain-specific information to be adaptively incorporated during optimization. Unlike methods that rely on
storing historical samples or explicitly maintaining domainspecific representations, MAIR performs adaptation directly
in the feature space and therefore does not require retention
of raw patient data. Moreover, its feature-level formulation

The FGKR strategy addresses catastrophic forgetting

FGCARL therefore provides a unified solution to the
three key challenges in medical image segmentation continual learning: simultaneous domain and class shifts, privacy constraints associated with rehearsal-based learning,
and adaptation across heterogeneous segmentation architectures. Unlike conventional domain-specific normalization approaches [7, 39, 13], MAIR performs bidirectional

observed datasets. Furthermore, its non-parametric distribution matching formulation does not impose restrictive Gaussian assumptions on medical imaging feature distributions,
allowing it to accommodate complex and potentially nonGaussian representations. By combining representationlevel domain adaptation with Fisher-guided parameter-level
knowledge retention, FGCARL enables continual learning
across domains with different imaging characteristics and
evolving anatomical or pathological label spaces while
mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
Extensive experiments across multiple cross-institutional
segmentation tasks and heterogeneous segmentation architectures demonstrate that FGCARL consistently outperforms state-of-the-art incremental learning methods, validating its effectiveness, robustness, and generalizability for
domain- and class-incremental medical image segmentation.

2. Related Works
2.1. Class Incremental Learning
CIL aims to sequentially incorporate newly introduced
classes while preserving the segmentation performance of
previously learned classes. Existing CIL approaches can
be broadly categorized into four main paradigms. First,
replay-based methods retain a subset of historical data or
generate synthetic samples and replay them during incremental training to alleviate catastrophic forgetting [3, 57,
23, 64, 66, 16, 34, 37, 61]. Although effective, storing or
replaying historical medical images and annotations can
introduce substantial privacy, security, and data-governance
concerns in clinical applications. Second, architecture-based
methods dynamically expand model capacity by introducing
task-specific branches, sub-networks, or other architectural
components for newly encountered classes [15, 24, 29, 35,
45, 76]. While these approaches can isolate newly learned
knowledge from previously acquired representations, continual network expansion increases model complexity and
may limit scalability and consistency across incremental
tasks. Third, regularization-based methods constrain updates to parameters or representations that are important
for previously learned classes, thereby preserving historical
knowledge while adapting the model to new classes [8, 28,
30, 42, 53]. However, existing regularization strategies often
rely on parameter-importance estimation or optimization
mechanisms that are closely coupled with specific model
architectures, which can limit their generalizability across
heterogeneous segmentation backbones. Fourth, knowledgedistillation-based methods transfer knowledge from a previously trained model to the incrementally updated model by
encouraging consistency between their predictions or intermediate representations, thereby reducing forgetting of previously learned classes [3, 12, 38, 54, 62, 65, 68, 75]. However, distillation-based approaches typically require maintaining a previous model and designing appropriate distillation objectives to preserve knowledge across changing
label spaces, which can become challenging when newly
introduced classes and previously learned classes have substantially different distributions or semantic relationships.

2.2. Domain Incremental Learning
DIL focuses on sequentially adapting models to evolving
data distributions, enabling segmentation models to accommodate changes in imaging modalities, acquisition protocols, and institutional characteristics. Similar to CIL, however, DIL is inherently susceptible to catastrophic forgetting,
as adaptation to a newly encountered domain can overwrite
knowledge acquired from previously observed domains.
Early DIL approaches commonly employed exemplar-based
rehearsal, retaining data from previous domains for joint
training with the current domain [74]. To reduce the dependence on historical data, Li et al. proposed recovering information from previously encountered domains using an auxiliary generative model [33]. However, generating realistic
and sufficiently representative medical images solely from a
trained model remains challenging [73] and may introduce
additional privacy risks if sensitive patient information is
inadvertently encoded or reproduced [77]. More recently,
several studies have explored extending DIL toward more
general continual learning settings. Nevertheless, existing
approaches remain constrained by important assumptions
that limit their applicability to medical image segmentation.
Some methods are primarily developed for natural-image
classification and therefore do not directly accommodate
dense, voxel-wise prediction in 3D medical image segmentation [31, 55]. Other approaches are specifically designed for
conventional convolutional architectures and may not readily
generalize to transformer-based segmentation models [19,
42]. In addition, several methods rely on access to previously
observed data during incremental adaptation [18, 19], which
is undesirable in clinical settings because historical patient
data may be inaccessible across institutions or subject to
strict privacy and data-governance constraints. These limitations become particularly pronounced for high-dimensional,
multimodal 3D medical images, where effective continual
adaptation must preserve rich spatial and semantic representations while accommodating substantial distribution shifts.
Normalization-based approaches have also been explored
for domain adaptation by adjusting feature statistics to
reduce distribution discrepancies across domains [51, 49, 4].
However, conventional instance normalization and related
approaches primarily focus on aligning representations toward the current target domain and do not explicitly preserve domain-specific knowledge acquired from previously
encountered domains. Consequently, directly applying such
approaches to DIL may improve adaptation to the current
domain at the expense of historical knowledge. This motivates a continual representation adaptation mechanism that
can not only adapt feature statistics to newly encountered
domains but also retain and recover knowledge associated
with previously learned domains without requiring access
to historical patient data.

result in inconsistent intermediate feature representations.

3.3. Non-Gaussian Distribution Alignment
Although instance normalization and affine transformations regulate the first two moments of feature distributions, medical imaging features may exhibit substantial nonGaussian characteristics, including skewness, heavy tails,
and multi-modality, due to heterogeneous tissue composition, pathological variations, imaging artifacts, and differences in acquisition protocols. Consequently, aligning only
the mean and variance may not sufficiently characterize
distributional discrepancies across domains.
To address this limitation, FGCARL incorporates higherorder moment matching as a non-parametric distribution
alignment mechanism. Rather than imposing a specific
parametric distribution on the learned features, the proposed formulation progressively constrains the statistical
characteristics of the feature distributions through multiple
moments.

representation drift while avoiding direct access to previously collected patient data.

explicit parametric distributional assumption, making it suitable for capturing non-Gaussian characteristics of medical

distributions, whereas Wasserstein alignment constrains the
overall embedding-level discrepancy.

hierarchical adaptation strategy: forward normalization for
representation stability, backward realignment for historical
knowledge retention, higher-order moment matching for
non-Gaussian distribution alignment, and Wasserstein regularization for explicit embedding consistency. This design
enables the model to simultaneously mitigate instance-level
variation, preserve previously learned statistical characteristics, and constrain residual representation drift during
incremental adaptation.

4.2. Implementation details
All experiments were implemented in PyTorch and conducted on a single NVIDIA Tesla A100 PCIe GPU with 40
GB of memory. We employed Swin UNETR as the vision
transformer (ViT)-based backbone and nnU-Net as the convolutional neural network (CNN)-based backbone [21, 26].
Both architectures were evaluated under the same incremental learning settings to assess the architecture-agnostic
capability of FGCARL. The models were optimized using
AdamW with an initial learning rate of 1×10−4 and a weight
decay of 1 × 10−5. We adopted a linear warm-up followed
by cosine annealing learning-rate scheduler, with a warm-up
period of 50 epochs. All models were trained for 300 epochs
with a batch size of 2.
Raw volumetric images were preprocessed to 3D patches
of size 96 × 96 × 96 and standardized using Z-score normalization. During training, patches were augmented using
random flipping, random 90◦rotations, and random intensity
scaling and shifting. Each dataset was divided into training
and test sets at a ratio of 70% ∶30%. Unless otherwise specified, all compared methods used the same data splits, preprocessing procedures, augmentation strategies, and training
settings to ensure a fair comparison.
Segmentation performance was evaluated using the Dice
Similarity Coefficient (DSC), reported as a percentage. For
multi-class segmentation tasks, the DSC was calculated for
each foreground class and then averaged across the target
classes.
4.3. Comparison with state-of-the-art methods
We compared FGCARL with various state-of-the-art
(SOTA) CIL and DIL methods for 3D multi-modal medical image segmentation. The methods include replay-,
regularization-, architectural-, and knowledge-distillationbased approaches, covering both CNN- and ViT-based segmentation architectures. We considered two experimental
stages to separately assess the segmentation capability before incremental adaptation and the ability to simultaneously
accommodate domain and class shifts. In Stage 1, segmentation models were trained independently on the UCSF-BMSR
and BraTS-METS 2023 datasets. These results provided
a reference for the achievable segmentation performance
when no incremental adaptation was implemented. In Stage
2, models were initially trained on UCSF-BMSR to segment
ET using three modalities, including T1w-pre, T1w-post,
and FLAIR, and were subsequently adapted to the BraTSMETS 2023 domain, where they were required to segment
ET, TC, and SNFH using four modalities, including T1wpre, T1w-post, T2w, and FLAIR. Therefore, this setting
simultaneously introduced substantial domain shifts caused
by differences in data distributions and modality configurations and class shifts caused by the introduction of
new segmentation targets. To accommodate the different
numbers of input modalities between the source and target
domains, we additionally constructed a subtraction modality
by computing the difference between T1w-pre and T1w-post
images, thereby maintaining a consistent number of input
channels across the two domains.

4.4. Ablation Study
4.4.1. The effectiveness of MAIR
We first evaluated the effectiveness of the proposed
MAIR for domain-incremental learning under unaligned
modality settings. We compared MAIR with representative
SOTA DIL methods and several normalization-based approaches, including Batch Renormalization and Continuous
Batch Renormalization. We also compared the incorporation
of MAIR with the only forward MAIR. Experiments were
conducted using both nnU-Net and Swin UNETR, with domain adaptation evaluated in both directions: UCSF-BMSR
→BrainMetShare and BrainMetShare →UCSF-BMSR.
This bidirectional evaluation allows us to assess whether
MAIR provides consistent adaptation benefits across different source-target domain configurations.
Comparison with existing DIL methods. MAIR consistently achieved the strongest performance among the evaluated methods across both segmentation architectures and
adaptation directions (Table 2). With Swin UNETR, the proposed Forward & Backward MAIR achieved DSC scores of
77.32% and 68.27% on UCSF-BMSR and BrainMetShare,
respectively, when adapting from UCSF-BMSR to BrainMetShare. In the reverse adaptation setting, it achieved
81.62% and 62.15% DSC on UCSF-BMSR and BrainMetShare, respectively. These results substantially outperformed existing DIL methods, including Lifelong nnUNet,
UNEG, Mdvit, and CFIL. In particular, for UCSF-BMSR
→BrainMetShare, Forward & Backward MAIR improved
the BrainMetShare DSC from 65.11% achieved by Lifelong
nnUNet to 68.27%, while simultaneously retaining substantially higher performance on the previously learned UCSFBMSR domain (77.32%).
Effect of bidirectional MAIR. The ablation results
demonstrate that the the combination of Forward and Backward components is critical to the effectiveness of MAIR.
For nnU-Net, Forward MAIR alone achieved only 50.13%
DSC on UCSF-BMSR after adaptation from UCSF-BMSR
to BrainMetShare, whereas incorporating both Forward and
Backward MAIR increased the DSC to 72.47%. A similar
improvement was observed for the reverse adaptation direction, where the UCSF-BMSR DSC increased from 77.98%
with Forward MAIR alone to 78.96% with Forward &
Backward MAIR, while the BrainMetShare DSC increased
from 47.99%to 56.27%. These results indicate that forward
normalization alone can facilitate feature adaptation to the
incoming domain but is insufficient to preserve the feature
statistics associated with previously learned domains. The
backward alignment mechanism provides the complementary capability of restoring domain-specific feature characteristics, thereby improving the stability of previously
acquired representations during incremental adaptation.

4.4.2. The effectiveness of FGKR
To further evaluate the effectiveness of FGKR under class-incremental learning, we conduct experiments on
BTCV and LiTS using a stage-wise incremental learning
setting, where new classes are introduced sequentially across
domains. All methods achieved identical performance at
Stage 1, providing a consistent starting point for evaluating
their ability to adapt to the subsequent incremental stage
(Table 3).
After introducing the new classes in Stage 2, FGKR
consistently achieved competitive or superior performance
compared with existing class-incremental learning methods. In particular, FGKR combined with Swin UNETR
achieved the best performance on both BTCV and LiTS,
reaching 83.08%and 47.59% DSC, respectively. On BTCV,
this corresponded to a substantial improvement over the
strongest competing baseline, demonstrating the effectiveness of FGKR in preserving and adapting semantic knowledge when new classes are introduced. FGKR also achieved
the best performance on LiTS when paired with either
nnUNet or Swin UNETR, with the latter providing the
strongest overall results.
The consistent improvements across the two datasets
and segmentation architectures demonstrate that the effectiveness of FGKR is not restricted to a particular network or dataset. Notably, the stronger performance obtained
with Swin UNETR suggests that FGKR can effectively
complement different segmentation architectures and facilitate knowledge retention and adaptation during classincremental learning. Overall, these results provide further
evidence that FGKR is robust to sequential class expansion
and domain variation in realistic incremental segmentation
scenarios.

normalization provided by FGCARL can also enhance the
representation learned by transformer-based segmentation
networks even in the absence of domain shifts.
More importantly, FGCARL maintained its effectiveness during continual adaptation from UCSF-BMSR to
BraTS-METS 2023. In Stage 2, FGCARL achieved the highest performance among the evaluated incremental learning
methods on both the source and target domains for both
network architectures. For nnU-Net, FGCARL substantially
improved retention of the previously learned UCSF-BMSR
knowledge while simultaneously achieving the strongest
performance on BraTS-METS 2023. A similar trend was
observed with Swin UNETR, where FGCARL considerably
outperformed the competing incremental learning methods
on both domains. These results demonstrate that the benefits
of FGCARL are not tied to a particular segmentation architecture and that its adaptive normalization and knowledge
retention mechanisms can be effectively combined with both
convolutional and transformer-based networks.
4.8. Parameter sensitivity analysis
We evaluated the sensitivity of segmentation performance, measured by DSC, to the regularization weighting
hyperparameters in the UCSF-BMSR →BraTS-METS 2023
setting. Specifically, we independently varied the weights
of the three regularization components, 𝜆seg, 𝜆dist, and 𝜆FG
(Figure 6 (A)–(C)), respectively.
Overall, the three regularization terms exhibit similar
trends across the evaluated tumor subregions. Increasing
the regularization weight from 𝜆= 0 generally improves
segmentation performance, indicating that each component
contributes to effective continual adaptation and knowledge
retention. Across the evaluated components and tumor subregions, 𝜆= 1.0 provides a favorable balance between
retaining previously learned knowledge and adapting to the
target domain, yielding consistently strong performance on
both the source and target datasets. We therefore set the regularization weight to 𝜆= 1.0 in the remaining experiments.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we presented Fisher-Guided Continuously
Adaptive Representation Learning (FGCARL), a unified
framework for continual 3D medical image segmentation
under evolving domain and class shifts. By integrating Mutual Adaptive Instance Representation (MAIR) for adaptive
domain alignment with Fisher-Guided Knowledge Retention
(FGKR) for selective preservation of previously acquired
knowledge, FGCARL enables continuous adaptation while
mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Extensive experiments
across multiple cross-institutional segmentation tasks and
diverse segmentation architectures demonstrated that FGCARL consistently outperforms existing continual learning
approaches, achieving effective adaptation to newly encountered domains and classes while maintaining knowledge of
previously learned ones. These results highlight the potential
of FGCARL as a unified and generalizable solution for
continual medical image segmentation in dynamic clinical
environments.
