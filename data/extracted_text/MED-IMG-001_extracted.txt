Abstract
Automated segmentation is routinely used as the first step in quantitative image-based
clinical workflows, where predicted masks are subsequently reviewed and corrected by human
operators.
Automated correction of the residual structural errors is currently addressed
one anatomy at a time, so the number of trained correctors scales with the number of
segmentation targets.
We present GRACE, a retrieval-augmented refiner that edits an
input mask under context drawn from a shared knowledge base of verified prior cases.
The refiner is trained once on a multi-organ corpus spanning compact organs, cavities,
branching vessels, and tumour lesions. In the held-out dataset-transfer evaluation, three
held-out datasets query a fixed knowledge base built from training data only. A separate
leave-one-anatomy-out experiment tests parameter-free index extension: verified cases of
an unseen solid organ enter the knowledge base, and the refiner stays frozen. A shapeaware retrieval projector ranks the neighbours. This light projection head sits on a frozen
multimodal encoder and maps embedding similarity to the shape agreement of the masks.
Thus, the knowledge base returns cases that the refiner can use as a shape prior. We evaluate
GRACE on 18 public datasets spanning CT, MRI and ultrasound, including three held-out
CT/MRI datasets for held-out dataset transfer, using nine refinement configurations and
seven upstream segmentors that span weak, mid, and strong operating regimes. We report
each change with the DSC of its input mask. On the weak tier, the input DSC is 0.386 and
GRACE adds up to +0.35 DSC. On a strong segmentor, the input DSC is 0.932 and the
mean change is small. Transfer to an imaging modality that is absent from training and
from the index is partial and depends on the anatomy. We present it as an open research
direction.
Our results suggest retrieval-conditioned refinement is a practical alternative
to anatomy-specific correction models. Open-source code and implementation available at
https://github.com/robustml-eurecom/grace.

Introduction
Medical image segmentation has advanced rapidly with deep learning (Isensee et al., 2021; Hatamizadeh
et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2024), becoming the de facto approach for extracting anatomical structures in clinical
workflows. Although widely adopted, state-of-the-art models remain prone to structural errors and artifacts
that require costly manual correction before clinical use (Azad et al., 2023; Galati et al., 2022). These artifacts
typically manifest as over-segmentation, under-segmentation, boundary imprecision, or topological violations.
Challenging cases involve ambiguous boundaries, small structures, or out-of-distribution pathologies that
deviate from the expected normative anatomy (Galati et al., 2022).
Despite these advances in segmentation accuracy, automated correction of segmentation errors remains an
open problem. Unlike segmentation, which predicts pixel-wise labels from image intensities, correction requires reasoning about anatomical plausibility and restoring coherent structures from imperfect predictions.
Despite growing interest in automated segmentation quality control and refinement (Valindria et al., 2017;
Robinson et al., 2018; Audelan & Delingette, 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Robinson et al., 2019; Larrazabal et al.,
2020; Painchaud et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Fournel et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2023;
Marcianò et al., 2026), the field currently lacks generalisable, fully-automated solutions.
Existing approaches largely fall into two restrictive paradigms. On the one hand, single-anatomy specialists (Larrazabal et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Painchaud et al., 2020) learn to encode anatomical shape
priors, which are then used to validate and improve anatomically implausible segmentations. However, while
effective, these approaches require training a dedicated model from scratch for each new organ or imaging
modality. Another set of techniques interprets refinement as a signal restoration problem, where given a
corrupted mask, the goal is to recover the anatomically plausible structure (Yuan et al., 2020; Terris et al.,
2026). While such models demonstrate strong capabilities in generic signal restoration, they do not explicitly
incorporate structured anatomical priors, which are critical for correcting geometric segmentation errors.
We argue that medical segmentation refinement can be naturally formulated as a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) problem (Lewis et al., 2020). Given a corrupted segmentation, a model retrieves anatomically
similar cases from a knowledge base and conditions the refinement process on this retrieved context. In this
way, anatomy-specific information is provided at inference time rather than being encoded into the model
parameters, enabling a single refiner to operate across organs and imaging modalities represented in the
knowledge base. Although retrieval has become a powerful paradigm for knowledge-intensive learning, its
potential for anatomy-agnostic segmentation refinement remains largely unexplored. To this end, we present
GRACE (Generalist Retrieval-Augmented Correction for Segmentation Errors) as a retrieval-conditioned
segmentation-refinement framework (Fig. 1) that separates anatomy-aware retrieval from the refiner representation. The framework itself does not commit to a particular refinement representation: pixel-space
and latent-space refiners are treated as interchangeable instantiations of a common retrieval interface. Our
contributions are, thus, summarised as follows:
• A retrieval-augmented framework for segmentation refinement whose common interface supports both
pixel-space and latent-space refiners. The retrieval step supplies anatomy-specific information at inference
time, allowing a single anatomy-agnostic refiner to generalise across the organs and imaging modalities
represented in the knowledge base. The framework can use a fixed knowledge base or incorporate verified
support cases without retraining the refiner.
• Exemplar-conditioned refinement. The retrieved mask enters the refiner as a spatial channel and gives the
shape prior for the correction. When we set this channel to zero, the refiner loses 40% of its gain at the
most severe preset (Sec. 4.5). A shape-aware retrieval projector on a frozen multimodal encoder selects
the same-class case. Its contribution is smaller, and we report it as a component ablation (Table 5).
• Three complementary instantiations of the proposed framework spanning deterministic pixel-space refinement, deterministic latent refinement, and latent flow matching. We compare all three across the
upstream operating regimes and on the trade-off between accuracy and runtime.
• Extensive validation across 18 public datasets, multiple imaging modalities, seven upstream segmentors,
and nine refinement configurations, including three held-out datasets for held-out dataset transfer and a
leave-one-modality-out study. We also analyse retrieval, knowledge-base scaling, robustness to a wrong

2
Related Work
Segmentation quality control and refinement.
Segmentation refinement is a distinct problem from
segmentation itself and has been approached along three main lines. The first is classical, hand-designed
post-processing that enforces geometric or topological constraints on the raw prediction: morphological operations and connected-component filtering to remove small false-positive blobs or retain the largest anatomically plausible regions (Isensee et al., 2021; Dulau et al., 2023) and topological penalties ensuring continuous
boundaries (Hu et al., 2019; Li et al., 2025). These methods are fast and interpretable but cannot recover
structure that was never in the raw prediction, and their handcrafted rules do not transfer across anatomies.
The second line replaces handcrafted rules with learned anatomical priors, typically encoded through autoencoders, variational autoencoders, or more recently latent diffusion models (Oktay et al., 2018; Larrazabal
et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Painchaud et al., 2020; Galati & Zuluaga, 2021; Marcianò et al., 2026). These
methods learn a manifold of anatomically plausible segmentations that guides refinement. However, as the
manifold is typically learned for a single anatomy, these methods require retraining for each new organ or
imaging modality. Finally, the third line does not consider a dedicated corrector, approaching the problem
via test-time adaptation (TTA) (Karani et al., 2021). Instead, the upstream segmentor is briefly refitted on
each test volume to close a domain gap. TTA improves segmentation performance without producing a corrected mask directly. Therefore, it is complementary to a refiner rather than a substitute for it. GRACE sits
in the refiner slot, replaces the per-anatomy autoencoder with a single retrieval-conditioned refiner trained
once on multi-organ data, and preserves the conservative behaviour of morphological post-processing on
inputs that do not need correction.
Foundation models in medical imaging.
Recent foundation and generalist models have broadened
anatomical coverage through diverse paradigms, including prompt-based segmentation (Ma et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2025b; Ma et al., 2025; Wong et al., 2024), large-scale supervised learning (Wasserthal et al.,

2023), few-shot segmentation (Butoi et al., 2023), and text-conditioned segmentation (Rokuss et al., 2026).
While these methods demonstrate broad generalisation across anatomies and modalities, they are designed
for forward segmentation prediction rather than post-hoc correction and refinement. RAM (Terris et al.,
2026) extends the foundation-model paradigm to generic inverse imaging problems without task-specific
training, but operates in pixel space and does not explicitly exploit anatomical context for segmentation
correction.
Retrieval-augmented learning in medical imaging.
Retrieval-augmented learning (Lewis et al., 2020)
has recently been explored in medical AI for report generation (Xia et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2024; Hsu
et al., 2025) and few-shot segmentation from retrieved annotated examples (Ahmed et al., 2026; Zhao et al.,
2025). In these settings, retrieval provides external knowledge to improve prediction or support inference.
In contrast, retrieval for direct segmentation refinement has received little attention.
GRACE retrieves
anatomically similar cases to provide inference-time context for correcting an existing segmentation.
Latent-space exploration in medical imaging.
Compact latent representations have become a standard paradigm for modelling anatomically plausible structures in medical imaging. Early formulations use
variational autoencoders (VAEs) as shape priors, learning latent manifolds of plausible organ shapes for
segmentation refinement and shape reconstruction (Painchaud et al., 2020; Larrazabal et al., 2020; Beetz
et al., 2022). Vector-Quantised VAEs (Razavi et al., 2019) and multi-scale volumetric encoders (Pinaya et al.,
2022; Fernandez et al., 2024) extended this framework to high-resolution 3D image and mask generation.
Latent diffusion models (Rombach et al., 2022) and, more recently, rectified flow models (Lipman et al.,
2023) enable efficient generative sampling directly in these compact latent spaces, making full-volume 3D
synthesis computationally tractable (Guo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Tang et al., 2025). Conditioning strategies have subsequently evolved from partial-noise editing, such as SDEdit (Meng et al., 2022), to
retrieval-guided generation, where retrieved exemplars provide structural guidance during sampling (Molino
et al., 2026). GRACE builds on this latter paradigm by performing class-filtered, shape-aware retrieval to
guide segmentation refinement, and supports deterministic pixel-space, deterministic latent-space, and latent
flow-based refiners within a unified framework.

3.2
Anatomy-aware knowledge base
For each verified case, the binary mask is overlaid as a contour on each corresponding slice of the paired
scan, aggregated in a 3D volume as suggested in Sellergren et al. (2025), and the composite is paired with a
structured textual prompt:
You are an expert radiologist.
The highlighted region (red contours) represents:
{class_name}.
Describe its visual characteristics.
The composite is processed by a frozen multimodal encoder P. Unless otherwise stated, we use MedGemma
4B (Sellergren et al., 2025) to obtain raw embeddings ei ∈R2560. The main pipeline maps these embeddings
to 256-dimensional shape-aware vectors using ϕψ (Sec. 3.3); the projected vectors are L2-normalised and
indexed with FAISS (Douze et al., 2026). Raw-space indexing is used only in the corresponding ablations.
FAISS is a similarity-search library.
It keeps the normalised embeddings in a flat inner-product index.
Because the vectors have unit length, the inner-product search returns the cosine nearest neighbours. The
index makes the top-k search fast enough for use at inference. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting embedding
topology via a k-nearest-neighbour connectivity graph and hierarchical clustering, revealing strong intrinsic
modality alignment and an emergent anatomical hierarchy within the embedding space. Extending K with
a verified support case requires only embedding and indexing that case, not retraining the refiner.
Construction protocol.
In the main experiments and the held-out-dataset evaluation, cases enter the
knowledge base only from the training splits listed in Sec. 4.1; validation cases, test cases, and every case
from the three held-out datasets in Sec. 4.3 are excluded. The leave-one-anatomy-out support experiment
(Sec. 4.4) and the optional longitudinal-memory analysis (Appendix G) are explicitly identified extensions of
this fixed-index protocol. Their indexed support cases are disjoint from the corresponding evaluation queries.
Multi-class masks are decomposed into per-class binary instances, and each indexed instance is one entry.
A same-class index is materialised at indexing time, avoiding class filtering over the full database during
inference. No scored evaluation query is indexed as its own retrieval candidate. In the reported configuration,
K contains N=3,913 per-class instances drawn from the training splits of the 15 training datasets (Table 12),
out of the 11,900 non-empty instances of these splits (Appendix F).

3.4
Refiner interface
The retrieved Yr enters Rθ as a spatial conditioning channel that supplies a shape prior, while the sequence
{erj}k
j=1 provides semantic context through cross-attention, whenever supported by the underlying architecture. This interface is compatible with both supervised feedforward networks trained on pixel-wise labels
and conditional generative models that sample from p(Y | Y ∗, X, Yr, {erj}k
j=1). The refiner may operate
directly in pixel space or in a compact latent space obtained through a mask autoencoder. In the latter case,
the retrieved Yr is projected through the same encoder. The latent refinement interface is independent of
the particular autoencoder architecture; throughout this work, the latent variants use the frozen encoder of
the MAISI VAE (Guo et al., 2025) together with a mask-tuned decoder. Any compatible mask autoencoder
can be substituted without modifying the refinement procedure.
These two factors, the refinement paradigm (deterministic or generative) and the representation (pixel or
latent space), can be combined to instantiate different refinement architectures within the same retrieval
framework. In our work, they give rise to three variants: a deterministic pixel-space network (GRACE-U), a
deterministic latent autoencoder (GRACE-M), and a latent flow-matching model (GRACE-M-D). GRACEU is a 3D U-Net with three input channels: the corrupted mask, the image and the retrieved exemplar. It
gives a foreground logit for each voxel in one forward pass. GRACE-M encodes the corrupted mask and the
exemplar into a 4×243 latent with a frozen encoder. A small module fuses the two latents, and a mask-tuned
decoder gives the refined mask. GRACE-M-D uses the same latent interface but integrates a learned velocity
field with an ODE solver. Thus, several runs on one input give a set of plausible refinements. All three
variants use the same retrieval context. Appendix A gives the full architectures.
3.5
Two-phase training and corruption schedule
Training Rθ directly on corrupted masks produced by one specific upstream segmentor risks injection bias:
the refiner overfits to the failure modes of that segmentor. To prevent this bias, we train the refiner on
synthetic corruptions of the ground-truth masks. A corruption combines morphological erosion and dilation,
elastic boundary deformation, boundary noise, false-positive blobs and axis-aligned slice dropout (Jebril
et al., 2025). Three severity presets (default, mid, strong) define the synthetic-corruption experiments; a
fourth training preset (mixed) samples uniformly across the three severities. Two more presets, very_strong
and extreme, go beyond the range of the training severities. We use them only for evaluation, in the analyses
of Sec. 4.5. Representative synthetic corruptions are shown in Fig. 5.
Training has two phases. Phase 1 trains the refiner, and Phase 2 fits the retrieval projector ϕψ of Sec. 3.3.
In Phase 1, we train the refiner from random initialisation for 100 epochs at 963 resolution. We use AdamW
with a learning rate of 10−4, a cosine schedule, batch size 8, gradient clipping at 1.0 and mixed precision.

4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental setup
Datasets.
The training corpus covers 15 public datasets across CT, MRI, and US and contains 3,866
volumes over 38 anatomical structures across the abdomen, thorax, pelvis, brain, and cardiac chambers
(Table 12 in Appendix F). Multi-class labels are converted to per-organ binary instances; for multi-channel
Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD) (Antonelli et al., 2022) tasks the T2-weighted modality is used.
Three datasets are excluded from both training and indexing and are used for held-out dataset-transfer evaluation: ProstateX (Armato III et al., 2018) (346 subjects, multiparametric prostate MRI), CHAOS (Kavur
et al., 2021) (40 CT subjects, liver / kidneys / spleen; CHAOS also has MRI scans of these organs, which
Sec. 4.4 uses), and LUMIERE (Suter et al., 2022) (91 GBM patients across 2–20 longitudinal visits each,
599 timepoints).
Metrics.
We report Dice coefficient (DSC, ↑), 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95, ↓), and signed
deltas over the raw upstream prediction. Unless a caption states otherwise, “±” denotes the sample standard
deviation across cases. Figure 3 instead shows variability across anatomy–dataset pairs, and Table 6 reports
runtime variability across repeated runs. HD95 is reported in millimetres for real-segmentor evaluations and
in voxels for synthetic-corruption analyses.
Upstream segmentors.
Seven segmentors span three operating regimes.
Weak: a MONAI (Cardoso
et al., 2022) 3D U-Net trained for 30 epochs on 10% of each organ split (U-Net@30). Mid: MedSAM2 (Ma
et al., 2025) and ScribblePrompt (Wong et al., 2024) used with an oracle bounding box derived from the
ground truth on the highest-coverage axial slice, and SwinUNETR-BTCV (Hatamizadeh et al., 2022). ScribblePrompt and SwinUNETR-BTCV are used for the held-out dataset evaluation (Sec. 4.3). Strong: TotalSegmentator (Wasserthal et al., 2023) in fast mode on CT organs; a MONAI 3D U-Net trained for 150
epochs on the full split (U-Net@150); a MONAI SegResNet trained for 150 epochs on the full split (SegResNet). This split characterises GRACE when the input carries little usable signal as well as when it is close
to correct.
What the refiner is trained on.
We split each dataset case-wise into disjoint training and test parts.
(i) For Table 1 and the synthetic analyses, one refiner trains on the training split, and we evaluate it on the
test split. It sees other cases of the same datasets, but never the evaluated cases. (ii) For Table 2, we apply
the same refiner to three datasets that are absent from training and from the index. (iii) For Table 3 (Block
A), we retrain the refiner from scratch for each held-out anatomy. That anatomy is absent from training
and from the index. At test time, only support cases enter the index, and they are disjoint from the queries.
(iv) Block B of Table 3 applies the same protocol to a full modality. We rebuild the index for each protocol.
Refiner baselines.
Nine refiner variants are compared: (i) no refinement (raw upstream); (ii) morphological (binary closing then binary opening with a 3×3×3 structuring element); (iii) test-time augmentation
(TTAug) with eight views and mean aggregation; (iv) PostDAE (Larrazabal et al., 2020); (v) RAM (Terris
et al., 2026); (vi) a MONAI 3D U-Net refiner without retrieval (U-Net w/o RAG) that takes (Y ∗, X) as
input; (vii) the same U-Net with the retrieved exemplar as a third input channel (GRACE-U); (viii) a latent autoencoder refiner that fuses the exemplar in the latent bottleneck before decoding (GRACE-M); (ix)
the same latent refiner with flow-matching inference (GRACE-M-D), averaged over three ODE seeds. No
baseline receives the target class as input. PostDAE uses the class implicitly, through one model for each

PostDAE.
PostDAE decreases the DSC on every tier. The largest loss is on TotalSegmentator (−0.487

Comparison of the variants.
The three variants achieve comparable aggregate performance but differ in
their behaviour on severely degraded inputs. Consistent with Table 1, GRACE-M produces the larger mean
lift on the mid and most of strong tiers, while all three variants have small non-negative mean changes on
the strongest segmentor, TotalSegmentator. Mechanistically, GRACE-U is a single-pass feedforward network
and produces more conservative corrections when little reliable signal is available; GRACE-M refines in a
frozen latent space and can synthesise anatomically plausible completions guided by the retrieved exemplar.
GRACE-M-D stays within 0.01 DSC of GRACE-M and needs about seven times more compute (Table 6).
Its value is the uncertainty estimate for each sample, and it does not increase the accuracy.

gives the rest. On the four organs of Table 11, the share is from 39% (pancreas) to 62% (kidneys, spleen).

the exemplar and less from the multimodal retrieval space. We therefore describe GRACE as exemplarconditioned refinement. The refiner uses the retrieved exemplar as a shape prior, and retrieval selects it from

Robustness to a wrong class query.
In a fully automatic pipeline, the class label can be wrong. We
keep the trained refiner and compare three query conditions on four organs at four severities. The conditions
are the correct class, a random wrong class and no exemplar (Table 11). At the extreme preset, the input
DSC is from 0.11 to 0.31. The correct class gives the largest gain on every organ. A wrong class gives 0.077
to 0.151 DSC less. On the compact organs (liver, kidneys, spleen), a wrong-class exemplar still recovers 50 to
72% of the correct-class gain over no exemplar. On the pancreas, which is small and variable, a wrong-class
exemplar is slightly worse than no exemplar. Thus, the robustness to a wrong class depends on the shape of
the target. The stabilisation of refinement under extreme class noise is an open question.

5
Discussion and Conclusions
Discussion.
A central question motivating this work is whether retrieval-conditioned refinement can generalise beyond anatomy-specific correction models. Our experiments indicate that the greatest benefit of
retrieval arises when the upstream segmentation is unreliable, precisely the regime in which manual correction is most costly in clinical practice (Galati et al., 2022), while already accurate predictions receive only
minimal changes. The retrieved exemplar gives 40% of the gain at the most severe preset. The correct class
already gives a strong shape prior, and the shape-aware projector adds a smaller gain in that class.
When is refinement preferable to a strong segmentor?
We see four settings. (i) No strong segmentor
exists for the target. TotalSegmentator covers CT anatomy, but not cardiac chambers in ultrasound or most
lesion classes. (ii) The upstream is a black box, for example a vendor model without access to weights
or training data. GRACE needs only the output mask. (iii) A new anatomy must be supported without
retraining (Sec. 4.4). (iv) Promptable models receive imperfect prompts. Our MedSAM2 and ScribblePrompt
results use an oracle bounding box. With real prompts, their masks are probably worse and closer to the
range where refinement helps. For this reason, we put them in the mid tier. For strong CT segmentors, we
do not claim an improvement. GRACE keeps good masks almost unchanged on average, but it degrades
some of them (Appendix D).

Limitations.
The effectiveness of retrieval depends on the diversity of the knowledge base: anatomies or
pathological presentations that are not represented in K provide weaker retrieval context, and the refiner
then remains closer to the raw upstream prediction. Verified support cases can be added to K without
updating the refiner, but genuinely novel morphologies may still require retraining. At the high end of input
quality, 3.8% of the cases with an input DSC above 0.8 lose more than 0.05 DSC (Appendix D). A mechanism
that skips refinement for a probably correct input is a logical next step. GRACE-M-D needs about seven
times more compute than GRACE-M, and its results change with the ODE seed. Transfer to a held-out
modality is partial on MRI and fails on ultrasound. The leave-one-anatomy-out setting needs 118 to 302
verified masks of the held-out organ. We present both as open research directions.
Conclusions.
We introduced GRACE, a retrieval-conditioned framework that replaces anatomy-specific
correctors with a single refiner guided by an anatomy-aware knowledge base. Across upstream segmentors
and held-out datasets, it improved unreliable predictions while leaving accurate ones largely unchanged, and
verified support cases extend the knowledge base without a parameter update.

Appendix Overview
Appendix A compares the three refiner paradigms (deterministic pixel-space, deterministic latent autoencoder, and latent flow-matching) as instantiations of the refiner interface. It reports the mechanistic reasoning
behind the empirical ordering in Sec. 4.2, as well as the associated computational costs of each variant. Appendix B reports the architectural ablation of the latent RAG refiner behind the design choices in Sec. 3.4.
Appendix C explains the ultrasound result of the leave-one-modality-out experiment. Appendix D gives the
case-level failure analysis. Appendix E gives the retrieval-encoder comparison and the per-organ class-query
results. Appendix F summarises the dataset scope, per-dataset case and class counts, and the aggregate
number of non-empty binary-mask instances. Finally, Appendix G reports an emergent temporal-memory
property of the retrieval pipeline on the LUMIERE dataset.
A
Refiner paradigms: deterministic, latent autoencoder, latent flow-matching
The refiner interface (Sec. 3.4) admits three concrete instantiations. They are evaluated with the same data,
corruption schedule, and retrieval pipeline, but differ in network architecture, refinement representation (pixel
or latent), training objective, and inference procedure (deterministic forward pass or generative sampling).
The first instantiation, GRACE-U, is a supervised 3D U-Net trained on pixel-wise labels that consumes
the corrupted mask, the image, and the retrieved exemplar as channel-concatenated inputs and returns a
per-voxel foreground logit. It uses one forward pass with no latent codec or sampler. Its single-pass design
makes it the fastest variant and empirically produces the more conservative behaviour described in Sec. 4.2.
Consistent with Table 1, GRACE-M produces the larger mean DSC gains on the mid tier and on two of the
three strong segmentors, whereas GRACE-U leads on the weak tier and on U-Net@150; all three variants
have small non-negative mean changes on the strong tier.
The second instantiation, GRACE-M, moves the refinement into the mask latent space. A frozen encoder
maps the corrupted mask and the retrieved exemplar into a compact 4×243 latent. A small fusion module
(concat, FiLM, or cross-attention) combines the two latents, while a mask-tuned decoder returns the refined
mask. Since the decoded output is drawn from the learned mask manifold, this paradigm can produce a
plausible completion even when the input carries almost no signal. The reported latent variants use a frozen
MAISI encoder together with a decoder fine-tuned on the binary-mask distribution (Appendix B).
The third instantiation, GRACE-M-D, replaces the deterministic latent decode with an ODE integration
of a learned velocity field conditioned on the retrieved exemplar, following flow matching (Lipman et al.,
2023) for latent generative models. Inference becomes stochastic in the choice of initial noise, so a batch of
runs on the same input yields a family of plausible refinements; averaging or selecting among them supports
the sample-level uncertainty estimates that neither deterministic paradigm provides.
On the aggregate
benchmark in Table 6, its 30-step aggregate DSC closely matches the deterministic GRACE-M decode
while inference is roughly seven times slower; its practical value therefore lies in downstream workflows that
consume uncertainty rather than in additional aggregate DSC.
These architectural differences are reflected in the empirical behaviour observed across experiments. Table 1
supports regime-wise comparisons for the three variants: GRACE-M has the larger mean lift on the mid and
most of the strong tiers, while all three variants have small non-negative mean changes on the top performing
model (TotalSegmentator). On the aggregate benchmark of Table 6, averaged over three ODE seeds, the
30-step GRACE-M-D stays within 0.01 DSC of GRACE-M.

B
Architectural ablations of the latent RAG refiner
The refiner interface of Sec. 3.4 admits several concrete instantiations. This appendix reports the empirical
characterisation that motivates the choices adopted in the main paper. Three design axes are examined in
controlled comparisons: (i) how the retrieved exemplar Yr enters the refiner (fusion module); (ii) whether
inference uses the deterministic GRACE-M decode or the GRACE-M-D flow-matching trajectory; and (iii)
the training loss, exemplar selection, and velocity-network width for GRACE-M-D. Settings outside the axis
under study are held fixed within each comparison. The same fusion-module ablation is repeated on the
pixel-space variant GRACE-U for completeness.
Fusion module for RAG.
Three interfaces for injecting the retrieved exemplar into the refiner are
compared. The concat module stacks the encoded corrupted latent and the encoded exemplar latent along
the channel axis and applies a 1×1×1 convolution with identity initialisation so the refiner recovers the
corrupted-only forward pass at step zero. The FiLM module pools the encoded exemplar over the spatial
dimensions, passes it through a small MLP that emits per-channel scale and shift, and applies them to the
corrupted latent. The cross-attention module is a single-head projected attention layer with the corrupted
latent tokens as queries and the encoded exemplar tokens as keys and values. The pixel-space ablation
applies the corresponding conditioning operations to U-Net feature maps. Table 7 reports refined DSC for
both refiner variants. Concat gives the highest score for each variant and is retained as the default for its
accuracy, parameter economy, and interpretability. Additionally, Table 8 reports controlled combinations of
training changes for GRACE-M-D: the auxiliary Dice loss and the K-NN exemplar each improve the common
baseline, their combination improves it further, and widening the velocity network produces the highest score
at roughly twice the parameter count.
Decoder handling.
All reported latent variants use the frozen MAISI encoder with a decoder fine-tuned
on the binary-mask distribution; no frozen-decoder comparison is included in the reported ablations.
Inference procedure.
On the aggregate benchmark in Table 6, the 30-step flow-matching procedure
reaches 0.810 DSC versus 0.815 for the deterministic GRACE-M latent decode and is about seven times
slower. The deterministic procedure is therefore used for the reported GRACE-M numbers.

Retrieval encoder choice
Retrieval encoder.
Table 10 evaluates the impact of the retrieval encoder while keeping the refiner and
the knowledge-base size fixed. We compare two families of retrieval embeddings: four pretrained foundation
encoders (DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024), BiomedCLIP (Zhang et al., 2025), MedSigLIP (Sellergren et al.,
2025), and MedGemma) and three mask-domain variants derived from a frozen MAISI (Guo et al., 2025) VAE
encoder. The three mask-domain variants use (i) pooled latent features directly, (ii) pooled features followed
by PCA reduction, and (iii) a learned two-layer self-projector trained on the same latent representation.
Among foundation encoders, MedGemma achieves the best performance, followed closely by the remaining
medical encoders, whereas DINOv2 consistently trails them. The strongest mask-domain variant closely
approaches MedGemma despite relying solely on anatomical shape information, suggesting that shape alone
carries a strong retrieval signal.

Figure 6: Emergent patient-specific retrieval (LUMIERE). Each row corresponds to one visit as prior
scans are added to K. Left: UMAP of training-pool embeddings with three patient visits (⋆). Right: top-3
retrieved neighbours (gold rings) and similarity scores.
An emergent behaviour of the retrieval pipeline appears on the longitudinal LUMIERE dataset when the
temporal-memory workflow is enabled: as a patient’s visits are embedded into K, later refinements retrieve
the patient’s own prior scans instead of cross-patient atlases (Fig. 6). At the first visit, retrieved neighbours
are tumour examples from other patients in the training pool; once the patient’s first scan is indexed, it
becomes the highest-ranked retrieval at the next visit; by the third visit, prior visits dominate the top-k
list. No temporal supervision is used: retrieval is driven by similarity in the embedding space. This optional
workflow is separate from Table 2, whose held-out-dataset protocol uses the fixed training-only index and
therefore excludes every LUMIERE case from the candidate pool. On the subset of LUMIERE with at least
one prior visit (n=508 timepoints), enabling same-patient retrieval yields an additional DSC lift of +0.021 on
the ScribblePrompt upstream, on top of the fixed-index score of the corresponding row of Table 2; whether
this lift is admissible depends on the clinical workflow (a longitudinal QC pass at visit t that already trusts
the visit-(t−1) mask, versus a per-visit review pipeline that does not) and is reported separately rather
than folded into the headline number. The mechanism acts as an implicit temporal memory and supports
longitudinal workflows without dedicated temporal architectures.
