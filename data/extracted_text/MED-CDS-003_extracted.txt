RADIOLOGY GUIDELINE-DERIVED DECISION GRAPH FOR
EFFICIENT CLINICAL DECISION SUPPORT
Xuewen Zhou, Zhen Chen†
Department of Data Science and Artificial Intelligence, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University
ABSTRACT
Radiological risk stratification and management planning are
essential downstream tasks in medical imaging. Automating
these tasks is challenging because clinical guideline criteria are distributed across prose, tables, and cross-referenced
definitions.
We present Guideline-derived Decision Graph
(GDGraph), a framework that decouples guideline compilation from case evaluation. During compilation, a large language model partitions guidelines into section-based chunks,
extracts local decision subgraphs with linked definitions,
and aggregates related branches into an executable decision
graph. During inference, a fast System One model answers
localized clinical questions while deterministic traversal resolves Boolean logic, turning opaque single-pass predictions
into a traceable decision path without task-specific training.
Across 347 cases spanning renal cyst, adnexal mass,
and thyroid nodule guidelines, GDGraph improves System
One accuracy from 57.3% to 90.8%, outperforming retrievalaugmented GPT-4o (80.1%) and graph-guided GPT-4o with
chain-of-thought (82.7%) at substantially lower latency and
cost. The source code will be available.
Index Terms— Clinical guidelines, decision graphs, radiological decision support, System One models
1. INTRODUCTION
Risk stratification and management planning are essential
downstream tasks in medical imaging that connect radiological findings to clinical care [1]. Clinical guidelines standardize these decisions across tasks such as renal cyst management [2], adnexal mass assessment [3], and thyroid nodule
stratification [4], where final recommendations follow from
explicit, discrete decision criteria. Automating these tasks
therefore requires systematically following the interdependent decision steps specified in each guideline, progressing
from case-specific findings through successive criteria to a
final category or recommendation.
Pretrained language models can process free-text clinical reports and imaging findings directly through prompting
without task-specific training [5, 6], yet have been shown to
frequently deviate from established diagnostic guidelines [7].
†Corresponding author.
Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting helps these models with
multi-step clinical problems by eliciting reasoning steps in
text before a conclusion [8]. In guideline-based risk stratification and management, clinical criteria and conditional rules
also provide a structured basis for decomposing the overall
task into a series of localized decisions, such as verifying a
specific imaging feature or comparing a measurement against
a threshold. System One models such as Jev [9] offer a suitable interface for these localized decisions by directly scoring discrete candidate options without generating intermediate text . Thus, the primary challenge is to organize these local
judgments into an explicit decision process that preserves the
guideline’s conditional dependencies and yields a traceable
outcome for high-stakes decisions [10].
Existing clinical frameworks incorporate guideline knowledge in different ways. For a given patient case, retrievalaugmented generation (RAG) systems [11] such as accGPT
[12] retrieve relevant medical passages for the language
model to interpret during response generation. CPGPrompt
[13] converts segmented guideline text into decision trees
for stepwise evaluation, with its reported workflow relying
on manual selection of relevant text sections and manual
validation of the generated trees. Such decision structures
could harness the capabilities and efficiency of System One
language models to support practical applications in clinical decision-making.
However, automatic construction of
these structures from raw guideline documents remains challenging because it requires integrating tabulated criteria and
thresholds with terminology definitions and exception rules
distributed across the document.
To address this, we present Guideline-derived Decision
Graph (GDGraph), an automated framework that compiles
raw clinical guidelines into executable decision graphs for
fast, traceable System One inference (Fig. 1).
By decoupling offline LLM-based guideline structuring from online
deterministic traversal, GDGraph reduces multi-step classification to localized node-level judgments while recording an
explicit decision trace. Across 347 radiology cases spanning
three guidelines, GDGraph raises System One accuracy from
57.3% to 90.8%, outperforming unstructured RAG (80.1%)
and graph-guided GPT-4o with CoT (82.7%) at substantially
lower latency and cost. These results suggest the practical
value of GDGraph for clinical decision support.

2. METHODOLOGY
2.1. Overview
Fig. 1 illustrates the proposed Guideline-derived Decision
Graph (GDGraph) framework, which decouples offline guideline compilation from online case evaluation. In the offline
stage (Fig. 1(a); Algorithm 1), given a clinical practice guideline D containing narrative text, figures, and tables, an LLMbased pipeline partitions D into m section-based chunks
{Ci}m
i=1, extracts local subgraphs {Gi}m
i=1 grounded in a
global definition memory M via Term Frequency–Inverse
Document Frequency (TF-IDF) retrieval [14, 15], and aggregates related branches across sections into a unified decision
graph G = (S, A, E, Y).
In the online stage (Fig. 1(b)),
given the imaging findings of a clinical case X, a fast System One model answers localized questions at active nodes
using attached definitions, while deterministic traversal resolves Boolean transitions along the selected path to reach a
traceable category or management decision.
2.2. Guideline-derived Decision Graph
Graph Representation. The decision graph G = (S, A, E, Y)
comprises clinical states S (clinical contexts or intermediate
conclusions, with entry states S0 ⊂S), atomic conditions
A, directed transition rules E, and candidate outcomes Y (diagnostic categories or management actions). Each state and
outcome carries a clinical label, a source-derived definition,
and supporting text spans from D. Each atomic condition
a ∈A represents a single traceable criterion, written as
a = (τa, κa, Da), where τa specifies the clinical feature and
its anatomical or temporal scope (e.g., lesion diameter), κa
denotes the comparison operator and value (e.g., < 10 cm or
presence/absence), and Da links to the corresponding definitions in M. Deterministic templates render each a and its
referenced definitions Da into a self-contained question for
model evaluation.
Directed rules e ∈E take the form e = (se, ϕe, Te, πe)
and connect source states to targets through Boolean combinations of atomic conditions. Here, se ∈S is the prerequisite
state, ϕe is a logical expression over A using standard operators (∧, ∨, ¬, and AtLeastk), Te ⊆S ∪Y denotes the target
states or outcomes, and πe indicates whether the rule directly
determines, supports, or rules out Te. For instance, requiring both imaging pattern P and diameter below 10 cm for the
same lesion is formulated as ϕe = aP ∧a<10.
Section-Based Chunking and Local Subgraph Generation. We split D by document sections into chunks C =
{C1, . . . , Cm} while keeping tables and footnotes intact. An
LLM scans all chunks to build a global definition memory M
of clinical terms, aliases, and definitions. For each chunk Ci,
we retrieve relevant definitions di ⊂M via TF-IDF similarity while keeping explicitly cited definitions, and prompt the
LLM to extract states Si, atomic conditions Ai, and rules Ei
into a local subgraph Gi. Named imaging patterns are kept
as single conditions linked to M, additional criteria such as
numerical thresholds are separated into individual conditions,
and negative findings are denoted by logical negation (¬).

overlapping sets B = {B1, . . . , BL}, processed sequentially
on the latest graph. Within each group Bl, the LLM uses the
source text and definitions dl to merge nodes that share identical clinical meaning and judgment criteria, while keeping

2.3. Graph-Guided Selection
Prior to evaluation, an LLM maps source-supported conclusions in S ∪Y, including intermediate states and terminal outcomes, to the target task’s standardized answer options, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). During online inference, traversal starts
at S0. At each active state, the System One model answers the
questions of outgoing atomic conditions based on the reported
imaging findings and attached definitions Da, while program
logic evaluates ϕe to advance along satisfied branches. The
final prediction is derived from the task-relevant conclusions
reached during traversal, with the clinical case interpreted
against static option definitions.
The traversal log records
each evaluated question and its answer, preserving the traversed edges as a traceable decision path.

3.3. System One Performance and Inference Efficiency
We next evaluate whether pairing GDGraph with the System
One model Jev improves decision accuracy (Table 3) and
compare its online inference efficiency against the GPT-4o
baselines (Fig. 2). Given only the case content, the candidate
options, and their definitions (Direct Jev), Jev reaches 57.3%
overall accuracy (35.0% on K-TIRADS). When the same
cases are evaluated using GDGraph (GDGraph+Jev), performance improves across all three tasks, reaching 90.8% overall
accuracy and achieving the highest Acc., WF1, and MCC in
each guideline. Additionally, GDGraph+Jev achieves higher
accuracy than both GDGraph+GPT-4o CoT (82.7%) and
RAG+GPT-4o CoT (80.1%), while requiring 0.79–1.62 s per
case in online API time (vs. 6.16–7.15 s for RAG+GPT-4o
CoT and 17.10–34.97 s for GDGraph+GPT-4o CoT) and
0.56–1.07 × 10−3 USD per case in API cost (Fig. 2). These
results indicate that organizing guidelines into localized node
judgments and explicit rules enables the System One model
to resolve multi-condition criteria with low online inference
latency and cost.
3.4. Error Analysis
Among the remaining errors, we identify two recurring patterns that highlight the main limitations of GDGraph-guided
inference. In 11 K-TIRADS cases, “ill-defined margins” or
“irregular shape” were interpreted as “irregular margins,” activating a higher-risk branch because node definitions did not
explicitly contrast these terms. In three O-RADS cases, errors arose when mapping qualitative vascularity descriptions
(e.g., moderate flow) to node-level criteria. These observations highlight the importance of normalizing radiological descriptions and aligning imaging concepts with model representations across varied reports.
4. CONCLUSION
We proposed GDGraph, a framework that converts radiology
guidelines into executable decision graphs for risk stratification and management planning. During compilation, an LLM
constructs the graph through section-based chunking, local
subgraph extraction, and cross-branch aggregation. During
inference, the System One model answers localized questions
along the graph, yielding a traceable decision path. Experiments on three guidelines show that GDGraph+Jev achieves
higher accuracy than CoT-based GPT-4o baselines at lower
latency and cost, indicating its practical value for auditable
clinical decision support.
