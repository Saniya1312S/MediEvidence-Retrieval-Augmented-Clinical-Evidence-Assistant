Abstract. 
Background: During transitions from hospital to home, older adults are frequently affected by fragmented 
communication, limited coordination between levels of care, and insufficient post-discharge follow-up. Digital 
technologies, including remote patient monitoring (RPM), digital communication, and artificial intelligence (AI), offer 
opportunities to improve continuity of care. However, their integration needs to reflect the organizational and clinical 
contexts of different healthcare systems. This study presents the development and early implementation of digitally 
supported transitional care pathways within the TransCare project. 
Methods: Care pathways were analyzed and redesigned across three pilot sites in Italy, Romania, and Norway. 
Existing clinical and organizational processes were mapped to identify challenges and opportunities for 
improvement. Technology-augmented pathway matrices were developed to integrate RPM, digital communication 
tools, and AI-supported analytics across the stages of assessment, hospitalization, discharge, and post-discharge 
follow-up. The pathways were iteratively refined through a co-design process involving healthcare professionals, 
patients, caregivers, and technical partners. Early implementation experiences were subsequently analyzed across 
the three sites. 
Results: Common challenges included fragmented communication and poor care coordination, particularly during 
discharge and post-discharge follow-up. However, the underlying cause and required solutions differed across the 
pilot sites, reflecting variations in patient profiles, healthcare organizations, available resources, and digital maturity. 
The redesigned pathways improved role clarity, strengthened discharge planning, and enabled more systematic 
follow-up. Early implementation experience also highlighted the importance of patient and caregiver preparation, 
professional training, organizational readiness, and adaptable digital solutions. 
Conclusion: Digitally supported transitional care requires the integration of technology with organizational and 
clinical process redesign. Future work will focus on testing the redesigned pathways in real-world pilot settings and 
evaluating their feasibility and impact on transitional care outcomes and patient experience. 
Keywords: Care Pathways, Transitional Care, Rehospitalization rate, Artificial Intelligence, Remote patient 
monitoring. 
1. Introduction 
Population ageing is placing sustained pressure on European healthcare systems, particularly at the point where 
patients transition from hospital to home [1]. This transition, commonly referred to as transitional care, is a well-

--- PAGE 2 [pdf_text] ---
2 
 
documented weak point in the care continuum, especially for frail older adults with chronic, multimorbid conditions 
[2]. At the same time older adults’ hospital readmissions are a large-scale, cross-cutting problem rather than one 
associated with a specific disease [3]. Longitudinal evidence indicates that roughly one in five older adults receiving 
home healthcare services is readmitted within 30 days of discharge. The risk of readmission varies with the reason 
for the original hospitalization, with infections and other acute conditions among older populations [4]. Moreover, 
avoidable readmissions vary widely, depending on the setting and methodology used, underscoring that the causes 
are heterogeneous rather than tied to any single pathology. These findings suggest a broader structural problem 
rather than isolated clinical failures, including: discontinuity of information, fragmented responsibility between 
hospital and community-based providers, and insufficient post-discharge follow-up mechanisms. 
The role of digitally supported transitional care and telemedicine in mitigating readmissions has received increasing 
attention in recent years [5]. Modern remote patient monitoring (RPM) systems can integrate AI-based analytics to 
support proactive, data-driven transitional care. By detecting early signs of clinical deterioration from remotely 
monitored patient data, these systems can shift follow-up from reactive to anticipatory intervention [6]. AI tools in 
transitional care also aim to enhance transitions from hospital to home by improving discharge planning, follow-up 
care, and interoperability. Care continuity and complexity management are among the most frequently targeted 
areas, while patient and caregiver engagement remain comparatively underserved [7]. Evidence from RPM and 
telemonitoring trials further suggests that digitally enabled follow-up can reduce avoidable hospitalizations when 
appropriately integrated into clinical workflows [8]. However, technology adoption alone is insufficient without 
accompanying redesign of roles, heterogeneous care pathways, communication channels, and reimbursement 
structures. At the same time, research has largely focused on evaluating individual technologies in isolation or 
proposing generic frameworks that have not been validated through comparative, multi-country implementation. 
This gap in translating pathway-level redesign methodologies into pilot-validated, context-adapted implementations 
motivates the present work. 
This redesign process, however, presents several important challenges. First, technology integration must be adapted 
to the digital maturity, infrastructure, and organizational context of each care setting. A single standardized pathway 
cannot simply be transferred across healthcare systems with different levels of resourcing, interoperability, and 
regulatory constraints [9]. Second, successful redesign depends on clearly defined roles and responsibilities among 
the many actors involved, including patients, family caregivers, general practitioners (GPs), hospital teams, and 
community or municipal services. Ambiguity at key handover points, particularly during discharge, is where 
continuity of care most often breaks down [10]. Third, adequate training and preparation of both patients and 
healthcare professionals before discharge are essential. The benefits of RPM and digital communication tools depend 
heavily on user engagement and correct use, particularly among frail older adults and individuals with cognitive 
impairment [11]. Fourth, redesigned pathways must be validated through iterative real-world piloting rather than 
assumed to be effective at the design stage. Early implementation frequently reveals discrepancies between intended 
workflows and actual clinical practice, highlighting the need for continuous refinement [12]. Finally, sustainability 
and scalability remain important concerns. Financial models, data protection requirements, and interoperability 
between digital platforms and healthcare organizations must be addressed before technology-enabled pathways can 
move beyond pilot-stage validation and into routine practice [13]. 
Simply layering digital tools onto existing transitional care processes is insufficient to improve continuity of care [14]. 
Technology alone does not resolve coordination challenges; rather, it acts as an enabler within a well-structured care 
pathway. Redesigning care pathways to explicitly integrate RPM, AI-based decision support, and digital 
communication platforms is therefore a necessary step. Such a redesign enables care teams to move from reactive, 
episodic contact to proactive, data-driven follow-up. It embeds patient screening and technology onboarding before

discharge, continuous monitoring and alert-based interventions after discharge, and scheduled follow-up at defined 
intervals directly into the care pathway rather than treating these activities as add-ons. 
TransCare (New care pathways for supporting transitional care from hospitals to home using AI and personalized 
digital assistance) is an EU-funded project under the Transforming Health and Care Systems (THCS) programme [15]. 
The project brings together a consortium of clinical, technological, and municipal partners across three countries: 
Italy, Romania, and Norway. Its overarching goal is to design, implement, and clinically validate a technology-assisted 
transitional care model that integrates RPM, Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based decision support, and digital 
communication platforms into redesigned, patient-centered care pathways. The model aims to improve continuity 
of care, reduce hospital readmissions, and ease the burden on healthcare providers. The pilot sites are comprised of 
northern, eastern, and southern EU and cover the entire care continuum. Istituto di Ricovero e Cura a Carattere 
Scientifico IRCCS-INRCA in Italy is a geriatric clinical and research hospital, and it is the leader of Italian Ageing 
Network. They will play an important role in transitional care by providing support and services to individuals who 
are transitioning from one healthcare setting to another, bridging the gap between acute care and long-term care or 
home-based care. The Heart Institute Niculae Stăncioiu (HINS) is one of the most important tertiary cardiology 
centers in Romania. This pilot will play a key role in conducting clinical trials by identifying suitable candidates from 
their inpatient population who meet the defined eligibility criteria and evaluating and refining transitional care 
practices for cardiovascular diseases. Farsund municipality in Norway (FAR) is a community-based care organization 
supporting rehabilitation and independent living. The objective is to support the transition of these patients to 
receive care at home by implementing specific municipality health services using the technology developed in the 
project. 
The main contributions of this paper are: 
• 
to identify key challenges in transitional care and analyse existing care pathways across the three TransCare 
pilot countries; 
• 
to re-design technology-augmented care pathway matrices that demonstrate how RPM, AI, and digital 
communication can be integrated into each phase of the patient journey, from assessment and admission 
through hospital stay, discharge, and post-discharge follow-up, while preserving the distinct clinical and 
organizational context of different countries.; 
• 
to discuss early findings and derive cross-site lessons learned, providing transferable insights for the design 
of technology-enabled transitional care pathways in European healthcare systems. 
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 presents the methods used for transforming traditional 
care pathways into technology enabled Pathways. Section 3 presents the redesigned care pathways. Section 4 
discusses the findings and lessons learned. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper. 
2. Methods 
2.1. National challenges in transitional care 
It is well recognized that transitions from hospital to municipal care is challenging due to communication gaps 
between healthcare providers, inadequate discharge planning, resource limitations in community settings, and the 
need for effective interprofessional collaboration. Additionally, patients and their families often feel unprepared for 
the transition, leading to anxiety and stress. Addressing these challenges requires health and social care providers 
to work together, with shared focus on effective discharge planning and smoother care transitions. Within the 
TransCare project, the three pilot countries (Italy, Romania and Norway) conducted interviews with patients, GPs, 
hospital staff, and municipality healthcare professionals to identify local challenges in transitional care. Workshops

4 
 
were also conducted to obtain an interdisciplinary perspective on transitional care. The main challenges identified 
across the three pilot countries are presented below. 
In Italy, the main challenges in transitional care identified within the TransCare project include regional disparities, 
lack of standardized discharge protocols, financial constraints, and limited integration between hospital and 
community services [16-18]. Regional differences in the provision of community care services remain substantial. 
While some regions have invested heavily in community-based services and are close to meeting national targets, 
others are still in the early stages of development. In addition, funding allocated through the National Recovery and 
Resilience Plan (PNRR) was established before the recent period of inflation and primarily covers capital 
expenditures, with limited provision for increased costs related to personnel, medical devices, and medications. 
Difficulties in recruiting healthcare professionals represent an additional challenge. These difficulties are linked to 
financial constraints and to resistance among some GPs, who are concerned that a shift toward community-based 
care may reduce their professional autonomy. As a result, integration between hospital and community services 
remains limited. This challenge is further compounded by the need to modernize digital infrastructure and accelerate 
the digital transition. Digitalization must also address the needs of an ageing population, many of whom may be 
unfamiliar with or reluctant to use digital services. Furthermore, the discharge of older adults with complex and often 
chronic medical conditions can make effective management at home particularly challenging. 
In Romania, the healthcare system faces significant challenges in transitional care. Most patients are discharged to 
home following treatment in tertiary care centers. In some cases, patients do not have close relatives and are unable 
to care for themselves independently, requiring healthcare professionals to involve social services. According to 
current legislation, people with severe disabilities may receive social support services upon request or through an ex 
officio decision, including access to a caregiver or personal assistant. The role may be assumed by a family member, 
who receives financial compensation for providing care. However, there is a severe shortage of social workers funded 
through the public healthcare and social support system, limiting access to these services. According to Eurostat, the 
Romanian social assistance system is among the least effective in the European Union [19]. In this context, digital 
health solutions may offer support, but they require a level of digital competence that many patients do not possess. 
Communication and information sharing between healthcare providers remain limited. In many cases, medical 
information is not exchanged effectively between care providers, and the management and transfer of medical 
documentation continue to represent significant challenges within the Romanian healthcare system. Although the 
GP is theoretically the healthcare professional with the most complete overview of the patient's medical history, this 
role is not always fully utilized. Greater involvement of GPs in transitional care could strengthen coordination 
between specialist care, social services, and the patient's family. 
Regional disparities present an additional challenge, as access to healthcare services remains limited in many rural 
areas. GPs, particularly in these regions, are often overburdened, manage long patient waiting lists, and have limited 
capacity to engage in additional aspects of patient care and follow-up [20-21]. 
In Norway, one of the main challenges in transitional care is poor coordination between municipal healthcare services 
and hospitals. This disconnect often leads to fragmented care and unclear responsibilities for patient follow-up after 
discharge. Older adults are particularly affected by these coordination challenges. The National Health and Hospital 
Plan has sought to address this issue through the establishment of health communities aimed at strengthening 
cooperation between hospitals and municipal services [22]. Another pressing challenge is the shortage of healthcare 
professionals, particularly within municipal health services. A lack of trained personnel makes it difficult to provide 
continuous and effective care transitions, including patient transfers and post-discharge follow-up. According to the 
National Health and Hospital Plan, workforce shortages reduce the system's ability to meet patient needs and 
increase pressure on existing staff, potentially affecting the quality of care. The absence of standardized procedures 
further contributes to inconsistent care during transitions. Municipalities and hospitals often rely on local routines

and practices, creating communication gaps and delays in treatment. As a result, the lack of uniform care pathways 
remains a significant barrier to effective transitional care and highlights the need for improved integration across 
healthcare services [23]. Patient and family involvement represents another important challenge. Many patients and 
their families feel excluded from decision-making during care transitions, leaving them insufficiently prepared to 
manage care after discharge. This may contribute to avoidable readmissions and poorer health outcomes. Involving 
next of kin as active partners in the transition process can improve both communication and quality of care [24]. The 
integration of technology into transitional care also remains limited. Despite the potential of digital solutions to 
improve communication and coordination across care levels, many healthcare providers continue to rely on 
fragmented information systems, resulting in insufficient information sharing. Follow-up after hospital discharge is 
often inadequate, increasing the risk of complications, readmissions, and patient dissatisfaction, particularly among 
individuals with chronic illnesses or complex care needs. Consequently, insufficient follow-up remains a systemic 
challenge, largely driven by poor coordination between primary and specialist healthcare services. 
Despite their contextual differences, all three sites converge on the same underlying problem: transitional care 
pathways that were never explicitly designed to support structured information exchange, clearly defined 
responsibilities, and technology-enabled follow-up across the hospital-to-home continuum. Therefore, ensuring 
continuity of care requires multidimensional assessment, effective communication between hospital and community 
services, standardized care processes, and systematic sharing of patient information. 
2.2. Analytical framework 
The methodology for redesigning transitional care pathways in TransCare is based on a structured, evidenceinformed framework that combines qualitative insights with process mapping (see Figure 1). The analytical 
framework comprises three interrelated activities: 
• 
Mapping current practices through care pathway matrices that visualize the patient journey from hospital 
to home. 
• 
Identification of systemic and local challenges using stakeholder interviews and interdisciplinary workshops. 
• 
Iterative co-design to develop improved pathways that integrate digital tools and reflect real-world 
constraints and opportunities. 
This framework ensures that the redesigned pathways are both context-sensitive and aligned with the technological 
capabilities developed in TransCare. The redesign process is deeply participatory, involving a wide range of 
stakeholders across all pilot sites: 
• 
Patients and caregivers were interviewed to understand their experiences, needs, and expectations during 
care transitions. 
• 
GPs, hospital clinicians, and municipal care providers contributed insights into current workflows, 
coordination challenges, and opportunities for improvement. 
• 
Multidisciplinary teams participated in workshops to collaboratively map existing pathways and identify 
opportunities for improvement. 
• 
Technology partners and researchers supported the integration of digital solutions and ensured 
methodological rigor.

This co-design approach promotes ownership, relevance, and feasibility of the redesigned pathways, ensuring that 
they are tailored to the specific context of each healthcare setting. Several tools and techniques were employed to 
support the mapping, analysis, and redesign of care pathways: 
• 
Care Pathway Matrices: Originally developed by PA Consulting for the Norwegian welfare technology 
program [25] these matrices were adapted for each pilot site to visualize care process, stakeholder roles, 
and information flows. They served as diagnostic tools to identify inefficiencies and fragmentation and 
opportunities for improvement. 
• 
KS Modell: A structured framework [26] used to guide patient pathway planning, emphasizing assessment, 
data sharing, patient engagement, and follow-up. The framework was adapted to reflect the specific 
organizational and healthcare context for each country. 
• 
Mural Tool: A digital visualization platform [27] used to collaboratively map patient journeys and identify 
challenges within the care process. The plattform supported stakeholder engagement and facilitated the 
visualization of complex care transitions. 
• 
Workshops and Interviews: Conducted across all sites to gather qualitative data and validate findings. These 
activities ensured that the redesign process was informed by the experiences and perspectives of those 
directly involved in delivering and receiving care. 
• 
 Technology integration: The methodology is closely aligned with the digital infrastructure developed in 
TransCare [15], ensuring that redesigned pathways are compatible with RPM, AI-based analytics, and digital 
communication tools. 
This multi-method approach ensures that the redesigned pathways are not only clinically sound, but also 
operationally feasible and technologically integrated. 
2.3. Design of the care pathway matrices 
The initial care pathway matrices were developed using principles derived from the KS Modell and adapted for the 
TransCare context to systematically examine current transitional care practices, with particular emphasis on 
communication and discharge planning. The matrices map the patient journey from home to hospital and back to 
the community, highlighting critical transition points where coordination between hospitals and municipal services 
is essential. They also identify common challenges, including delayed or missing notifications to municipalities,

7 
 
unclear or inconsistent documentation, and insufficient collaboration during discharge planning. By visualizing these 
processes and challenges, the matrices serve as a tool for identifying gaps and improving continuity and quality of 
care across healthcare settings. 
Figure 2 illustrates the current patient journey at INRCA across four main phases: (1) assessment and clarification, 
(2) hospital admission, (3) hospital stay, and (4) discharge. During the assessment phase, patients seek medical 
attention following an illness or deterioration in their condition. Family members are often involved in supporting 
communication with healthcare professionals and facilitating access to care. GPs assess the patient and may refer 
them to specialist services, while home healthcare services report emergencies when required. During hospital 
admission, patients undergo admission procedures and a comprehensive geriatric assessment. Family members 
contribute relevant clinical and medication information, while home healthcare services suspend ongoing services 
and update patient records as appropriate. The hospital stay phase involves comprehensive geriatric assessment, 
monitoring of nutritional and cognitive status, and rehabilitation when necessary. Care is coordinated through 
collaboration among physicians, nurses, and rehabilitation professionals, with ongoing communication regarding the 
patient's progress and care plan. At discharge, the hospital develops a therapeutic plan, prepares discharge 
documentation, and coordinates follow-up services. Home healthcare services may resume care following discharge, 
including assessments and ongoing monitoring. Throughout the pathway, family members play an important role in 
supporting communication, coordinating care, and facilitating the transition from hospital to home, particularly for 
patients with reduced functional independence. Overall, the pathway demonstrates the complexity of transitional 
care and the need for effective communication, coordination, and information sharing among multiple stakeholders 
to ensure continuity of care.

the importance of coordination between primary care, specialist cardiology services, and hospital-based care to 
support continuity of care and effective follow-up after discharge.

2.4. Technology integration 
The TransCare platform integrates a range of digital technologies designed to support older adults during the 
transition from hospital to home. These technologies aim to enhance patient self-management, strengthen 
communication across care settings, and support continuity of care while reducing the risk of hospital readmission. 
 
Figure 5: Overview of the digital care management platform. 
 The Digital Care Management Platform (TelluCare, see Figure 5) serves as the central hub for data aggregation, 
visualization, and communication. It supports HL7 FHIR standards for interoperability and includes secure APIs for 
integration with hospital systems and municipal healthcare services. 
• 
Remote Patient Monitoring (RPM): Patients are equipped with CE-certified medical devices, including smart 
scales, blood pressure monitors, glucometers, pulse oximeters, and thermometers. These devices 
automatically transmit health data via Bluetooth to the platform. 
• 
Mobile Application (Dialogg): Patients use the Dialogg application to report measurements, receive 
reminders, and access personalized health information. The application also facilitates asynchronous 
communication between patients and care teams.

• 
Activity Tracking Devices: A wearable smartwatch (Fitbit) continuously monitors physical activity, sleep 
patterns, heart rate, and other wellness indicators. These data provide longitudinal insights into patient 
health and behaviour over time. 
Before discharge, patients are screened for eligibility, and those enrolled in the intervention are introduced to the 
TransCare technology ecosystem, including both medical and non-medical monitoring devices. Patients receive 
training in the use of the equipment and the Dialogg application, which serves as the primary interface for daily 
reporting and communication. Individual care plans are configured within the TelluCare platform, linking monitoring 
schedules with clinical oversight. Following discharge, patients begin using the devices in their home environment. 
Vital signs, activity levels, and other health indicators are collected and transmitted to the platform. Data captured 
through the Dialogg application include (i) intraday Fitbit time-series data, (ii) daily summary information, and (iii) 
data from other sensors and devices integrated into the RPM platform, such as blood pressure monitors and other 
connected medical devices. The platform visualizes health trends and generates alerts based on predefined 
thresholds. Healthcare professionals access dashboards to monitor patient status, review alerts, adjust care plans, 
and intervene when clinically appropriate. 
The Digital Assistant (Memas, see Figure 6) provides patients with educational content and self-assessment tools 
designed to support health literacy, engagement in care, and self-management. Memas aims to promote safety, 
coping, independence, and overall quality of life. Within TransCare, the Memas Rehabilitation application supports 
individuals following a care plan after hospital discharge and throughout the rehabilitation process at home. Memas 
serves as both a communication aid and an information resource for patients, family members, and caregivers during 
the post-discharge period. 
The platform includes two user roles with corresponding applications. The Patient (end user) is the primary user of 
the Memas application and receives support throughout the transitional care period following hospital discharge. 
The application provides: 
• 
a calendar of appointments and activities; 
• 
contact information for relevant healthcare providers and support persons; 
• 
information related to the patient's diagnosis and care plan; 
• 
personalized recommendations on diet and physical exercise; 
• 
access to well-being and self-assessment questionnaires; and 
• 
direct access to the Dialogg application for communication and monitoring.

The Administrator is a support person responsible for configuring, customizing, and maintaining the application for 
the patient. Administrators can create and manage multiple patient profiles, and more than one administrator can 
be assigned to the same patient when required. 
ML-Based Analytics (see Figure 7) apply AI techniques to the analysis of daily activity and vital-sign data collected 
through the RPM platform. The aim is to generate clinically relevant insights that support proactive intervention and 
help healthcare professionals identify situations that may require further clinical assessment. In its current 
implementation, the component employs a personalized deep-learning model based on the Transformer architecture 
to predict heart rate during physical activities such as walking, running, swimming, and cycling [28]. By comparing 
predicted and observed heart rate values, the system can identify deviations that may indicate changes in a patient's 
condition and warrant clinical attention. A personalized modelling approach is used, allowing the model to adapt to 
each patient's physiological characteristics and patterns of activity. This recognizes the considerable variation in 
physiological responses between individuals and supports more accurate interpretation of patient data. The model 
operates on anonymized data and provides decision-support information to healthcare professionals through the 
TransCare platform. By analysing trends over time and identifying potential anomalies, the system contributes to 
more proactive monitoring and follow-up of patients during the transition from hospital to home.

3. Results 
The redesigned care pathways in TransCare integrate digital technologies across multiple stages of the patient 
journey to support safer transitions from hospital to home, strengthen care coordination, and provide healthcare 
professionals with real-time clinical insights. Technology is a core component of the redesigned care model, 
supporting communication, monitoring, and decision-making throughout the care pathway. However, its 
implementation is carefully adapted to the specific needs, infrastructure, and digital maturity of each pilot site. In 
Italy, the focus is on older adults with frailty and multimorbidity. The platform is configured to support both patients 
and caregivers, with particular attention to accessibility for individuals with mild cognitive impairment through 
simplified user interfaces and localized educational content aligned with national healthcare standards. Continuous 
training is provided to promote effective and sustained use of the platform. In Romania, the emphasis is on 
cardiology patients at increased risk of readmission. The RPM system is integrated into existing hospital workflows, 
with cardiologists playing a central role in patient recruitment, clinical interpretation of monitoring data, and ongoing 
patient engagement. In Norway, the system is integrated within municipal healthcare services and is complemented 
by technologies such as camera supervision and personal alarm solutions to support patients receiving care at home, 
particularly in rural areas. Across all pilot sites, implementations are adapted to local organizational requirements

and operational realities. The solutions comply with national regulations, language requirements, and data 
protection legislation, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), ensuring the secure and ethical 
management of patient data. 
The redesigned matrix (Figure 8) in Italy aims to strengthen continuity of care and improve coordination between 
hospital services, patients, and home-based follow-up activities. The matrix retains the same macro-phases 
described in the original pathway while incorporating the interventions introduced through the TransCare project. 
The redesigned pathway clearly defines interactions between the key stakeholders involved in the care process, 
including patients, hospital professionals, and healthcare services responsible for post-discharge follow-up. Patients 
and caregivers receive training and support for home-monitoring activities during the first 90 days after discharge. 
This includes weekly telephone follow-up from TransCare project staff to support continued engagement and 
promote effective use of home-monitoring devices. Overall, the redesigned pathway provides a structured 
framework for improving transitional care at IRCCS-INRCA through enhanced communication, standardized 
assessments, patient empowerment, and the integration of home monitoring into the post-discharge follow-up 
process.

clinical status and promotes timely intervention when required. Overall, the redesigned pathway aims to reduce 
fragmentation of care by establishing a more continuous and coordinated follow-up process after discharge, thereby 
strengthening continuity of care and supporting safer transitions from hospital to home.

4. Discussion 
The development and early implementation of the redesigned transitional care pathways across the three pilot sites 
provide valuable insights into both shared challenges and context-specific differences. Based on project findings and 
iterative pathway refinements, the results suggest that improving transitional care requires a combination of 
organizational change, technology integration, and continuous stakeholder engagement. 
A key finding across all sites is the presence of fragmentation in communication and coordination between levels of 
care, particularly during discharge and post-discharge follow-up. While the underlying causes differ, ranging from 
limited integration between hospital and community services (INRCA), to resource constraints and high patient 
volumes (HINS), to coordination and interoperability challenges across municipal services (FAR), the consequences 
are similar: reduced continuity of care and an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including hospital readmission. 
These shared challenges highlight the importance of designing pathways that not only define clinical activities but 
also explicitly structure information exchange, responsibilities, and follow-up processes. 
At the same time, important differences between the pilot sites influenced how the redesigned pathways were 
adapted and implemented. INRCA operates within a geriatric context characterized by frailty, multimorbidity, and 
limited integration with community services, emphasizing the need for strengthened post-discharge coordination 
and caregiver involvement. HINS, as a high-capacity cardiology center, faces challenges related to high patient 
volumes and limited resources available for follow-up, making efficiency, prioritization, and RPM particularly relevant. 
In contrast, FAR represents a decentralized, municipality-based healthcare system where coordination across services 
and geographical distances are central challenges, and where the role of the SPOC is particularly important. These 
differences demonstrate that transitional care models must remain flexible and adaptable rather than standardized 
if they are to remain relevant and feasible across diverse healthcare systems. 
A major lesson learned is that the integration of digital technologies, including RPM, digital communication platforms, 
and AI-supported analytics, can strengthen continuity of care when accompanied by clearly defined workflows, role

clarification, and adequate training. Technology alone does not resolve coordination challenges; rather, it acts as an 
enabler within a well-structured care pathway. Early implementation experiences underline the importance of 
preparing patients and caregivers before discharge, supporting healthcare professionals in the adoption of new tools, 
and aligning digital solutions with existing organizational practices and capacities. 
Another important insight is the value of the co-design approach adopted throughout the project. The active 
involvement of patients, caregivers, clinicians, and technology partners contributed to pathways that are more 
realistic, acceptable, and aligned with stakeholder needs. 
 This approach facilitated the identification of practical barriers, increased stakeholder ownership, and supported the 
iterative refinement of both care pathways and technologies. The findings also highlight that successful 
implementation depends not only on technical and clinical validity, but also on user engagement, organizational 
readiness, and sustained stakeholder commitment. 
Positive outcomes observed during the redesign process include greater clarity of roles and responsibilities, more 
structured discharge planning, and enhanced follow-up supported by RPM and proactive communication. The 
introduction of continuous monitoring and data-driven insights creates opportunities for earlier identification of 
deterioration, more timely intervention, and a more personalized approach to care. Furthermore, increased 
involvement of patients and family members may contribute to empowerment and improved adherence to care 
plans. 
However, the study also confirms that transitional care redesign is a complex and iterative process. Given the early 
stage of implementation, many findings remain preliminary, and additional evaluation is required to assess 
effectiveness, long-term impact, scalability, and sustainability. Organizational constraints, resource limitations, and 
varying levels of digital maturity continue to influence implementation and require ongoing adaptation. At the Italian 
pilot site, the vulnerability and frailty of the target population also present practical challenges in identifying patients 
who both meet the inclusion criteria and possess sufficient independence to use digital monitoring technologies 
effectively. 
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the redesigned pathways are 
currently in the early stages of implementation, and comprehensive outcome data are not yet available. Second, the 
pilot sites operate within substantially different healthcare systems, organizational structures, and patient 
populations, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, implementation experiences have been 
influenced by local resource availability, workforce capacity, and digital maturity. Finally, the study primarily reports 
pathway redesign and early implementation experiences rather than clinical effectiveness outcomes. 
In summary, the TransCare experience demonstrates that improving transitional care requires a balanced integration 
of process redesign, technology, and stakeholder collaboration. The iterative and context-sensitive approach adopted 
in this project provides a valuable foundation for continued refinement and offers transferable lessons for healthcare 
systems seeking to strengthen transitions from hospital to home. 
5. Conclusion 
The TransCare experience demonstrates that effective hospital-to-home transitions require the integration of care 
pathway redesign, digital technologies, and stakeholder collaboration. Across the three pilot sites, digitally supported 
pathways addressed fragmentation in communication, coordination, discharge planning, and post-discharge followup while remaining adaptable to different healthcare contexts. RPM, digital communication tools, and AI-supported 
analytics have the potential to strengthen continuity of care and support earlier intervention when embedded within 
clearly defined workflows and supported by appropriate training and organizational readiness. The co-design and

iterative approach facilitated the adaptation of both care processes and technologies to the needs of patients, 
caregivers, and healthcare professionals. Although further evaluation is required to assess long-term outcomes and 
scalability, the findings provide transferable lessons for the development of context-sensitive, technology-enabled 
transitional care pathways across European healthcare systems. Future work will focus on pilot testing the 
redesigned pathways in real-world settings and evaluating their feasibility, usability, and impact on transitional care 
outcomes and patient experience.
