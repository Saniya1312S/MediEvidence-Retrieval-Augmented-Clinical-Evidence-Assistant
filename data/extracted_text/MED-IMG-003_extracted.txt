Abstract
Temporal medical image segmentation relies on pixel-wise
annotations, which are prohibitively expensive for long image sequences. Existing weakly-supervised methods reduce
the annotation burden per frame but still require repeated
annotations across the sequence. Furthermore, sparse supervision can result in temporally unstable representations.
Addressing both challenges requires a model that can leverage strong spatial priors from large-scale pretraining while
minimizing reliance on dense annotations. Frozen 2D vision foundation models provide a promising basis for this
by offering transferable spatial representations with low
trainable parameters. However, their lack of explicit temporal modeling limits their ability to propagate reliable information across anatomical sequences. To address this,
we present a weakly-supervised framework for temporal
segmentation from a single scribble-annotated frame, with
all remaining frames left unlabeled. The framework combines parameter-efficient adaptation of DINOv3 encoders
present in the mean-teacher architecture.
Furthermore,
a set of complementary losses, including a temporal loss
helps generate reliable segmentation priors for the unlabeled frames. This formulation decouples annotation costs
from sequence length while retaining the transferable spatial knowledge of the pretrained encoder.
Extensive experiments on the publicly available CAMUS and CardiacNet datasets demonstrate that our approach outperforms
other scribble-supervised methods. These results show that
accurate and temporally consistent segmentation can be
achieved from extremely sparse supervision, substantially
reducing annotation requirements for temporal echocardiographic segmentation and providing a practical framework
for scalable clinical deployment.
1. Introduction
Segmentation of temporal medical sequences is vital in
many clinical applications, as it enables consistent quantification and tracking of anatomical and pathological changes
over time [2, 19, 23]. However, achieving reliable sequence
segmentation remains constrained by the annotation bottleneck. Methods achieving high segmentation performance
conventionally rely on dense pixel-wise annotations for every frame, while obtaining such large-scale annotations in
clinical settings remains a significant challenge [20, 22].
This challenge is particularly pronounced in echocardiography, where speckle noise obscures anatomical boundaries
and adjacent tissues can exhibit similar acoustic properties,
making structures difficult to distinguish without expert interpretation [6, 24]. Consequently, annotation of echocardiographic sequences is both time-consuming and dependent on specialist expertise, with annotation effort increasing with every additional frame in a sequence.
Current methods that reduce annotation requirements
can broadly be divided into two categories.
Semisupervised learning (SSL) combines a small subset of
densely annotated frames with a larger set of unlabeled
frames, as shown in Mean Teacher [21] and Cross Pseudo
Supervision (CPS) [5]. The labeled subset provides direct
supervision through segmentation losses, while the unlabeled subset provides consistency regularization [13] that
encourages reliable and temporally coherent predictions.
Weakly-supervised learning (WSL), in contrast, replaces
dense masks with coarse annotations such as bounding
boxes [8, 9, 15], points [27, 30] or scribbles [1, 3, 4], typically provided on every training frame. These approaches
reduce annotation burden in different ways. SSL reduces
the number of frames requiring dense annotation while
WSL reduces the amount of annotation required per frame.
However, both remain dependent on annotations across the

To address this limitation, we propose a weaklysupervised framework for segmenting echocardiographic
sequences from a single scribble-annotated frame, with all
remaining frames left unlabeled. The framework consists of
three complementary components that convert sparse spatial supervision into reliable sequence-wide segmentation.
Firstly, a mean-teacher framework generates dense pseudolabels and propagates supervision outward from the annotated frame in a bidirectional manner. Secondly, a frozen
DINOv3-based [18] encoder, adapted to medical images
using Low-rank Adaptations (LoRA) [7] provides transferable spatial representations while restricting adaptation to
a small number of trainable parameters. The representations are fed into a lightweight U-Net-style decoder [16],
keeping trainable parameters low. Thirdly, complementary
spatial and temporal losses enforce consistency across the
sequence while allowing anatomically plausible changes in
cardiac shape.
We evaluate the proposed framework on two publicly
available datasets, Cardiac Acquisitions for Multi-structure
Ultrasound Segmentation (CAMUS) [10] and CardiacNet [25], spanning three different cohorts. Experimental
results demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms existing scribble-supervised baselines in both Dice
and HD95 metrics, while achieving a more favorable balance between precision and recall. These results show that
accurate and spatially well-localized temporal segmentation
can be achieved from only one scribble-annotated frame,
substantially reducing annotation requirements for echocardiographic sequence segmentation. Our key contributions
are summarized as follows:
• We propose a weakly-supervised temporal segmentation
framework for medical imaging sequences based on single slice scribble annotations on the middle frame.
• We demonstrate that a LoRA-adapted foundation model
encoder can improve segmentation performance with significantly fewer parameters compared to state-of-the-art
scribble segmentation methods
• We also propose an anchor-relative bidirectional temporal loss, Ltemp, that can propagate supervision outward
from the anchor frame in both directions while allowing
for drifting so that segmentation masks follow the natural
progression of anatomical structures.
2. Related Work
2.1. Scribble-supervised Segmentation
Scribble-supervised segmentation addresses the problem
of learning dense segmentation masks from sparse pixel
annotations.
Common methods such as partial crossentropy (pCE) compute the supervised loss only over annotated pixels but do not extend supervision to unlabeled
regions. Therefore, existing scribble-supervised methods
extend sparse scribbles through augmentation, consistency
regularization and architectural modifications.
Augmentation-based methods perturb the training samples and enforce consistency between the corresponding
predictions. CutMix [26] constructs a new training sample by combining regions from two images, with the corresponding labels mixed using the same spatial transformation.
This exposes the model to additional combinations of foreground and background regions while preserving the available sparse supervision.
CycleMix [28] extends mixed-sample augmentation with cycle-consistency
constraints, using complementary transformed views to exploit information from both labeled and unlabeled regions.
Together, these strategies encourage predictions to remain
consistent under input perturbations.
Architecture-based approaches instead modify the segmentation framework to better exploit the limited spatial
supervision. ScribFormer [12] combines a convolutional
branch with a transformer branch and a class-activation
branch to integrate local appearance with global contextual information, allowing the network to infer object structure beyond the annotated scribbles. Other methods combine architectural changes with consistency-based training.
HELPNet [29] introduces hierarchical perturbations at multiple spatial granularity and enforces consistency between
the resulting predictions, together with edge-guided refinement to improve boundaries that are not directly specified
by the scribble. Despite different mechanisms, these methods process frames independently and require scribble annotations throughout the sequence, causing annotation cost
to scale with sequence length.
2.2. Vision Foundation Models and ParameterEfficient Adaptation
Large-scale self-supervised pretraining has produced vision backbones with transferable representations that can be
reused across downstream tasks with limited task-specific
supervision [11]. DINOv3 extends this capability through
large-scale self-supervised pretraining, providing spatial
representations that can be transferred to domains where labeled data are scarce. We use a general-purpose DINOv3
rather than an echocardiography-specific foundation model
so that the supervision scheme remains modality-agnostic
rather than tied to a single domain.
Adapting large pretrained backbones to a target medical domain introduces a second challenge. Full fine-tuning
of all backbone parameters increases computational cost
and risk of overfitting [17]. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning
(PEFT) methods instead preserve most of the pretrained
representation while restricting task-specific adaptation to
a small number of parameters.

3.3. Parameter-Efficient Encoding and Multi-Scale
Decoding
A frozen DINOv3 encoder is adapted using LoRA on the
query and value projections of three transformer blocks,
introducing 27.6 K trainable parameters corresponding to
0.03% of the 85.7 M while preserving the pretrained representation under sparse supervision. Patch tokens are extracted from the three adapted blocks. Each token corresponds to a 16 × 16 pixel patch so a 512 × 512 input yields
a grid of 32×32 tokens. Since the transformer block applies
no spatial downsampling, all three LoRA block readouts
share this resolution and differ only in semantic depth. They
are resampled to strides 4, 8 and 16, producing 128 × 128,
64 × 64 and 32 × 32 spatial feature maps. A fourth level at
stride 32 is obtained by average pooling the stride-16 map
to 16 × 16, which provides the decoder a coarser representation and a larger effective receptive field.
Each pyramid level is independently projected using a
1 × 1 convolution followed by group normalization and
ReLU. The projected features are then passed to a U-Net
style decoder, beginning at stride-32 level and progressively
upsampled through three stages. At each stage, the upsampled decoder features are concatenated with the corresponding projected feature map from the pyramid. The final decoder output is converted to segmentation logits and bilinearly resized to the original input resolution.
3.4. Dense Supervision from Single Scribbleannotated Frame
3.4.1. Mean Teacher Construction and Scribble Supervision
The framework maintains structurally identical student and
teacher branches. The student is optimized directly through
gradient descent while the teacher parameters are updated
as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student parameters. Since the DINOv3 backbone is frozen throughout training, its pretrained weights are shared between the
two branches, while the LoRA adapters, feature projections and segmentation decoder are maintained separately
and updated through the corresponding student and teacher
mechanisms.
For each training sequence, the student receives a weakly
augmented view generated using brightness and contrast
perturbations together with additive noise. These transformations modify image appearance while preserving spatial geometry, ensuring pixel-wise alignment between the
student predictions and the supervision generated from the
unaugmented sequence.

xm, which is the only frame containing scribble annotations. We apply pCE exclusively to pixels labeled as foreground and background

4. Experiments and Results
4.1. Datasets
We evaluate our method on two publicly available echocardiography datasets, CAMUS and CardiacNet. All datasets
are divided into training, validation and test sets using a
70:10:20 ratio.
CAMUS contains echocardiographic sequences from 500 patients, from which we use the twochamber (2CH) half-sequence subset. Ground-truth masks
are provided for all frames, so the mask-derived scribble
annotations is on the middle frame of the sequence. Performance is evaluated on three anatomical structures namely
the left-ventricular endocardium (LVendo), left-ventricular
epicardium (LVepi) and left atrium.
CardiacNet comprises two pathology-specific cohorts:
atrial septal defect (ASD) and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). Segmentation masks are released for only
a subset of the acquired sequences and a small number of
the released files could not be read. We therefore use every
sequence for which a usable annotation exists, giving 135
ASD and 299 PAH sequences. Unlike CAMUS, groundtruth masks are provided for approximately five frames
within each sequence, so the scribble is placed on the middle annotated frame rather than the middle frame of the sequence. Both cohorts are evaluated on four cardiac structures: left ventricle (LV), left atrium (LA), right ventricle
(RV) and right atrium (RA). Across both datasets, training
supervision is restricted to one background and one foreground scribble with all remaining frames unlabeled.
4.2. Implementation Details
All experiments were implemented in PyTorch with Python
3.12 and CUDA 13.2 and conducted on a single NVIDIA
RTX PRO 5000 Blackwell GPU. We use DINOv3 ViTB/16 as the vision foundation model backbone, with rank-3
LoRA adapters applied to the query and value projections
of transformer blocks 3, 6 and 9. Single-channel ultrasound
frames are used across three channels, resized to 512×512,
and normalized using the ImageNet channel-wise mean and
standard deviation.
Models are trained for 40 epochs using AdamW with a
learning rate of 1 × 10−4, weight decay of 1 × 10−4 and
mixed-precision training. The partial cross-entropy, Dice,
temporal consistency and gated-CRF losses are weighted by
1.0, 0.5, 0.1 and 0.1 respectively. The weights of the three
unsupervised objectives are linearly ramped from zero to
their respective values over the first five epochs. The teacher
parameters are updated using an EMA decay of 0.99 and
the connectivity-filtered teacher and student predictions are
mixed using a pixel-wise Bernoulli mask with β = 0.5.

4.3. Baseline and Evaluation Metrics
We compare our method against a U-Net trained with pCE
alone and four scribble-supervised methods: CutMix, CycleMix, ScribFormer and HELPNet. A consistent evaluation protocol is applied across all methods, with performance measured on all frames with available ground-truth
annotations to assess generalization beyond the annotated
frame. We report the Dice similarity coefficient and 95thpercentile Hausdorff distance (HD95), together with precision and recall to further characterize segmentation errors.
Tab. 1 shows that our method achieves the highest
average Dice across all three cohorts, with scores of
0.784, 0.744, and 0.754 on CAMUS, CardiacNet-ASD
and CardiacNet-PAH respectively.
Performance is lowest on the CardiacNet-ASD cohort, where the septal defect disrupts the normal boundary between the atria, increasing ambiguity in chamber delineation. Compared to
consistency-based methods, our method improves over CutMix by 0.097, 0.338, and 0.192 Dice respectively.
The
improvement over dedicated scribble-supervised methods is
larger, particularly on CardiacNet. While ScribFormer and
HELPNet achieve competitive performance on some CAMUS structures, their average Dice remains low on both
CardiacNet cohorts, indicating poor generalization while
our method achieves 0.744 on CardiacNet-ASD and 0.754
on CardiacNet-PAH. Overall, our method achieves the highest Dice on 10 of 11 structures, demonstrating more consistent performance under single-frame scribble supervision.
It also shows that our method achieves the lowest average HD95 across all three cohorts, with 31.25,
41.72, and 28.30 pixels on CAMUS, CardiacNet-ASD, and
CardiacNet-PAH respectively. Consistent with the Dice results, CardiacNet-ASD remains the most challenging cohort, although our method maintains considerably lower
boundary errors across all four structures.
Given the substantial performance gap observed in both
Dice and HD95, we further investigate why existing methods degrade under single-frame supervision. As illustrated
in Figs. 2 to 4, their poor performance is primarily driven
by severe over-segmentation rather than localized boundary
errors, with predictions extending substantially beyond the
target anatomy. This explains the low Dice and high HD95
observed in the quantitative results. To better characterize
this failure mode, we additionally analyze precision and recall in Tab. 2.
Tab. 2 further corroborates this failure mode. The pCE
baseline, CycleMix and ScribFormer consistently exhibit
high recall but extremely low precision, particularly on
the two CardiacNet cohorts.
This pattern shows oversegmentation, where predicted masks cover large regions
of the image, leading to substantial overlap with the ground
truth mask. Thus, recall values are inflated despite poor
localization quality. In contrast, our framework improves
precision while maintaining competitive recall, with precision consistently exceeding recall across all three cohorts.
Overall, the improvements in Dice and HD95 can be attributed primarily to a reduction in false-positive predictions
rather than increased anatomical coverage, consistent with
the qualitative segmentation results.
5. Ablations
All ablation experiments are conducted on the CAMUS
dataset using the LV endocardium. CAMUS is selected as
ground-truth masks are available for every frame so metrics
can be computed on the entire sequence, enabling a more
comprehensive evaluation of temporal segmentation performance. The LV endocardium is used as a representative
structure to systematically isolate the contribution of each
component in the proposed framework. We evaluate the effects of the architectural design, LoRA adaptation, meanteacher framework and individual loss terms.
5.1. Effectiveness of Architecture
We further examine the depth of the feature pyramid by
reducing it from four to three levels, removing the coarsest scale while keeping the frozen DINOv3 encoder and all
other components unchanged. The three-level model decreases Dice from 0.824 to 0.734 on CAMUS and from
0.779 to 0.763 on CardiacNet-PAH. The coarsest level aggregates information over a larger area of the image, allowing the decoder to reason about global shape and spatial
relationships between structures, which cannot be inferred
from local appearance alone. Without this level, the decoder must recover such context from finer scales that look
at smaller areas, so spatially separated regions are processed
largely independently. This is particularly detrimental under scribble supervision, where most pixels and unlabeled
and the network must rely on global shape priors to extend sparse annotations to complete, anatomically plausible
masks.
5.2. Effectiveness of LoRA
Removing the low-rank adapters reduces Dice from 0.824
to 0.784 on CAMUS and from 0.779 to 0.771 on
CardiacNet-PAH. The improvement on CAMUS indicates
that adapting the pretrained DINOv3 features is important
for capturing echocardiographic characteristics such as lowcontrast boundaries and speckle patterns.
5.3. Effectiveness of Mean Teacher Framework
Removing the EMA teacher while retaining all other components reduces Dice from 0.824 to 0.799 on CAMUS
and from 0.779 to 0.772 on CardiacNet-PAH. The consistent reduction across both datasets demonstrates the benefit of maintaining a temporally averaged teacher. Without EMA updates, both branches adapt directly to similar

substantially improves segmentation performance to 0.770
and 0.626 respectively, demonstrating the importance of
exploiting pseudo-label supervision beyond the annotated
pixels. Introducing the temporal consistency loss, Ltemp
further increases Dice to 0.812 on CAMUS and 0.790
on CardiacNet-PAH, indicating that coherence between
neighboring frames provides valuable supervision. Finally,

6. Conclusion and Future Work
We propose a weakly-supervised framework for temporal
echocardiography segmentation using only two scribbles on
an anchor frame. By combining a mean-teacher framework,
a LoRA-adapted DINOv3 encoder and connectivity-filtered
pseudo-labels with spatial and temporal losses, supervision
is propagated across unlabeled sequences. Experiments on
CAMUS and CardiacNet demonstrate consistent improvements over existing scribble-supervised methods in Dice
and HD95 while substantially reducing false-positive predictions. These results show that annotation cost can be decoupled from sequence length without sacrificing reliable
temporal segmentation.
