Decisions by Discrimination: Differential Evidence for Medical Reasoning
Yunpeng Xiao1, Yuzhang Xie1, Junhao Huang1, Carl Yang1, Xiao Hu2, Kai Shu1
1Department of Computer Science, Emory University,
2Nell Hodgson Woodruff School of Nursing, Emory University
{yunpeng.xiao, yuzhang.xie, junhao.huang,
j.carlyang, xiao.hu, kai.shu}@emory.edu
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly studied for clinical decision support,
and medical question answering provides a
testbed for evaluating this capability. In particular, medical multiple-choice questions (MCQs)
reflect a core challenge in clinical decisionmaking: plausible options often share overlapping evidence, while only a few decisive
factors distinguish the correct option from incorrect ones. However, existing retrieval-based
and reasoning-oriented methods mainly focus
on supplying broadly relevant knowledge or
eliciting reasoning paths, rather than identifying the compact differential evidence that
separates confusing alternatives. In this work,
we present a retrieval-grounded differential evidence framework for medical MCQs. Our
method extracts option-specific pieces of differential evidence and organizes them into concise evidence prompts for answer selection.
This design better aligns external knowledge
with differential reasoning while reducing irrelevant context. Experiments show consistent
accuracy gains across multiple datasets and
target LLMs. Ablation studies further show
that our framework outperforms both directly
using retrieved evidence chunks and closedsource LLM-generated general hints, and that
its improvements are not solely explained by
answer-proximal guidance, but are consistent
with differential evidence serving as a compact
aid for reasoning over competing alternatives.
Our findings also offer insights for medical reasoning and other decision-making settings with
multiple possible choices.
1
Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly
explored as clinical assistants, powering systems
for triage, diagnosis support, and patient education
(Singhal et al., 2023, 2025; Wu et al., 2024). However, even medically tuned LLMs remain prone to
unsafe reasoning behaviors. A common challenge
in clinical reasoning is that many candidate diagnoses or treatment decisions share a large core of
overlapping facts, while differing only in a small
number of critical discriminatory factors (Bowen,
2006; Cook and Décary, 2020). If a model fails
to identify and reason over these few distinguishing facts, then even access to abundant relevant
knowledge may still lead to misdiagnosis or other
incorrect clinical decisions.
In medical multiple-choice questions (medical
MCQs), this challenge is especially well exposed,
making them a natural benchmark for studying differential clinical reasoning. In real clinical practice,
competing diagnoses or treatment options often
share substantial overlapping evidence, with only a
few decisive factors determining the correct choice.
Medical MCQs capture this structure naturally: several answer options may each align with part of the
vignette, but only one is supported by the discriminative evidence that resolves the case. This makes
the benchmark useful for evaluating whether models can identify clinically meaningful differences
among plausible hypotheses, rather than relying on
broadly relevant but non-discriminative facts.
However,
existing
methods
are
still
not
well aligned with this requirement.
Retrievalaugmented generation (RAG) has become a common strategy for medical QA, supplementing
LLMs with external knowledge from structured
resources such as knowledge graphs and unstructured corpora such as textbooks. Current medical
RAG pipelines are primarily designed to retrieve
generally relevant evidence, not the small set of
discriminative facts that separates plausible answer
options. As a result, they often return a broad supportive context while failing to surface the evidence
needed to resolve near-miss alternatives. Figure 1
presents a concrete example in which two clinically
similar diseases share multiple symptoms, while
only a small number of distinctive symptoms determine the final diagnosis.

We therefore propose a retrieval-grounded differential evidence framework for medical MCQs.
Instead of appending broad supporting context, our
method extracts anchor cues, retrieves cue–option
co-occurrence passages from textbooks, and distills them into compact option-specific evidence
for answer selection. This design reduces retrieved
context length by about 85% while improving accuracy across benchmarks.
Our main contributions are:
• We formulate differential evidence as an evidence organization problem, emphasizing
option-discriminative evidence rather than
broadly relevant knowledge.
• We propose a four-stage framework that extracts anchor cues, retrieves cue–option cooccurrence evidence, and distills it into compact option-specific evidence for answer selection.
• Experiments on three medical MCQ benchmarks show consistent gains across multiple
LLMs. Ablation studies with raw retrieved
text, general hints, and evidence-only settings
suggest that the gains come from differential
evidence rather than more text or general answer cues.
2
Related Work
2.1
Medical QA and Retrieval-Augmented
Generation (RAG)
Large-scale medical QA benchmarks, such as
MedQA (Jin et al., 2021), MedXpertQA (Zuo
et al., 2025), and MedBullets (Chen et al., 2025),
have become important testbeds for evaluating finegrained clinical reasoning in LLMs. In particular,
multiple-choice medical QA provides a constrained
decision-making setting in which several candidate
options may all appear clinically plausible at first
glance. This makes such benchmarks especially
useful for assessing whether models can assist in
real-world clinical decision making (Chen et al.,
2025; Wei et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025b).
Many medical QA tasks, especially MedQAstyle problems, require reasoning beyond the information explicitly stated in the question stem,
thus models often need to draw on background
medical knowledge, including disease mechanisms,
diagnostic criteria, treatment indications, and contraindications (Liévin et al., 2024; Lucas et al.,
2024; Bhasuran et al., 2025). Since this knowledge is broad, evolving, and difficult to capture reliably through parametric memory alone,
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over external medical knowledge bases has emerged as
a natural solution (Yang et al., 2025b). By retrieving evidence from curated resources such as
clinical guidelines, biomedical ontologies, textbooks, and knowledge graphs, medical RAG systems can ground answer selection in explicit evidence rather than latent memorization (Amugongo
et al., 2025; Ke et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025).
Recent work has explored this direction through
methods such as MIRAGE (Xiong et al., 2024a),
K-Comp (Cho and Lee, 2025), MKRAG (Shi
et al., 2025), MedRAG (Zhao et al., 2025), BriefContext (Zhang et al., 2025a), RAG2(Sohn et al.,
2025), KGARevion (Su et al., 2024), and SelfBioRAG (Jeong et al., 2024). In addition to traditional text-based RAG, recent studies have explored KG-guided retrieval and reasoning frameworks, such as Dr.Knows (Gao et al., 2025),
MedIKAL (Jia et al., 2025), KERAP (Xie et al.,
2025), KG4Diagnosis (Zuo et al., 2024), and
KGARevion (Su et al., 2024), which leverage
knowledge graphs to facilitate answer generation,
diagnostic reasoning, and evidence retrieval.
Although retrieved external evidence can im-

prove factual grounding, conventional retrieval generally prioritizes overall semantic relevance to the
question, often returning broad background knowledge that may support multiple candidate options
simultaneously (Amugongo et al., 2025; Liu et al.,
2025c; Xiong et al., 2024b). However, for medical multiple-choice reasoning, the key challenge is
often not general relevance but discriminative utility: the retrieved evidence should help the model
determine why one option is more appropriate than
other clinically plausible alternatives under the specific case context (Chen et al., 2023; Singh and
Shrivastava, 2025; Alhazmi et al., 2024). These
observations make differential evidence especially
well-suited for medical QA.
2.2
Option Discrimination and Evidence
Selection in Multiple-Choice Reasoning
Existing work has increasingly suggested that the
main challenge in multiple-choice reasoning is not
merely identifying generally relevant knowledge,
but distinguishing among candidate options that
may all appear partially plausible.
Early work
on multi-choice reading comprehension emphasized joint interactions among the passage, question, and candidate answers, rather than questiononly matching (Wang et al., 2018). Recent studies
further show that LLMs are sensitive to candidateoption structure: some methods model elimination over options (Ma and Du, 2023), while others find that predictions can vary under answerorder permutations, indicating unstable discrimination among alternatives (Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024). Related analyses also show that
benchmarks often contain several plausible candidates, and that models may select the least incorrect option rather than one that is distinctly correct
(Wang et al., 2025a; Palta et al., 2024). This issue is especially salient in medical MCQs, where
options often share overlapping clinical facts and
differ only in a few decisive mechanisms, contraindications, or management roles. In parallel,
retrieval-based methods have incorporated answer
options into retrieval or knowledge selection, such
as question-aware knowledge graph prompting and
answer-conditioned support retrieval (Zhang et al.,
2025b; Liu et al., 2025b). However, these methods
mainly improve option-aware knowledge access,
while compact differential evidence for optionlevel comparison remains underexplored. Unlike
prior option-aware retrieval methods that primarily
improve access to option-related knowledge, our
focus is on organizing knowledge into differential
evidence that clearly distinguishing between the
various options.
3
Methods
In this section, we will introduce the proposed
differential evidence reasoning method for clinical decision-making, especially in multiple-choice
questions. Figure 2 shows the framework of our
proposed method, which consists of four stages:
anchor cue extraction, cue-option co-occurrence
retrieval, differential evidence generation, and
evidence-based inference. The first three stages
form the differential evidence construction pipeline,
while the fourth stage uses the generated evidence
for final answer prediction.
3.1
Definition of Differential Evidence
Given a multiple-choice question with candidate
options
O = {o1, o2, . . . , om},
we define differential evidence for an option oi as
external evidence that supports oi more strongly
than competing options under the current case context. In other words, differential evidence is not
merely relevant knowledge, but knowledge with
discriminative utility for separating clinically plausible alternatives.
Formally, if s(e, o) denotes the degree to which
an evidence item e supports option o, then ideal
differential evidence for oi would satisfy
s(e, oi) > s(e, ok),
∀k ̸= i,
or more generally provide substantially stronger
support for oi than for the strongest competing
option.
In practice, explicit support scoring is difficult
because raw textbook passages are often long, redundant, and only indirectly aligned with optionlevel distinctions. We therefore approximate differential evidence through a retrieval-and-distillation
pipeline. The following subsections describe the
first three stages of the framework for differential
evidence construction, while the fourth stage performs evidence-based inference.
3.2
Definition of Atomic Facts and Anchor
Cues
In medical multiple-choice questions, one data sample typically consists of the parts: the background
context C, the explicit question Q and the options

Figure 2: The framework of our proposed method.
O. Since the key clinical evidence is usually conveyed in the background description, we decompose C into a set of atomic facts, following the
intuition of fine-grained factual decomposition in
fact-checking (Min et al., 2023):
C = {f1, f2, . . . , fn},
where each fi denotes a minimal clinical fact, such
as a symptom, laboratory finding, medical history
element, or treatment detail.
However, atomic decomposition alone is insufficient, as the fact set may still contain substantial
redundancy and many facts that are only weakly
relevant to the question Q and final decision. We
therefore define anchor cue, which is to distill the
original fact set into a compact set of highly salient
cues. Formally, we select a subset of atomic facts,
A = {a1, a2, . . . , ai} ⊆C,
i ≪n,
where A denotes the set of anchor cues.
This step can be understood as a general content
distillation problem in text understanding: from
a larger collection of fine-grained facts, we aim
to retain only those that best preserve the core semantics and decision-critical information of the
scenario. This step is not intended for full clinical
reasoning or direct answer prediction, but to distill
the scenario into a few salient anchor cues, similar to focused reading comprehension over clinical
text. For example, the context mentions that the
patient developed tinnitus while the question asks
Which medication caused this phenomenon, then
"tinnitus" serves as the anchor cue for this specific
question.
3.3
Textbook Knowledge Retrieval through
Anchor Cue–Option Co-occurrence
Similar to traditional RAG, we also need to leverage external knowledge to enhance the performance of the target model in medical scenarios.
However, as noted in Section 2, current knowledge graphs are not yet sufficient to distinguish the
subtle nuances between options and generate discriminative evidence; therefore, we identify chunks
in textbooks that exhibit co-occurrence between anchor cues and the options as our preliminary source
of external knowledge.
Specifically, by leveraging the powerful semantic understanding capabilities of external LLMs,
we first input the context C and the question Q into
these models to extract anchor cues
A = {a1, a2, . . . , ai},
and normalize these cues and generate their common variants (e.g., singular/plural forms, abbreviations, etc.). Similarly, we input the options O into
the external LLMs, subjecting them to the same
normalization process and generating their common variants
On = {o1, o2, . . . , oj}.

--- PAGE 5 [pdf_text] ---
This strict input separation reduces the risk that
external LLMs act as implicit solvers of the task.
Instead, their role is limited to localized semantic
normalization and variant generation, rather than
answer inference over the full MCQ instance.
Next, we use the textbook provided in the
MedQA dataset (Jin et al., 2021) to identify cooccurring chunks
pij = Co-occurr(ai, oj).
A chunk is considered co-occurring if it contains
at least one normalized variant of an anchor cue
and one normalized variant of an option concept.
In this work, to control prompt length and reduce
redundant evidence, we extract only one chunk
for each anchor cue-option pair. Specifically, we
extract a maximum of |A| × |O| paragraphs. More
details are shown in appendix.
3.4
Textbook-Constrained Differential
Evidence Generation
Compared with knowledge graphs, textbook knowledge provides substantially richer semantic content.
However, it is often fragmented, redundant, and
not explicitly organized around option-level distinctions, which makes it difficult to directly obtain discriminative evidence for specific answer
choices. In addition, textbook retrieval may still
miss relevant information due to limited coverage
or retrieval failures. To address this, we use external LLMs to generate option-discriminative evidence while treating retrieved textbook knowledge
as a constraint on the generation process. Formally, we generate the set of differential evidence
D = {dA, dB, ...} where dA denotes the evidence
specific to Option A, as follows:
D = ExternalLLM(A, Q, O, P).
Here, A is the set of anchor cues, O is the set of
original options, P = S pij is the set of retrieved
external knowledge, and Q is the question. Each do
is generated as a short, option-specific, conditionoriented statement capturing the conditions that
most clearly distinguish option from competing
alternatives. When retrieved evidence is missing
for a given option, the LLM is allowed to provide
limited supplementation from internal knowledge.
3.5
Differential Evidence Quality Control
Because external LLMs may have been pretrained
on overlapping medical QA data, we introduce several safeguards to reduce answer leakage and bias.
First, during anchor cue extraction and normalization, the external LLM only sees the context and
question C, Q without the options O; during option extraction and normalization, it only sees the
options O without the context and question C, Q;
during differential evidence generation, it only sees
the anchor cues, questions, options A, Q, O without context C. As a result, the external model never
has access to the full MCQ instance at any stage.
Second, the system prompt explicitly instructs the
model to prioritize retrieved textbook evidence and
only supplement missing details when retrieval
is insufficient. Finally, for differential evidence
generation with external LLMs, we use carefully
designed prompts to encourage the production of
option-discriminative evidence while preserving as
much neutrality as possible.
3.6
Differential Evidence-Based Inference
Upon obtaining the differential evidence set, the
target LLMs—typically distinct from the external
LLM used in the preceding step—infer the answers
to the medical MCQs using the following method:
Answer = TargetLLM(C, Q, O, D).
Here, C is the clinical context, Q is the question,
O is the set of original options, and D is the generated differential evidence. The target LLM takes
these components as input and produces the final
answer.

4.1
Datasets and Models
Datasets. We evaluate our method on MedQA
(Jin et al., 2021), MedXpertQA-Text (Zuo et al.,
2025), and MedBullets (Chen et al., 2025) because
together they provide a strong testbed for scenariogrounded, option-discriminative medical reasoning.
By contrast, some medical QA benchmarks, such
as PubMedQA (Jin et al., 2019) and MedMCQA
(Pal et al., 2022), generally feature shorter question
stems and are more oriented toward knowledgecentric medical question answering. Therefore,
they are less suitable for evaluating the scenariogrounded differential reasoning targeted in this
work.
External LLMs. Motivated by RAG2 (Sohn
et al., 2025), we view the external LLM as a differential evidence generator. Its quality mainly affects the generated evidence, which then influences
downstream reasoning. We tested several closedsource LLMs, including Claude-family models,
and selected GPT-5 mini (Singh et al., 2026) as
the evidence generator because it offered the best
overall trade-off among evidence quality, instruction following, cost, and API stability. To improve
reproducibility and assess dependence on closedsource models, we also tested open-source models.
Among them, DeepSeek-V3.2 (Liu et al., 2025a)
produced the most reliable differential evidence in
our experiments. We therefore report an additional
ablation using DeepSeek-V3.2 as the evidence generator.
Target LLMs. We evaluate our method on a series of open-source LLMs. These include Llama 3
(Grattafiori et al., 2024), Mistral (Mistral AI, 2025),
Gemma (Gemma Team et al., 2025) and Qwen 3
(Yang et al., 2025a). All experiments are conducted
through Amazon Bedrock under a unified inference
setup, using deterministic decoding. More details
are shown in appendix.
4.2
Performance of Differential Evidence
(RQ1)
We use four settings for the experiments: Baseline,
CoT, Differential Evidence and CoT + Differential Evidence . Baseline directly predicts the
answer from the original context, question, and
options. CoT uses the same input with an additional zero-shot chain-of-thought instruction. Differential Evidence further augments the input with
our generated option-specific differential evidence.
CoT + Differential Evidence setting simultaneously utilize CoT and differential evidence.
As shown in Table 1, the CoT + Differential
Evidence setting achieves better performance than
both Baseline and CoT across all five models. Notably, using Differential Evidence alone already
achieves performance close to CoT in many settings, indicating that the generated evidence itself
provides useful external knowledge and optiondiscriminative information.
We further examine whether the improvement is
tied to a single proprietary generator by replacing
GPT-5 mini with DeepSeek-V3.2 as the evidence
generator. Table 2 shows that DeepSeek-generated
evidence improves all five target models, increasing the average MedQA accuracy from 63.38% to
69.09%. While GPT-5 mini remains stronger on
average, the consistent gains from DeepSeek-V3.2
indicate that the benefits of differential evidence
are not exclusive to one generator, though generator
quality still affects the magnitude of improvement.

4.3
Benefits Beyond Generic Retrieved
Evidence and Generic Hints (RQ2)
To examine whether the gains of differential evidence go beyond simply adding more external text,
we compare two CoT-based settings: CoT + Text,
which directly appends the retrieved co-occurrence
paragraphs to the input, and CoT + Differential
Evidence, which instead provides the model with
compact option-specific differential evidence distilled from those retrieved materials.
As shown in Table 3, directly using the retrieved
co-occurrence text does not reliably improve performance. In many cases, CoT + Text even performs
worse than the CoT baseline, especially on MedQA
and MedBullets. By contrast, CoT + Differential
Evidence consistently yields positive gains over
CoT across all three datasets. This contrast suggests that the benefit does not come from merely
exposing the model to more retrieved content, but
from organizing external knowledge into a concise
and discriminative form that is more aligned with
multiple-choice decision making.
We further compare the output length of the two
methods using word count. As shown in Table 4,
differential evidence is substantially more compact
than directly concatenated retrieved text across all
three datasets. For example, on MedQA the average length decreases from 677.3 words for cooccurrence text to 108.5 words for differential evidence, with similar reductions on MedBullets and
MedXpertQA-Text. This suggests that the gain
of our framework comes not from adding more retrieved content, but from organizing external knowledge into a shorter and more discriminative form.
Moreover, the substantial number of non-empty cooccurrence retrievals suggests that our method is
supported by sufficient retrieved paragraphs, rather
than depending purely on the external LLM to fabricate evidence.
These findings indicate that generic retrieved text
can be too long, noisy, or weakly structured to effectively support reasoning, and may even distract
the model from the decisive clues in the question.
This observation is also consistent with prior studies reporting that directly concatenating retrieved
medical text often provides limited benefit and can
sometimes degrade performance due to irrelevant
or diffuse context (Kim et al., 2025). In comparison, differential evidence appears to make better
use of external knowledge by highlighting the most
distinguishing facts for separating competing options, rather than requiring the model to infer these
distinctions from raw retrieved paragraphs on its
own.
To test whether the gains come merely from
adding LLM-generated auxiliary medical text, we
construct a general-hint baseline using the same
GPT-5 mini generator. This baseline is conditioned
on the same anchor cues, question, and answer
options, but it does not use retrieved textbook cooccurrence chunks and is not instructed to identify
option-discriminative conditions. Instead, it asks
the generator to produce general option-level medical background notes.
As shown in Table 5, general hints provide only
a modest average improvement over the base setting, increasing MedQA accuracy from 63.38% to
64.71%. In contrast, differential evidence improves
the average accuracy to 71.31%. This gap suggests
that the benefit of our method is not simply due
to appending additional LLM-generated text, but
comes from organized differential evidence.

not be useful evidence. The goal of this analysis
is therefore not to show that evidence contains no
predictive signal, but to test whether it collapses
into an answer shortcut.
5
Discussion and Conclusion
We present a differential evidence framework for
medical multiple-choice question answering. Experiments across three medical QA benchmarks
and multiple target models show that differential
evidence consistently improves direct answering
and further complements chain-of-thought prompting. Comparisons with general hints and retrieved
chunks suggest that the gains are not merely due
to adding extra generated text, but depend on the
quality and organization of the evidence. Leakageoriented analyses further indicate that differential
evidence is informative but does not function as a
standalone answer channel. Although our experiments focus on medical MCQs, the underlying
idea may apply more broadly to decision-making
settings where candidate hypotheses, plans, or actions are explicitly provided or can be enumerated,
and where a small number of discriminative facts
determine the final choice.

Limitations
Our study still has some limitations. First, our
framework depends on the quality of the external evidence generator.
Although GPT-5 mini
and DeepSeek-V3.2 produce useful differential evidence in our experiments, weaker generators may
produce overly generic, overly short, or answerproximal statements, reducing downstream gains.
In addition, while our prompts and leakage analyses aim to prevent the evidence from directly revealing the answer, subtle answer-proximal cues may
still occur, particularly in management and contraindication questions. Finally, our experiments
are limited to medical multiple-choice benchmarks
with explicit candidate options, and extending the
framework to open-ended or real-world decision
settings will require additional validation.

A
Additional Detailed Results for RQ3
We report the full RQ3 results on all three
datasets in Appendix Table 6. We observe that
on MedXpert-Text, the evidence-only setting is relatively strong and for some models is comparable
to, or slightly better than, C+Q+O. A possible
reason is that the overall baseline performance on
this dataset is low, suggesting that the task is difficult for the tested models under the standard input.
In such cases, even a small amount of compact
external signal may lead to a noticeable gain. However, C+Q+O+E still yields the strongest overall
results, indicating that differential evidence is most
effective when combined with the original context.
B
Co-occurrence Retrieval Details
For external knowledge retrieval, we use the textbook corpus provided together with the MedQA
dataset (Jin et al., 2021). Besides the question data,
MedQA also releases reference textbooks that have
already been segmented into chunks, and we directly perform co-occurrence retrieval over these
pre-defined chunks. Since some chunks are overly
long, we additionally cap the length of each retrieved chunk at 400 words. When multiple chunks
satisfy the co-occurrence criterion for the same
anchor cue-option pair, we keep only the first retrieved chunk.
We also explored several retrieval variants, including BM25-based retrieval strategies for identifying anchor-option co-occurrence paragraphs. In
pilot experiments, these variants produced similar
downstream trends. Since retrieval in our framework mainly serves as a grounding constraint for
evidence distillation rather than as the final answerselection mechanism, we use a simple and reproducible retrieval strategy in the main experiments.
C
Implementation Details
We use a unified inference setup for all target models. For direct answer generation, we set max new
tokens =16; for CoT-based settings, we set max new
tokens =512 to allow sufficient space for intermediate reasoning. For decoding, we use temperature
=0.0 and top p=1.0 across all experiments.

Definition of differential evidence:
Differential evidence is a short,
option-specific clinical cue that places the
option on the most relevant decision axis
for this question, such as treatment role,
timing, mechanism, contraindication,
adverse-effect relevance, management
category, or typical use setting. It should
help distinguish the option conceptually
from competing options, but it must not read
like an answer explanation or a statement
about whether the option fits the case.
Guidelines:
- Use the original question only to infer
the general decision setting and the likely
clinical management axis.
- Do not restate or reconstruct the full
case.
- Use co-occurrence paragraphs as grounding
constraints when available.
- If no co-occurrence paragraph is
available, use internal medical knowledge
conservatively and keep it consistent with
the question setting.
- Prefer the option's most distinctive
decision role over generic drug summaries,
broad mechanism descriptions, or
nonessential side effects.
- For medications, prioritize therapeutic
role, management timing, treatment category,
or clinically important risk over general
pharmacology unless pharmacology is itself
the key distinguishing cue.
- Keep the evidence concise and focused.
- Write one sentence whenever possible.
Restrictions:
- Do not solve the question.
- Do not say or imply which option is
correct, incorrect, preferred, excluded, or
should be selected.
- Do not rank options.
- Do not copy the source text verbatim.
- Do not output reasoning steps, bullet
points, or extra commentary.
- Do not explain why the option matches or
fails to match the current case.
- Do not directly justify the statement
using the patient, vignette, described
findings, or anchor facts.
- Do not distinguish the option by saying
what it is not used for, not part of, or
less appropriate than.
- Avoid negative contrast constructions such
as: "rather than ...", "instead of ...",
"not part of ...", "not used for ...", "less
appropriate", "ruled out".
- Express the evidence as a positive
description of the option's own clinical
decision role.
- Avoid generic textbook descriptions that
are not useful for distinguishing the option
