Abstract 
Efficient medical image segmentation is essential for clinical applications requiring 
rapid and accurate delineation, including surgical navigation, radiation therapy, and realtime image-guided intervention. Despite substantial advances in convolutional neural 
networks and Transformers, achieving high segmentation accuracy with limited 
computational resources remains challenging. In particular, reducing model complexity 
often comes at the expense of fine-grained structural details and effective global context 
modeling, resulting in an unfavorable trade-off between computational efficiency and 
segmentation accuracy. To address this challenge, we propose LeViT-UNet++, an 
efficient encoder–decoder architecture designed to achieve a better balance between 
local detail preservation and global context modeling. Specifically, we introduce a Haar 
Wavelet Multi-Scale Downsampling (HWMD) module that explicitly decomposes feature 
representations into low- and high-frequency components and extracts multi-scale 
structural information to mitigate the loss of fine-grained details during downsampling. 
Furthermore, we develop a depthwise convolution-based decoder that efficiently 
integrates multi-scale global representations from Transformer blocks with local spatial 
features, enabling progressive feature refinement while reducing computational 
overhead. We evaluate LeViT-UNet++ on four medical image segmentation 
benchmarks—Synapse, ACDC, LA2018, and MSD Heart—using both 2D and 3D 
experimental settings. Extensive experiments show that the proposed approach yields 
competitive segmentation accuracy with a compact parameter footprint and fast 
inference speed. On the Synapse dataset, for instance, LeViT-UNet++ attains a DSC of 
76.57% with merely 8.025M parameters and an inference rate of 241.72 FPS, confirming 
its favorable accuracy–efficiency balance. This work demonstrates the effectiveness of 
integrating wavelet-based multi-scale feature extraction and lightweight local–global 
feature fusion for efficient medical segmentation. The source code will be publicly 
available at https://github.com/apple1986/levit_unet_pp. 
Keywords: medical image segmentation, light-weight network, convolution neural 
networks, transformer, wavelet transform, efficient segmentation

1. Introduction 
Efficient medical image segmentation is essential for clinical applications that require 
rapid and accurate anatomical delineation, including surgical navigation, radiation 
therapy, and real-time image-guided intervention [1]. Over the past decade, encoder–
decoder architectures have become a dominant paradigm for medical image 
segmentation, with representative approaches such as U-Net [2], TransUNet [3] and Swin 
UNETR [4] demonstrating substantial improvements in segmentation accuracy. However, 
the pursuit of increasingly powerful feature representations has often been accompanied 
by growing computational and memory requirements, which can hinder the deployment 
of these models in resource-constrained and latency-sensitive clinical environments. 
Therefore, developing medical image segmentation networks that achieve a favorable 
balance between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency remains an 
important research challenge, particularly for clinical applications requiring accurate and 
timely delineation under stringent computational constraints. 
Currently, convolutional, and Transformer-based architectures have emerged as the 
mainstream paradigms for medical image segmentation. Convolutional architectures 
primarily exploit local spatial interactions to progressively extract semantic 
representations through hierarchical feature downsampling [5] (see Figure 1 (a)). As the 
spatial resolution decreases, the number of feature channels typically increases, resulting 
in substantial parameter and computational costs in deeper stages. Transformer-based 
architectures offer an alternative mechanism for modeling long-range dependencies 
through token-level self-attention (see Figure 1 (b)). This enables effective global 
contextual modeling but generally introduces substantial computational and memory 
costs, particularly when processing high-resolution medical images [6]. Thus, neither 
purely convolutional nor purely Transformer-based architectures inherently provide an 
optimal solution for simultaneously preserving local structural details, capturing global 
contextual information, and maintaining computational efficiency.

These observations motivate the exploration of hybrid architectures that exploit the

--- PAGE 3 [pdf_text] ---
complementary properties of convolution and Transformer representations. In particular, 
convolutional operations are well suited to capturing local spatial structures and finegrained anatomical boundaries, whereas Transformer-based representations provide 
broader contextual dependencies. An effective and efficient segmentation architecture 
should therefore integrate these complementary representations while minimizing 
redundant computation during feature extraction and decoding. The LeViT-UNet [7] (see 
Figure 1 (c)) represents an early effort to bridge convolutional and Transformer-based 
representations for efficient medical image segmentation. Specifically, it adopts vanilla 
LeViT [8] (a lightweight hybrid convolution–Transformer backbone originally designed for 
image classification) as the encoder and convolution-based blocks as the decoder, 
seeking to achieve a favorable trade-off between segmentation accuracy and 
computational efficiency. Its core design is based on the observation that convolutional 
operations can efficiently extract local spatial representations, while progressive 
downsampling reduces the spatial resolution of feature maps. Transformer blocks can 
subsequently model long-range dependencies on these low-resolution representations 
with substantially lower computational cost than applying global attention at high spatial 
resolutions. This hybrid design therefore provides an efficient means of combining local 
feature extraction with global contextual modeling for medical image segmentation. 
However, LeViT-UNet-like architectures [3, 7, 9] still face two important limitations. 
First, their efficiency-oriented convolutional encoders rely heavily on progressive spatial 
downsampling to reduce computational cost and expand the effective receptive field. 
Although this strategy enables efficient hierarchical feature extraction, conventional 
downsampling operations, such as max pooling and average pooling, inevitably discard 
spatial information [10]. This information loss is particularly problematic for medical 
image segmentation, where accurate delineation often depends on subtle boundary cues, 
thin structures, and small anatomical regions. In particular, high-frequency components 
contain important fine-grained structural information that may be attenuated or lost 
during repeated spatial reduction [11]. Once these details are discarded at early stages, 
subsequent upsampling operations cannot fully reconstruct the original structural 
information. Therefore, an efficient segmentation architecture should not rely solely on 
spatial downsampling for computational reduction, but should also provide an explicit 
mechanism for preserving complementary fine-grained structural information 
throughout the feature extraction process. 
Second, convolution-based decoders suffer from notable computational 
redundancy in feature learning [12, 13]. Within the standard encoder–decoder 
architecture, the decoder gradually fuses features from its previous stage with skipconnected features from the matching encoder layer. Although skip connections help 
restore spatial details, their fusion commonly employs standard convolutions over highdimensional feature maps. This adds a large number of convolutional parameters and 
computation overhead in the decoding branch. Furthermore, concatenated encoder and 
decoder features often contain overlapping, redundant spatial representations, where not 
all signals are essential for faithful feature reconstruction. This motivates a core research 
question: can the decoder maintain the representational capacity of conventional

--- PAGE 4 [pdf_text] ---
convolutional feature fusion while drastically cutting its computational redundancy? 
To address these two challenges, we introduce two complementary architectural 
enhancements to preserve fine-grained structural information during encoder 
downsampling and improve computational efficiency in the decoder. First, we propose a 
Haar Wavelet Multi-Scale Downsampling (HWMD) module that combines Haar wavelet 
decomposition with parallel depthwise convolutions of different kernel sizes. Haar 
wavelet decomposition separates feature representations into low- and high-frequency 
components, capturing complementary structural information, while multi-scale 
depthwise convolutions extract spatial patterns across different receptive fields with 
limited additional computational cost. This design helps preserve fine-grained anatomical 
details during downsampling while maintaining efficient feature extraction. 
Furthermore, we develop a Depthwise-based Local and Global feature fusion and 
learning decoder (DLGD) that employs lightweight depthwise convolutional blocks [14] 
to progressively integrate global contextual representations from Transformer blocks with 
local spatial features propagated through encoder skip connections. By enabling efficient 
feature fusion and refinement, the proposed decoder reduces computational overhead 
while integrating complementary local and global representations. Together, HWMD and 
DLGD improve the balance between structural detail preservation, contextual 
representation, and computational efficiency. 
In summary, we introduce LeViT-UNet++, an enhanced version of our previous 
LeViT-UNet [7] that improves both the encoding and decoding processes for efficient 
medical image segmentation. The main contributions of this study are as follows: 
(1) We introduce a Haar Wavelet Multi-scale Downsampling module (HWMD) that 
combines wavelet-based information preservation with parallel depthwise convolutions 
at multiple scales. HWMD retains complementary structural information during spatial 
downsampling while providing multi-scale representations with limited computational 
overhead for the encoder. 
(2) We propose a Depthwise-based Local and Global feature fusion and learning 
decoder (DLGD) that efficiently integrates local spatial features from the convolutional 
encoder with global contextual representations from the Transformer. By leveraging 
lightweight depthwise convolutional blocks for progressive feature fusion and refinement, 
DLGD reduces decoder computational overhead while effectively integrating 
complementary local and global information. 
(3) We develop LeViT-UNet++, a convolution – Transformer hybrid encoder –
decoder framework that integrates HWMD and DLGD to jointly preserve fine-grained 
structural information, capture global contextual representations, and improve 
computational efficiency. 
(4) We conduct extensive experiments on four challenging medical image 
segmentation benchmarks covering both 2D and 3D segmentation scenarios. The results 
demonstrate that LeViT-UNet++ achieves a competitive balance between segmentation 
performance and computational efficiency compared with representative state-of-theart methods.

2. Related Work 
2.1 Downsampling in Image Segmentation 
Downsampling is a fundamental operation in semantic segmentation networks, as it 
progressively reduces spatial resolution to decrease computational cost and enlarge the 
effective receptive field while constructing hierarchical feature representations. 
Conventional segmentation architectures typically employ pooling operations, such as 
max pooling [15] and average pooling [16], for spatial reduction. For example, SegNet 
[15] adopts pooling indices to facilitate efficient feature reconstruction in the decoder. 
Beyond conventional pooling, multi-scale feature aggregation has been widely explored 
to improve the representation of objects with varying spatial scales. PSPNet [17] 
introduces spatial pyramid pooling to aggregate contextual information from multiple 
spatial regions, while DeepLab [18] employs atrous spatial pyramid pooling to capture 
multi-scale contextual representations without further reducing spatial resolution. 
Despite their effectiveness, conventional downsampling operations inevitably 
discard spatial information as the feature resolution decreases. Such information loss can 
affect segmentation performance, particularly for objects with complex boundaries, small 
structures, or fine-grained details. To alleviate this issue, several studies have explored 
information-preserving downsampling strategies [10, 11, 19]. Haar wavelet-based 
downsampling (HWD) [11], for instance, replaces conventional spatial reduction with Haar 
wavelet decomposition to preserve complementary frequency components during 
downsampling. By retaining low- and high-frequency information, wavelet-based 
approaches provide richer representations for subsequent feature extraction while 
maintaining the computational benefits of spatial reduction. 
However, HWD employs standard convolution to process and fuse the decomposed 
frequency components, introducing additional learnable parameters and computational 
overhead compared with conventional pooling operations. Moreover, its feature 
extraction remains based on a fixed convolutional receptive field and does not explicitly 
exploit spatial patterns at multiple scales, which may limit its ability to capture structural 
variations across different object sizes and spatial contexts. These limitations motivate the 
development of a more efficient information-preserving downsampling strategy that can 
simultaneously retain fine-grained structural information and capture multi-scale spatial 
patterns. Hence, we introduce an efficient Haar Wavelet Multi-Scale Downsampling 
module for image segmentation to address these challenges in this study. 
2.2 Architectures for Efficient Neural Networks 
A broad range of strategies has been explored to develop lightweight neural 
architectures, with existing approaches generally falling into two complementary 
categories: efficient building-block design and holistic architectural optimization [20]. The 
former aims to reduce computational complexity at the individual module level, whereas 
the latter seeks to improve efficiency through the design and optimization of the overall 
network topology. 
At the module level, depthwise separable convolution is a fundamental technique

ShuffleNet V2 [24] combine group convolution with channel shuffling to reduce 
computational redundancy while enabling information exchange across channel groups. 
GhostNet [12] further improves computational efficiency through its Ghost module, which 
generates additional feature maps from a limited set of intrinsic features using 
inexpensive linear transformations, thereby reducing reliance on computationally 
intensive conventional convolutions. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate how 
carefully designed computational primitives can substantially improve the efficiency of 
deep neural networks without fundamentally compromising their representational 
capacity. 
In contrast, holistic architectural optimization targets the overall network structure, 
including the allocation of computational resources across stages, the scaling of network 
depth, width, and input resolution, and the organization of feature extraction pathways. 
EfficientNet [25, 26] systematically investigates the joint scaling of these dimensions 
through compound model scaling, achieving a more balanced trade-off between 
computational cost and representational capacity. Other representative architectures, 
such as BiSeNet [16], DDRNet [27], and LGNet [28], explore complementary two-branch 
strategies for learning and fusing local and contextual information. SegFormer [29], by 
contrast, designs efficient MLP-based decoders to aggregate multi-layer features 
extracted from the encoder. Instead of optimizing individual operations in isolation, these 
approaches emphasize coordinated design of network components and their global 
configurations to attain more favorable efficiency–accuracy trade-offs. 
While these lightweight architectural design principles were originally developed for 
natural image recognition, they have been increasingly adapted to medical image 
segmentation. Typical approaches include embedding efficient modules into existing 
networks or directly adopting lightweight backbones. Nevertheless, medical 
segmentation poses unique challenges: it demands a delicate balance among 
computational efficiency, multi-scale contextual modeling, and the preservation of finegrained anatomical details. Representative architectures, including LeViT-UNet [7], 
MedNeXt [30], Mobile U-ViT [31], explore different strategies for efficient feature 
extraction and contextual modeling. Despite these advances, the design of an effective 
and computationally efficient decoder remains a critical challenge. A well-designed 
decoder must progressively recover spatial details and integrate complementary local 
and global representations across multiple scales, while minimizing the computational 
and memory overhead associated with feature fusion and refinement. This challenge 
becomes even more pronounced in 3D medical image segmentation, where the high 
dimensionality of volumetric features substantially increases computational demands [32].

Therefore, developing lightweight decoders that effectively balance multi-scale feature 
fusion, anatomical detail preservation, and computational efficiency is essential for both 
2D and 3D medical image segmentation.

Specifically, the encoder consists of three sequential components: (1) a Haar Wavelet 
Multi-Scale Downsampling (HWMD) module, (2) four convolutional blocks, and (3) three 
Transformer blocks. First, HWMD reduces the spatial resolution of the input feature maps 
while aggregating multiscale structural information through Haar wavelet decomposition 
and parallel multi-scale spatial feature extraction. The resulting features are subsequently 
processed by four convolutional blocks, each incorporating convolutional layers, batch 
normalization, and hard-swish activation to extract local spatial representations. To 
progressively reduce computational complexity while expanding the effective receptive 
field, spatial downsampling is performed between consecutive blocks, except before the 
first convolutional block. Finally, the three successive Transformer blocks operate on the 
progressively downsampled feature representations to model long-range dependencies 
among spatial tokens, thereby enhancing global contextual modeling and 
complementing the local features extracted by the convolutional stages. 
Subsequently, the decoder is elaborately designed to progressively recover spatial 
resolution and fuse complementary multi-level feature hierarchies delivered by the 
encoder. It consists of a multi-scale global feature fusion module, three sequentially 
arranged depthwise separable convolutional blocks within the upsampling pathway, and

a final segmentation head. The fusion module aggregates hierarchical feature 
representations derived from the three Transformer blocks to achieve effective crossscale information integration and strengthen global contextual consistency. The 
integrated global features are further concatenated with local feature maps generated by 
the last convolutional block of the encoder. The concatenated global-local features are 
then progressively refined via three successive depthwise separable convolutional layers, 
each followed by a 2 upsampling operation. This design gradually restores fine spatial 
details while substantially reducing computational burden and parameter overhead. 
Compared with vanilla LeViT-UNet, our proposed DLGD decoder reduces the parameter 
count by nearly 49.5%. Ultimately, the segmentation head adopts a convolutional layer 
combined with bilinear upsampling to produce high-resolution pixel-wise segmentation 
predictions. 
3.2 Haar Wavelet Multi-Scale Downsampling Module 
The proposed Haar Wavelet Multi-Scale Downsampling (HWMD) module is 
designed to preserve fine-grained structural information while progressively reducing 
spatial resolution. HWMD explicitly decomposes the input feature map into low- and 
high-frequency components using the two-dimensional discrete Haar wavelet transform, 
extracts complementary representations through multi-scale depthwise convolutions, 
and subsequently integrates these representations using pointwise convolution. As 
illustrated in Figure 2 (a), HWMD consists of three successive operations: Haar wavelet 
decomposition, frequency-specific multi-scale depthwise convolution, and low–highfrequency feature fusion.

3.3 Depthwise-based local-and-global feature fusion and learning decoder 
The proposed decoder (DLGD) is designed to efficiently integrate multi-scale global 
contextual representations from the Transformer encoder with local spatial features, 
progressively refining the fused representations to recover fine-grained details for 
accurate segmentation. As illustrated in Figure 2 (b), the decoder consists of three 
principal components: (1) a multi-scale global feature fusion module that aggregates 
hierarchical representations from the Transformer blocks; (2) a local and global feature 
fusion and learning module comprising three depthwise separable convolutional blocks, 
which progressively fuse and refine complementary local and global features while 
maintaining computational efficiency; and (3) a segmentation head that generates the 
final pixel-wise prediction. 
Specifically, the multi-scale global feature fusion module combines hierarchical 
Transformer features to provide contextual guidance at different semantic levels. These 
fused global representations are subsequently integrated with local spatial features 
through the three successive depthwise separable convolutional blocks. By progressively 
refining the fused features, DLGD facilitates the interaction between global semantic 
context and local spatial information, thereby supporting the recovery of fine-grained 
anatomical structures and object boundaries. Furthermore, the use of depthwise 
separable convolutions reduces the computational cost of feature refinement compared 
with conventional convolutional operations, making the decoder more parameterefficient. Overall, DLGD provides a lightweight decoding strategy that balances multiscale contextual integration, local detail recovery, and computational efficiency. 
 
4. Experiments and Results 
4.1 Datasets 
(1). Synapse dataset 
The Synapse Multi-Organ Segmentation (Synapse) dataset contains 30 abdominal 
CT volumes, offering dense voxel-level annotations for eight abdominal organs: aorta 
(Aor), gallbladder (Gal), spleen (Spl), left kidney (LKid), right kidney (RKid), liver (Liv), 
pancreas (Pan), and stomach (Sto). We adopt the data split strategy described in [7], 
where 18 samples are allocated for model training and the other 12 samples are held out 
as the validation set.

--- PAGE 12 [pdf_text] ---
(2) ACDC 
The Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC) dataset collects cardiac cineMRI sequences captured at end-diastole and end-systole. Every subject is annotated 
manually for three key cardiac structures, namely the left ventricle (LV), myocardium 
(MYO), and right ventricle (RV), to construct pixel-wise ground truth. Consistent with the 
splitting scheme reported in [7], we select 20 subjects (40 volumetric scans) as the test 
set, and utilize the remaining 80 subjects (160 volumes) for network training. 
(3) LA2018 
The Left Atrium Segmentation Challenge (LA2018) dataset [33] comprises 100 
volumetric late gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (LGE-MRI) scans, 
each accompanied by a manually delineated segmentation mask of the left atrium (LA). 
In this study, the dataset is randomly partitioned into 70 volumes for training, 10 for 
validation, and 20 for testing. 
(4) MSD Heart 
The Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD) Heart dataset (Task02_Heart) [34] 
provides volumetric cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data with segmentation 
annotations for the target cardiac structure. The dataset contains 30 cases, including 20 
cases with ground-truth segmentation labels and 10 cases without publicly available 
labels. In this study, the 20 labeled cases are randomly partitioned into 14 training cases, 
2 validation cases, and 4 testing cases to evaluate the performance of the proposed 
method and competing three-dimensional (3D) segmentation models. 
Table 2 summarizes the four publicly available benchmark datasets used in this study, 
detailing their imaging modalities, target anatomical structures, and respective training, 
validation, and test splits. Different data preparation and evaluation protocols are 
adopted for the 2D- and 3D-based segmentation models. Specifically, for the Synapse 
and ACDC datasets, volumetric scans are decomposed into individual 2D slices for model 
training and inference. In contrast, the original volumetric data from the LA2018 and MSD 
Heart datasets are retained for training and evaluating 3D segmentation models. To 
ensure a consistent volume-level evaluation across different model architectures, the 
slice-wise predictions generated by the 2D models are subsequently reassembled into 
3D volumes in their original spatial order. All segmentation metrics are then computed 
at the volumetric level, providing a unified basis for comparing segmentation 
performance across the evaluated models.

4.2 Implementation details 
In this study, all experiments are implemented using Python 3.10.4 and PyTorch 2.11 
with CUDA 12.8 on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, running under Ubuntu 24.04.4 
LTS. For all training samples, augmentation strategies including random flipping and 
random rotation are adopted to enhance data diversity. For 2D model training, all input 
images are resized to a spatial resolution of 224 224. All 2D models are optimized with 
the Adam optimizer, using an initial learning rate of 5e-4 and weight decay of 1e-4. 
Cross-entropy loss combined with Dice loss serves as the objective function. The batch 
size is set to 8. We train the model for 350 epochs on the Synapse dataset and 400 epochs 
on the ACDC dataset. Following the strategy adopted in [3, 7], we select the final epoch 
for evaluation on the test set. 
For 3D model training, we use the MONAI framework and adopt the experimental 
configurations of Swin UNETR [4] as a reference for the LA2018 and MSD Heart datasets. 
Specifically, we extend the 2D HWMD and DLGD modules to their 3D counterparts based 
on 3D Haar wavelet transforms and convolutions for the 3D LeViT-UNet++. AdamW is 
used as the optimizer, with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a weight decay of 1 10⁻⁵. 
The training objective combines cross-entropy loss and Dice loss. The input volume size 
is obtained by randomly cropping 64 64 64 voxel patches centered on target 
regions, and the batch size is set to 4. The models are trained for 200 epochs on LA2018 
and 600 epochs on MSD Heart, respectively. During training, model selection is 
performed based on the mean Dice score on the validation set, and the checkpoint 
achieving the highest validation Dice score is retained for final evaluation on the 
corresponding test set. For methods with multiple publicly available variants, the smallest 
model in terms of parameter count is selected to ensure a consistent comparison of 
model efficiency.

5. Discussion 
LeViT-UNet++ was developed to improve the balance between segmentation 
accuracy and computational efficiency by addressing two complementary aspects of 
feature representation: structural information preservation during downsampling and 
computationally efficient feature integration during decoding. Specifically, HWMD 
incorporates Haar wavelet decomposition and frequency-specific multi-scale depthwise 
convolutions to retain complementary low- and high-frequency representations during 
the initial downsampling stage. Meanwhile, DLGD employs depthwise separable 
convolutions to progressively integrate local spatial features and global contextual 
representations while reducing decoder complexity. 
The experimental results across four medical image segmentation benchmarks 
demonstrate the potential of these complementary designs in both 2D and 3D 
segmentation settings. Compared with the original LeViT-UNet, the proposed 
architecture achieves notable improvements on the Synapse and LA2018 datasets while 
substantially reducing the number of model parameters. In particular, the parameter 
reduction is primarily attributed to the lightweight decoder design, whereas HWMD 
contributes to the preservation of complementary structural representations during 
feature extraction. These results suggest that improving architectural efficiency does not 
necessarily require sacrificing the representation of fine-grained structural information. 
Despite these promising results, the proposed architecture has several limitations 
that warrant further investigation. First, although LeViT-UNet++ achieves a favorable 
trade-off between segmentation performance and computational efficiency, its 
performance does not consistently surpass that of state-of-the-art segmentation 
methods, particularly those employing more sophisticated feature representation and 
fusion strategies. This suggests that improving computational efficiency while maintaining 
or further enhancing segmentation accuracy remains a challenging problem. One 
possible contributing factor is the limited adaptability of the current multi-scale feature 
fusion strategy. Specifically, DLGD progressively integrates local and global 
representations through concatenation-based fusion and skip connections. Although this 
strategy is computationally efficient, it may not fully capture the complex dependencies 
and complementary relationships among features at different spatial resolutions. More 
adaptive fusion mechanisms, such as attention-guided feature selection, content-aware 
feature aggregation, or learnable cross-scale interactions, may provide more effective 
ways to exploit complementary local details and global contextual information while 
preserving the lightweight nature of the architecture. Furthermore, knowledge distillation 
represents a promising paradigm for transferring knowledge from heavy, large-scale 
models into lightweight architectures such as our proposed LeViT-UNet++, which helps 
mitigate the requirement for massive annotated training samples in medical image 
segmentation. We hope these explorations will inspire further advances toward compact, 
data-efficient models for routine clinical workflows. 
Second, the evaluation was conducted on a limited number of publicly available 
benchmarks. Although these datasets cover different anatomical structures and imaging 
modalities, further validation on larger, more diverse, and clinically representative 
datasets is required to establish the robustness and generalizability of the proposed

--- PAGE 21 [pdf_text] ---
architecture. In addition, its computational efficiency requires systematic evaluation 
across diverse hardware platforms and deployment settings, especially under real-world 
clinical constraints. Furthermore, the current feature fusion strategy mainly relies on 
concatenation and depthwise convolution, which may restrict its capacity to adaptively 
model complex interdependencies among multi-scale features. Accordingly, our future 
work will focus on developing more effective yet computationally economical multi-scale 
feature fusion schemes, extending evaluations to larger clinical datasets, and exploring 
the feasibility of deploying LeViT-UNet++ in resource-limited medical imaging scenarios. 
6. Conclusion 
This study presents LeViT-UNet++, a lightweight convolution–Transformer hybrid 
framework for medical image segmentation, aiming to balance segmentation accuracy 
and computational efficiency. It integrates the Haar Wavelet Multi-Scale Downsampling 
(HWMD) module to preserve fine-grained structural information and the Depthwisebased Local and Global feature fusion and learning Decoder (DLGD) to efficiently 
combine local spatial details with global contextual representations. Extensive 
experiments across four benchmarks (Synapse, ACDC, LA2018, and MSD-Heart) under 
both 2D and 3D settings demonstrate that LeViT-UNet++ delivers competitive 
segmentation performance with substantially reduced model complexity and favorable 
inference speed. These findings highlight the effectiveness of combining wavelet-based 
structural preservation with efficient local–global feature fusion, offering a promising 
solution for medical image segmentation in resource- and latency-constrained 
environments.
